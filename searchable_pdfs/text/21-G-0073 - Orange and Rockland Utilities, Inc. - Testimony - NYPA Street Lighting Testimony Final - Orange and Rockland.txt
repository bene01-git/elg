II. 
BACKGROUND 
1 
Q.  
What enables municipalities to purchase their street light assets from utility 
2 
companies? 
3 
A.  
In November 2015, Governor Andrew Cuomo signed into law Chapter 495 of the 2015 
4 
Laws of New York, which added Section 70-a to the Public Service Law and established 
5 
procedures for the transfer of ownership of street lighting systems to municipalities or 
6 
other government entities. In an October 2016 order in Cases 15-E-0745, et al., which we 
7 
will refer to as the “2016 Street Light Transfer Order,” the Public Service Commission 
8 
(“PSC”) approved tariff changes for all of New York State’s investor-owned utilities, 
9 
including the Company, that implemented the new street lighting ownership transfer 
10 
procedures. This regulatory change opened new opportunities for municipalities to 
11 
achieve both energy and cost reductions that did not exist previously. 
12 
Q. 
Why do municipalities opt to purchase street light systems from utility companies? 
13 
A. 
Municipalities mainly choose to buy the street light systems in their jurisdictions because 
14 
they can achieve savings from both the reduced energy usage associated with conversion 
15 
to LED luminaires and reduced facilities charges associated with the street light system.  
16 
Q. 
Are there other factors driving street light asset sales?  
17 
A.  
Yes.  Purchasing their street lights allows municipalities to have control over their 
18 
required maintenance and ensure more streamlined communications with constituents 
19 
regarding street light maintenance issues. Furthermore, the municipality can achieve cost 
20 
savings by competitively bidding the maintenance work to reduce charges that otherwise 
21 
would be paid to the utility. Some municipalities also are interested in maintaining a

Q.  
What role does NYPA play in the conversion of customer-owned street light assets 
3

4

Governor Cuomo directed NYPA to lead the Smart Street Lighting NY Program in his 
5

10

13

III. 
SMART STREET LIGHTING TECHNOLOGY 
3 
Q.  
In addition to LED conversions that create energy savings, what services can be 
4 
provided to customers through street lighting modifications?  
5 
A.  
Smart street lighting, or “smart city” technologies, provide numerous other benefits and 
6 
services beyond the direct energy savings that arise from converting traditional 
7 
luminaires to LED. One example of these technologies is the network lighting control 
8 
(“NLC”) node.  
9 
Q.  
What is an NLC node? 
10 
A.  
NLC nodes are modules that take the place of photosensors installed in the street light 
11 
fixture’s National Electrical Manufacturers Association (“NEMA”) socket. They allow 
12 
the street lighting customer to control lighting levels—including dimming capabilities—
13 
and reduce energy consumption.  
14 
Q.  
What is NYPA’s experience with smart city technologies?   
15 
A.  
NYPA is working with municipalities all over the state through our Smart Street Lighting 
16 
NY Program to install the NLC nodes, which enable data-based insight into the 
17 
performance of the street lighting system and allow communities to control lighting 
18 
levels. NYPA is developing nearly 100 projects that will install a total of almost 100,000 
19 
NLC nodes across the state.   
20 
Q. 
What benefits are associated with the adaptive dimming control capabilities of the 
21 
NLC nodes?  
22 


--- Page 9 ---

A.  
The Seattle Study, which is included as Exhibit__(SLP-1) to our testimony, estimated 
1 
that street light dimming strategies could enhance the energy savings achieved through 
2 
LED conversion by as much as 25%, as compared to pre-LED energy usage levels and 
3 
depending on the dimming strategy utilized. Without network lighting controls, however, 
4 
street lights can only be turned off or operated at full brightness. Importantly, dimming 
5 
strategies enable energy savings without impacting overall lighting performance.  The 
6 
Seattle Study demonstrated that, based on field tests with new lamps, dimming does not 
7 
significantly impact object detection by the naked eye, and even dimming by 50 percent 
8 
did not significantly reduce object detection in dry pavement conditions.  
9 
Street light dimming with NLC nodes extends the useful life of the lamp without 
10 
sacrificing lighting quality or public safety. The LED driver typically fails first in a 
11 
fixture. Dimming extends the life of the fixture by reducing the load on the LED driver. 
12 
Operating at a lower output also allows the street light to operate at a lower temperature, 
13 
which further increases the light’s useful life. 
14 
Larger scale dimming strategies to reduce street light energy use also can benefit 
15 
customers. For example, additional energy savings can be realized by reducing lighting to 
16 
a greater extent during low-traffic, early morning hours. Strategic dimming of high 
17 
wattage luminaires can also be utilized for public safety purposes, for example by 
18 
brightening lights during police or first responder activities or blinking or flashing lights 
19 
to warn the public of natural disasters and/or direct residents away from danger.  
20

Q. 
Can NLC nodes be used to measure energy usage when dimming strategies are 
14 
implemented?  
15 
A.  
Yes, NLC nodes can serve a dual purpose. In addition to allowing municipalities to 
16 
monitor and control their energy usage by controlling the light, they also are high-quality 
17 
electric meters that measure energy usage and record data in real-time. Ultimately, NLC 
18 
nodes can be used as a cost-effective way to accurately measure energy savings from 
19 
dimming.

As an interim step while the Pilot is ongoing, we recommend that the Company

Q. 
Do you have specific recommendations on the adaptive operating schedules that 
3 
should be implemented? 
4 
A.  
Yes. Initially, customers should be able to opt in to one of four adaptive operating 
5 
schedules, 70%, 50% and 30% and 0% (“part-night”), as measured by the level of street 
6 
lighting energy usage between dusk and dawn.  After one year of operating experience 
7

8 
interested parties to determine if the adaptive operating schedules should be modified 
9

10 
We recommend maximum flexibility in setting the schedules, including allowing 
11 
municipalities to switch between the Company-approved schedules on a quarterly basis 
12 
and to select customized schedules within their jurisdiction. Customized schedules, for 
13 
example, could enable dimming for a shorter time in a busy downtown area and a longer 
14 
time in residential areas.  
15 
We also recommend that, once Company-approved schedules are established, 
16 
customers opting into the dimming schedules should submit a proposed weekly operating 
17 
schedule that identifies their planned dimming schedule. The Company then would 
18 
identify the most appropriate adaptive operating schedule for the customer based on their 
19 
anticipated usage and assign the customer to that schedule for billing purposes.

Q: Please summarize your background and expertise.
A: I am a co-founder of Concerned Health Professionals of New York (“CHPNY”), an initiative by
health professionals, scientists, and medical organizations to raise science-based concerns
about the impacts of hydraulic fracturing (“fracking”) on public health and safety. CHPNY
provides educational resources and works to ensure that careful consideration of science and
health impacts are at the forefront of the fracking debate. In June 2021, the Ceres Trust granted
funding for CHPNY to become a program of the Science and Environmental Health Network
(SEHN). Since 1998, SEHN has been the leading proponent in the United States of the
Precautionary Principle as a basis for environmental and public health policy. In service to
communities and future generations, the Science and Environmental Health Network is a
research institution that forges law, ethics, and science into tools for action.
CHPNY has worked with Physicians for Social Responsibility to produce eight editions of ​the
Compendium of Scientific, Medical, and Media Findings Demonstrating Risks and Harms of
Fracking (“the Compendium”). As one of the main editors of the Compendium, I personally
reviewed hundreds of studies on the public health and global warming impacts of fracking and
fracked gas infrastructure.
Q: Please describe The Compendium of Scientific, Medical, and Media Findings
Demonstrating Risks and Harms of Fracking.
A: ​The Compendium is a 500+ page, fully referenced compilation of evidence outlining the risks
and harms of fracking and fracked gas infrastructure. It is a public, open-access document that
is housed on the websites of Concerned Health Professionals of New York and Physicians for
Social Responsibility.
The seven earlier editions of the Compendium have been used and referenced all over the
world. The Compendium has been translated into Spanish twice. First, the Compendium was
independently translated in 2014 by a Madrid-based environmental coalition. Later, an official
translation of the third edition was funded by the Heinrich Böll Foundation. The Compendium
was subsequently updated in December 2019 with new data from the sixth edition. The
Compendium has been used in the European Union, South Africa, the United Kingdom,
Australia, Mexico, and Argentina.

Q: Summarize the scientific findings in the Compendium on the impacts of gas utility
pipeline delivery system leaks.
A: Studies have found that distribution lines that deliver gas into homes and offices are a
significant source of leaking methane. According to a study led by experts at Boston University,
for example, fugitive emissions from urban gas pipeline systems are the largest anthropogenic
source of greenhouse gas methane in the United States and contribute to the risk of explosions
in urban environments, with 15% of leaks qualifying as potentially explosive.1 The study’s
authors concluded that “all leaks must be addressed, as even small leaks cannot be
disregarded as ‘safely leaking.’”2 Another recent study concluded that leaky gas distribution
systems contribute to the death of urban trees.3 The amount of gas leaking from utility
distribution systems has been historically underreported, meaning the public health and climate
impacts are also underreported.

Natural gas is 85 to 95 percent methane. Moreover, all parts of the gas supply chain are prone
to leaks, and these leaks nearly always include emissions of not only methane but also other
health-damaging air pollutants (HDAPs) such as volatile organic compounds (VOCs). As a

Q: What is the relationship between radon exposure and gas infrastructure?
A: Consolidated Edison has confirmed that over 50% of its gas supply is extracted from
Pennsylvania’s Marcellus Shale fracking region. Communities living near Consolidated Edison’s
gas infrastructure would face health risks from exposure to radioactive materials that naturally
occur in shale and build up in pipelines and other gas infrastructure.
Naturally occurring radioactive substances often co-occur with oil and gas inside the deep shale
layers that are targeted for fracking. These substances are brought to the surface in the rocky
material removed during drilling (drill cuttings) and in fracking wastewater. Fracking itself can
open pathways for the migration of radioactive materials, which can be released as airborne
particles from the wellhead during operations. Radionuclides can build up in pipes, equipment,
and trucks. Exposure to increased radiation levels from fracking materials is a risk for both
workers and residents living near fracked gas infrastructure. Evidence suggests, moreover, that
fracked gas from the Marcellus Shale may contain much higher concentrations of radioactive
materials than previously estimated.6

The United Nations Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) estimates that over a

Q: Summarize the scientific findings in the Compendium on emissions from Liquified
Natural Gas (“LNG”) storage facilities like Consolidated Edison’s Astoria plant.
A: Liquified Natural Gas (“LNG”) tanks are leaky by design: to maintain the liquid at super-
chilled temperatures and prevent explosions, vaporized gas is vented from storage tanks
directly ​into the atmosphere. Larger tanks are engineered to capture boiled-off gas, but this
process is not leak-proof. Before it is combusted or sent down a pipeline, LNG must be
regasified via an energy-intensive process that requires massive infrastructure of its own,
including periodic flaring to control pressure.The pipelines that bring gas in and out of LNG
facilities also leak methane.
Refrigeration, venting, leaks, and flaring make LNG more energy intensive—and therefore
worse for the climate—than conventional natural gas.
LNG also creates acute public safety risks. LNG explodes when spilled into water and, if spilled
on the ground, can turn into rapidly expanding, odorless clouds that can flash-freeze human
flesh and asphyxiate by displacing oxygen. If ignited at the source, LNG vapors can become
flaming “pool fires” that burn hotter than other fuels and cannot be extinguished. LNG fires burn
hot enough to cause second-degree burns on exposed skin up to a mile away, and the intense
cold (-260 degrees F) can cause instant frostbite if first responders get too close to leaks. LNG
facilities pose significant risks to nearby population centers and have been identified as potential
terrorist targets.
Q: Summarize the scientific findings in the Compendium on emissions from fracked gas
power plants like Consolidated Edison’s East River Plant.
A: There are two types of gas-fueled power plants: combined cycle plants and simple cycle
plants. Both types are major emitters of carbon dioxide, uncombusted methane, and nitrogen
oxides, which contribute to the formation of ground-level ozone (smog).
Combined cycle gas plants reuse waste heat to generate additional electricity and are roughly
equivalent in efficiency to an older coal plant. Simple cycle gas plants—also called peaker
plants—can be turned on and off faster to meet fluctuating energy demands when electricity
needs peak, but they are much less efficient and more polluting than combined cycle plants.

Simple cycle peaker plants can often generate more nitrogen oxides and carbon monoxide than
coal plants.
Gas-fired combined cycle plants were formerly promoted as a bridge to reduce emissions while
renewables ramp up. However, renewable prices have fallen low enough to allow a transition
directly from coal to solar and wind power, revealing that gas plants, with long returns on
investment, are more barrier than bridge and serve to delay a speedy transition to renewable
energy. At the same time, the lifecycle greenhouse gas emissions of both types of gas-fired
power plants have been shown to be far higher than previously estimated.
Q: Summarize the scientific findings in the Compendium on the health impacts of these
emissions from fracked gas power plants.
A: Air pollution is strongly associated with cardiovascular disease. In one of the first studies of
its kind, a research team investigated the effects of air pollution exposure among workers in
natural gas-fired power plants in Nigeria and matched them with healthy controls. They found
increased systolic blood pressure, increased pulse rate, and higher levels of the inflammatory
marker C-reactive protein in the workers compared to the controls. The longer the workers were
employed there, the more abnormal their results.
Pregnant women living near gas-fired power plants were more likely to give birth prematurely,
according to a study of more than 400,000 infants born in Florida between 2004 and 2005. This
study investigated associations between adverse birth outcomes and residential proximity to
several types of power plants, including those burning oil, gas, and solid waste.
There is a high degree of uncertainty about the methane emissions from natural gas-fired power
plants. As part of a study that also included oil refineries, a Purdue University team evaluated
methane emissions from three gas-fired power plants in Utah, Indiana, and Illinois during hours
of peak operation. Both fugitive methane leaks from the facility at large, as well as uncombusted
methane from the stacks, were measured using aircraft. Results showed that average methane
emission rates were larger than facility-reported estimates by factors of 21 to 120. The authors
concluded that gas-fired power plants “may be significant contributors to annual methane
emissions in the U.S. despite lack of facility emission reporting in U.S. inventories. Furthermore,
results suggest that the primary source of methane emissions at these facilities may be from
noncombustion sources.”
Q: Summarize the scientific findings in the Compendium on emissions upstream from
utility distribution systems.
A:  Recent research shows that fracking operations and their ancillary infrastructure are emitting
significantly more methane than disclosed by the industry and several times more methane than
estimated by current greenhouse gas inventories.

Much of the methane emitted from drilling and fracking activities and associated infrastructure
originates not from accidental leaks but from purposeful losses that are inherent in the design of
the industry’s machinery or inherent in normal operating use. Such losses are therefore
impossible to mitigate. Methane is vented into the atmosphere during routine maintenance on
compressor stations and pipelines; to create evaporative cooling for LNG storage and transport;
during the flowback period after a well is fracked; and as an emergency procedure to control
pressures.
Methane levels began to rise in 2007 and then shot up sharply in 2014, a time period that
corresponds to a massive increase in the use of fracking in North America.
Q: The Company says it has joined the ONE Future Coalition to reduce industry methane
emissions to 1% or less by 2025. Is 1% or less by 2025 possible?
A: No. Studies have found that methane leakage rates are much higher than the U.S.
Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) estimates and much higher than the fracking industry
reports.

Q: Summarize studies on the health impacts of gas in homes.
A: The health effects of indoor air pollution from gas are significant: gas stoves—a primary
source of combustion pollution inside the home—can cause spikes in emissions of nitrogen
dioxide and carbon monoxide to levels that would exceed outdoor emissions standards.7
Children in a home with a gas stove face a 24-42% increased risk of developing asthma.8 A
recent landmark study by Stanford University researchers found that gas stoves—used in over
one-third of U.S. homes—can expose people to pollutants that trigger respiratory disease and
that methane leakage from gas stoves has the same climate impact as 500,000
gasoline-powered cars.9 These studies make clear that the indoor air quality and climate
impacts of gas stoves have historically been greatly underestimated.

Q: Are you familiar with the goals and mandates of the Climate Leadership and
Community Protection Act (CLCPA)?
A: Yes
Q: How does Section 7(3) of the CLCPA relate to public health and fossil fuel
infrastructure?
A: Section 7(3) is critical to address the historically disproportionate public health impact of fossil
fuel infrastructure on Black, Brown, and low-income communities. Section 7(3) stipulates that
state agencies must prioritize reductions of greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions and co-pollutants
in Disadvantaged Communities.
The CLCPA defines Disadvantaged Communities as “communities that bear burdens of
negative public health effects, environmental pollution, impacts of climate change, and possess
certain socioeconomic criteria, or comprise high-concentrations of low- and moderate- income
households, as identified pursuant to section 75-0111 of this article.”
As the Compendium documents, an overwhelming body of scientific literature has found that
fracked gas infrastructure is disproportionately sited in communities of color and low-income
communities.
Q: Does Consolidated Edison’s proposal to invest $70.4 million over the next 5 years in
its Astoria LNG facility align with the goals of section 7(3) of  the CLCPA?
A: No.
Q: Does Consolidated Edison’s proposal to invest $67 million over the next 5 years in its
East River Power Plant align with the goals of section 7(3) of  the CLCPA?
A: No.
Q: Do you support the Company’s proposal for a Certified Natural Gas pilot program as a
way to meet the goals of the CLCPA?
A: No. While the Company claims it cannot provide the percentage of its delivered gas that is
produced through hydraulic fracturing, at least 79% of U.S. natural gas is produced through
fracking. The Company has disclosed that over 50% of the gas it delivers comes from the
Marcellus Shale fracking region of Pennsylvania.
Our examination uncovered no evidence that fracking can be undertaken in a manner that is
safe for human health or climate stability (upon which human health depends).

Q: Do you have any other recommendations for the Public Service Commission during
this proceeding?
A: The Public Service Commission must take into consideration the urgency of the next three
years as the planet faces imminent climate catastrophe. In April 2022, the third report of the
Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), which focused on mitigation, made clear
that the window to avert runaway, irreversible climate impact is rapidly closing. To prevent global
warming from exceeding 1.5 degrees Celsius—after which severe harm will accelerate—rising
emissions must end before 2025. In a stark warning to fossil fuel investors, the IPCC made clear
that future fossil fuel assets will become stranded if governments act in accordance with the
science. The Public Service Commission should deny rate recovery for Consolidated Edison’s
fossil fuel infrastructure projects because these projects will—indeed, must—become stranded
assets if the Company is to adhere to its obligations under the CLCPA and if New York State is
to phase out fossil fuels on the timeline required by the CLCPA.

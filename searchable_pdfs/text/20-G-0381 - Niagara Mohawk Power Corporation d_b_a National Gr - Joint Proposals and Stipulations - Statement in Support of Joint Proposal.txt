POINT I 
 
 THE JOINT PROPOSAL IS IN THE PUBLIC INTEREST 
AND SHOULD BE ADOPTED WITHOUT MODIFICATION 
 
 
 
On July 31, 2020, Niagara Mohawk filed electric and gas rate cases with the 
Commission seeking to establish new electric and gas delivery rates effective for the period July 
1, 2021 through June 30, 2022 (the “Rate Year”).  Following the submission of direct and/or 
rebuttal testimony and exhibits by Niagara Mohawk, Staff, and many intervener parties (including 
but not limited to Multiple Intervenors), the parties engaged in extensive discovery and settlement 
negotiations, culminating in the filing of the Joint Proposal on September 27, 2021.  The Joint 
Proposal constitutes a comprehensive resolution of the issues raised in Cases 20-E-0380 and 20-
G-0381, as well as Case 19-M-0133, pertaining to Niagara Mohawk’s recording of a pension 
settlement loss.6  For the reasons set forth below, the Joint Proposal is in the public interest and 
should be adopted by the Commission, without modification, in these proceedings. 
 
Initially, Multiple Intervenors focuses herein on explaining its underlying rationale 
for executing and supporting the Joint Proposal.  In particular, Multiple Intervenors addresses the 
provisions of the Joint Proposal of most significance to its members that procure electric and gas 
service from Niagara Mohawk.  Importantly, the Joint Proposal is – and should be evaluated as – 
a comprehensive resolution of literally hundreds of complex and often interrelated issues by a very 
large group of Signatories (with participation by, and reflecting input from, many other active 
parties).

Multiple Intervenors supports the term of the Joint Proposal.  Under most – but 
perhaps not all – circumstances, three-year terms constitute the “sweet spot” for negotiated 
resolutions of utility rate proceedings.  In the litigation context, Commission rate decisions only 
are in effect for a minimum period of one year, which allows utilities to file new rate cases very 
shortly (i.e., within one month) following the conclusion of their prior rate proceedings.  In the 
settlement context, however, Joint Proposals routinely include provisions whereby the subject 
utility agrees to “stay-out” or refrain from filing for new rates to become effective prior to the 
conclusion of the settlement term, absent exigent circumstances.  (See, e.g., JP at 122-24.) 
 
While Multiple Intervenors asserts that no settlement is preferable to a bad 
settlement, where a Joint Proposal is in the public interest, a negotiated, multi-year rate plan 
provides certain potential advantages over a fully-litigated rate proceeding.  For customers, multi-
year rate settlements can provide increased rate certainty.  They also can provide an opportunity 
to “levelize” and/or moderate near-term rate impacts over a longer period of time.  From the 
utility’s perspective, increased revenue certainty is provided, allowing it to focus on operating as 
efficiently as possible without repeated, resource-intensive forays into the rate-setting process.  
Other benefits of multi-year rate plans to utilities, customers, and regulators include, but are not 
necessarily limited to: (a) the avoidance of annual or nearly-annual rate case litigation; (b) the 
opportunity to resolve certain issues creatively in a manner for which litigation often is not

conducive; (c) the opportunity to transition more gradually, over time, to certain desired outcomes; 
and (d) the opportunity to resolve multiple issues collectively in a manner that balances competing 
interests and equitable considerations, as opposed to the potential for “winner-take-all” outcomes 
that may flow from litigation. 
 
It also should be noted that comprehensive settlements of utility rate proceedings 
require extensive commitments of time and resources by active parties, often well in excess of 
what otherwise would be required under “normal” rate case litigation.  Consequently, it is 
questionable whether rate settlements shorter than three years in duration are “worth” the increased 
effort involved. 
 
On the other hand, because settlements rely, by necessity, on multi-year forecasts 
and projections of various cost elements, numbers of customers, quantities of deliveries, etc., they 
often present an increased level of risk and uncertainty – on all sides – compared to annual rate 
case litigation.  It is fair to state that the longer the rate plan, the greater the risk that the forecasts 
and the projections underlying the plan will prove to be materially inaccurate.  Thus, there is a 
definite balance between negotiating rate plans that are (i) not so short such that they fail to justify 
the increased time and resources required, and (ii) not so long in duration that they impose 
undesirable and/or unreasonable risks on customers and/or the subject utility. 
 
In Multiple Intervenors’ opinion, three-year rate plans often reflect the “sweet spot” 
of this balancing effort, while recognizing that, depending on the circumstances, negotiated rate 
plans of shorter or longer durations also may satisfy the public interest standard. 
 
Here, the Signatories have made provision in the event Niagara Mohawk elects to 
refrain from filing for new electric and gas delivery rates effective July 1, 2024, i.e., the day after 
Rate Year 3 ends.  This Stay-out Period, defined in the Joint Proposal (see JP at 7), would last up

During the Stay-out Period, were it to occur, Niagara Mohawk would be authorized 
to recover, via surcharge mechanisms: (a) the pre-tax electric and gas revenue requirements

Importantly, such potential recoveries are subject to several conditions.  First, any

via surcharges to the extent doing so would cause the utility to earn a return on equity (“ROE”) in

which recovery almost certainly would be granted by the Commission were the utility to file new

form of net regulatory liabilities.  Pursuant to the Joint Proposal, the Signatories agreed that in

Electric and Gas Revenue Allocations 
 
Multiple Intervenors supports the electric and gas revenue allocations set forth in 
the Joint Proposal.  (JP at 15, 25-26; App. 2 at Sch. 2; App. 3 at Sch. 2-3.)  Absent a satisfactory

positions on various cost-of-service issues.  Rather than seeking to reach unanimity on those

E. 
Electric and Gas Large Non-Residential Rate Designs 
 
In contrast to revenue allocation, which pertains to how responsibility for a utility’s 
revenue requirement is allocated amongst the utility’s different service classes, rate design refers 
to how rates within a particular class, or subclass, are designed to produce the targeted level of 
revenues for that class or subclass.  Thus, the resolution of large non-residential rate design issues, 
for instance, does not affect other types of customers.

Multiple Intervenors’ primary focus in on S.C. 3 and S.C. 3A rates.  The main elements of S.C. 3

Charge (applied on a per kW basis).12

electric revenue requirement changes in each of Rate Year 1, Rate Year 2, and Rate Year 3, the 
S.C. 3 and S.C. 3A Customer Charges would increase by specified amounts, with the remainder 
of those classes’ revenue responsibility recovered through Demand Charges.  (See JP at 15; App. 
2 at Sch. 3.)  The proposed modifications to the existing S.C. 3 and S.C. 3A rate designs are

POINT II 
 
 THE JOINT PROPOSAL SATISFIES THE CRITERIA SET 
FORTH IN THE SETTLEMENT GUIDELINES AND 
SHOULD BE ADOPTED WITHOUT MODIFICATION 
 
 
 
In 1992, the Commission formulated criteria upon which settlement agreements in 
rate proceedings should be reviewed and evaluated, and issued an order and guidelines setting 


--- Page 21 ---
19 
forth such criteria (hereinafter, “Settlement Guidelines”).16  As demonstrated below, the Joint 
Proposal filed in these proceedings satisfies the Settlement Guidelines in all respects and, 
therefore, should be adopted, without modification, by the Commission. 
 
The Settlement Guidelines include the following criteria for evaluating settlement 
agreements negotiated in rate proceedings: 
(1) the settlement’s consistency with law and with the regulatory, 
economic, social, and environmental policies of the Commission 
and the State; (2) whether the results compare favorably with the 
likely result of full litigation and is within the range of reasonable 
outcomes; (3) whether the settlement strikes a fair balance among 
the interests of ratepayers and investors and the long-term soundness 
of the utility; (4) the existence of a rational basis for decision; (5) 
the completeness of the record; and (6) whether the settlement is 
contested.17 
 
These criteria are satisfied in these proceedings. 
 
Initially, the Joint Proposal is consistent with the “law and with the regulatory, 
economic, social, and environmental policies of the Commission and the State.”  The Joint 
Proposal seeks to set electric and gas delivery rates for a three-year period.  As detailed in Point I, 
the Joint Proposal provides adequate funding for Niagara Mohawk to maintain, and hopefully 
improve, the provision of safe and reliable electric and gas service, while also utilizing available 
customer credits to moderate delivery rate impacts. 
 
With respect to the Commission’s social and environmental policies, Multiple 
Intervenors notes that the Joint Proposal, inter alia, reflects Commission-mandated funding for

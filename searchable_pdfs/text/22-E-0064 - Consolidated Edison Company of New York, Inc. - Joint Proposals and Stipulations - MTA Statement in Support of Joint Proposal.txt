The Metropolitan Transportation Authority (“MTA”) hereby submits this Statement in 
Support of the February 16, 2023, Joint Proposal, filed in the above-captioned proceeding.1  
From the MTA’s standpoint, the Joint Proposal resolves the issues that arose in the instant 
electric rate proceeding and satisfies the New York State Public Service Commission’s 
(“Commission”) settlement guidelines that are used to determine whether a settlement is in the 
public interest.  Therefore, it should be adopted. 
 
The MTA, including its subsidiary and affiliate agencies, operate North America’s largest 
public transportation network, serving a population of approximately 15.3 million people in the 
5,000 square mile area fanning out from New York City through Long Island, southeastern New 
York State, and Connecticut.2  The MTA is a high-volume electric consumer in the Consolidated 
Edison Company of New York, Inc. (“Company”) service territory and relies upon electricity for 
many of its operations to transport the public.   
 
The MTA operates a fleet of approximately 5,780 buses in New York City.3  It has the 
highest bus ridership in the country, with approximately 557 million annual riders—more than

The MTA actively participated in settlement meetings to provide input regarding barriers

reasonable steps to facilitate fleet electrification: the Estimated and Delayed Billing Metric

First, the Estimated Billing Metric addresses an issue that MTA has been experiencing 
over the past several years.  As noted in a December 2022 Report from the MTA Office of the 
Inspector General (“OIG”), many MTA electric accounts have been receiving estimated bills on 
a long-term—or even permanent—basis.8  These estimated bills are attributable to technical 
issues with the meters and lead to bills using incorrectly measured energy usage, which, in turn, 
can create difficulties for consumers to manage and predict operating expenses and can cause 
consumers to miss beneficial price opportunities with the New York Independent System 
Operator, Inc.9  For example, in an audit of MTA’s accounts receiving consecutive estimated 
bills over a 24-month period, OIG identified overpayments when estimates failed to account for 
known periods of service reductions, and therefore, lower electricity usage.10  The Company’s 
initial rate filing and testimony did not include any tangible commitments to address the issue.   
 
Over the course of settlement negotiations, the settlement parties agreed to the Estimated 
Billing Metric.11  The metric is split between “Metric 1” which measures estimated bill rates for 
electric residential, electric non-residential non-non demand, and gas accounts, and “Metric 2” 
which measures electric non-residential demand, New York Power Authority electric, and gas 
non-residential accounts.12  The metric would establish a baseline of the average percent of bills

Second, regarding the Interconnection EAM, a major focus of the MTA during this 
proceeding was to advocate for the Company to develop programs that facilitate the 
electrification of medium- and heavy-duty electric vehicle (“MHEV”) fleets.  The parties’ 
testimony in the proceeding evidences a broad consensus that the deployment of MHEVs would 
play a critical role in achieving the State’s climate and clean transportation policies, such as the 
CLCPA, which sets the goal of reducing net economywide GHG emissions of 40% by 2030 and 
85% by 2050 from 1990 levels.17  Notably, MHEV deployment would achieve 
disproportionately high reductions in GHG and particulate matter emissions per vehicle mile 
traveled as compared to light-duty vehicles, and would bring significant clean energy benefits to 
disadvantaged communities due to the location of MHEV depots and transportation routes.18  
The State is also a signatory party to the “Multi-State Zero Emission Medium- and Heavy-Duty 
Vehicle Initiative - Memorandum of Understanding” (“MOU”) in which the State established a 
goal to make 30% of sales of all new MHEV in its jurisdiction zero emission by 2030, and 100% 
zero emission by 2050.19  In addition, on September 8, 2021, Governor Kathy Hochul signed 
legislation, A.4302/S.2758, into law, setting a Statewide goal that 100% of medium- and heavy-
duty vehicles offered for sale or lease in the State be zero-emission by 2045 for all operations 
economywide where feasible.20

during settlement negotiations, Settlement Parties developed an alternative incentive mechanism 
to address a significant barrier to fleet electrification: the lengthy interconnection timeline.  
 
One of the main barriers the MTA has experienced in electrifying its bus fleet has been 
an excessively long interconnection process.  As shown in the Joint Proposal, requests for the 
installation of new vault service and new high-tension service—the categories of work necessary 
to meet the new load required by fleet electrification at MTA bus depots—take approximately 
three and six years, respectively, for the work to be completed.23  MHEV purchases by fleet 
owners is rendered pointless if they cannot charge.   
 
 
Accordingly, the Joint Proposal recommends the Interconnection EAM that would 
incentivize the Company for reducing the timeline of the load interconnection process.  The 
EAM would measure the percent reduction in the time for interconnection of transportation 
electrification projects requiring service for at least 300 kilowatts of new electric load.24  The 
baseline would be calculated by the historic average number of days from initial case submission 
to final energization, weighted by the number of megawatts for the jobs completed across six job 
categories.25  The EAM is measured by work categories to prevent the Company from focusing 
on smaller jobs to achieve its overall reduction target.  The Company would earn a maximum of 
six basis points if it reduces the interconnection timeline by an average of 25% in Rate Year 1, 
30% in Rate Year 2, and 35% in Rate Year 3.  The MTA believes that the Interconnection EAM 
would tangibly improve the load interconnection process for fleet owners and, therefore, 
supports its inclusion in the Joint Proposal.  
 
According to Commission’s Settlement Guidelines, a settlement should balance the 
interests of ratepayers and investors and the long-term viability of a utility.26  In addition, a 
settlement “should produce results that were within the range of reasonable results that would 
likely have arisen from a Commission decision in a litigated proceeding.”27  A settlement should 
also be consistent with Commission and State environmental, social, and economic policies.28

The objectives of the Joint Proposal’s Estimated Billing Metric and Interconnection EAM 
are consistent with the Commission’s Settlement Guidelines.  The Estimated Billing Metric is in 
the interest of ratepayers because replacing estimated meter bills with actual bills should 
facilitate accurately billing ratepayers for the energy they use; avoid over- or under-billing; and 
afford the ratepayers the opportunity to take advantage of beneficial pricing opportunities.  With 
respect to the Interconnection EAM, facilitating expeditious MHEV fleet deployment would 
reduce emissions to the benefit of ratepayers who are located in the Company’s service territory.  
In addition, discussion of these or similar programs would likely have arisen within a litigated 
proceeding.  Both metrics create an incentive for the Company to make meaningful 
improvements to their pre-existing billing and interconnection processes.  Moreover, as 
discussed, the objectives of the Interconnection EAM are consistent with Commission and State 
policies.   
 
Therefore, the Joint Proposal is in the public interest and should be adopted.

We, the undersigned, write to express our unqualified opposition to the joint proposal filed by National
Grid and the New York State Department of Public Service on June 2, 2021. We oppose it on the grounds
that it represents a failure of corporate and regulatory entities to use their considerable powers to steer
New York’s energy systems towards the more sustainable and equitable model that our dire ecological and
economic moment demands. We also oppose it on the grounds that, while it is the product of a proceeding
commenced, like so many others under the Commission’s purview, in the name of democratic stakeholder
participation, it does not adequately reflect the input of a majority of those stakeholders and was largely
negotiated in bad faith. We implore the PSC to reject this proposal on all of these counts.  
We joined these rate proceedings in hopes that, as parties with deep and longstanding relationships to the
communities of ratepayers we both belong to and represent, our voices would be heard. These ratepayers
are, after all, the ones who stand to bear the brunt of new infrastructure and the rate increases that would
likely accompany it. Yet many of us did this only after realizing that our voices risked not being heard in
another PSC proceeding, the denial of service investigation (Case 19-G-0678), for which we initially had
similar hopes. Our opposition to the joint proposal cannot be adequately understood apart from that
proceeding, and so we turn to it here.
Broken Promises and the Denial of Service Investigation
As nearly every page of the Order Adopting and Approving Settlement confirms, the denial of service
investigation was framed as a means of addressing the grievances of ratepayers affected by National
Grid’s gas moratorium and/or shut out of the decision-making process about infrastructure more
generally. Regardless of how it was understood internally to PSC or National Grid staff, it was offered to
the public as being for the public, who in turn took it on good faith that its opinions mattered this time and
would finally be heard.
The PSC and the Companies made gestures towards this end. Fines were issued, forcing the Companies to
compensate affected members of the public and contribute considerable sums to clean energy and energy
efficiency—solutions long favored by ratepayers. The Natural Gas Long-Term Capacity Report1 was
written to inform the public on possible long-term infrastructure solutions. Virtual public hearings and
opportunities for public comment were provided as a forum for debating those solutions. And the
Long-Term Capacity Supplemental Report (LTCSR)2 was provided as a way to tie public feedback on
possible solutions into a final assessment of options. All of this, we believed, was meant to democratically
involve ratepayers where they had not been involved before.
That those ratepayers were desperate to be involved was made obvious by the fact that, despite much of
this happening during the distraction-filled first weeks of the worst public health crisis in a century, they
showed up anyway—and in droves. Over 900 people attended the virtual hearings. Many more
contributed over 9,000 public comments. The Energy Futures Group submitted a report casting doubt on
National Grid’s claim for the need for more gas3 based upon the disparity between National Grid’s
numbers and those of the U.S. Energy Information Administration’s Annual Energy Outlook for 20204,

All of this was to come to a conclusion, if the PSC was to be believed, in June 2020, when the state was to
arrive at a decision on long-term capacity options. As the Order Adopting and Approving Settlement put

National Grid agrees that a long-term option or options should be identified and agreed to by the State of

long-term option or options in place and functioning in approximately two years.
The public was given no reason to believe that this deadline wouldn’t be honored, nor that, given the
public-directed nature of the case, it wouldn’t at least be notified if the deadline couldn’t be met. But the
public received no such notice, and June 2020 came and went without a word from the PSC. What did
come to the public’s attention was that National Grid had resumed confidential settlement negotiations in
the rate proceeding, raising suspicions that discussions related to lucrative infrastructure might be
continuing in that far less transparent and more explicitly revenue-focused domain. Were this to be true, it
would mean that the thousands of comments filed by ratepayers in the other proceeding would be left
behind there, preventing a significant record of opposition from being included on the record of an
essentially closed-door case with little to no public or press scrutiny. It is this fear that prompted most of
us to join the current proceeding with the intention of continuing to represent the thousands whose voices
had been stranded in the other case.
In public communications with stakeholders, Staff made it clear enough that it was indeed their intention
to continue discussion of long-term infrastructure in the rate proceeding, effectively blurring the line
between the two cases. It is thus a problem of the DPS’s own making that, from the perspective of the
undersigned, the current rate case has become a matter not only of the potential costs imposed on
ratepayers related to new infrastructure but also 1) a matter of that infrastructure itself and 2) what is still
owed to the public in the other proceeding. It is for this reason that complaints related to the insufficient
handling of Case 19-G-0678 cannot and must not be dismissed as irrelevant to the case at hand. It is also
for this reason that scrutiny of the joint proposal can only be considered fair and complete when
considering this larger narrative of what can only be called ratepayer betrayal.
A Dishonest Public Proceeding
Yet this matter is not as simple as a promising proceeding being hijacked by a less promising one. As we
would eventually learn, Case 19-G-0678 was itself a largely theatrical endeavor from the start.
The public had been led to believe that the options presented in the Long-Term Capacity Supplemental
Report were all truly contingent on the final decision to be given by Staff in June 2020. Yet as capex
reports reveal, National Grid had already spent nearly $1 million on Greenpoint vaporizers 13 & 14, for
example, by the time the LTCSR came out, and would go on to spend $12.5 million on them in 2020. It
did not help matters that the report was ambiguous as to whether the final phases of the MRI pipeline,
which were also already underway, were intended for overall gas system reliability or to supply a key
component in the Companies’ distributed-infrastructure alternative to the Williams NESE pipeline, which,
like NESE, the public had also vociferously opposed.

In short, construction on projects that were presented to the public as contingent on NESE being cancelled
had already begun well before the DEC rejected the NESE permits. (The independent monitor, in his
eighth quarterly report6, confirms that Vaporizers 13 & 14 were planned before NESE was cancelled).
Thus, it wasn’t so much that no decision was made by June in Case 19-G-0678 as that it had been made
long before then but was kept from the public. Case 19-G-0678 was largely a sham.
Negotiating in Bad Faith
This did not set a good precedent for confidential settlement negotiations in the rate proceeding. The
Companies are not in the habit of building infrastructure if they don’t expect to be allowed to ultimately
recover its costs from ratepayers, and much of the construction on this infrastructure was not only widely
opposed but had begun behind ratepayers’ backs. Moreover, the precedent of building millions of dollars
in widely opposed infrastructure before it has rate recovery approval is an egregious one that speaks as
much to the Companies’ fiduciary recklessness as it does to their arrogance. As of the release date of the
joint proposal, the Companies had completed at least $120 million of construction on widely opposed
infrastructure that did not yet have a cost recovery plan. This includes, among other things, the $12.5
million spent on LNG Vaporizers 13 & 14, which, as the independent monitor affirms, are in jeopardy of
not even receiving the necessary permits required for their operation. It also includes tens of millions for
the MRI pipeline, a project that is so widely loathed that activists have chained themselves to it on three
occasions to stop construction.
Staff clearly did nothing during confidential settlement negotiations to stop the Companies from forging
ahead with these projects, despite their obvious precarity, despite the overwhelming public opposition,
and despite the fact that the Companies did not yet have permission to bill them to ratepayers. Given the
circumstances described above, we should not need to explain why this only furthered our suspicion that
the process of approving or rejecting infrastructure in supposedly democratic forums was a fully rigged
one. Get it in the ground, the Companies seem to be saying, and it will be too late for Staff to reject it.
Pretend that we can’t do anything to stop construction, Staff seems to be saying, and we’ll have cover for
approving what would otherwise be a stranded asset. All of this, of course, at a time when we shouldn’t
be building any new fossil fuel infrastructure at all.
We are aware of the Demand Capacity Surcharge Mechanism, which establishes certain criteria that must
be met before certain projects can be built or charged to ratepayers. Yet precisely because of the inaction
on the part of Staff described above, coupled with the excessive time it has taken to come to this joint
proposal, the Companies have been able to fully complete several projects that should have been included,
or included more fully, in the DCSM purview—projects like Phase 4 of the MRI pipeline (especially
given the fierce and widespread opposition to it) and various CNG projects. Yet as the joint proposal
stands, ratepayers would be fully charged for the MRI pipeline, and very likely charged for the CNG as
well.
While certain metrics must be hit for the latter to happen, we in no way believe that there will be an
honest accounting of those metrics. The Companies have squandered too much of the public’s trust for it
to be otherwise. Moreover, we cannot be expected to trust that the “independent consultant” hired to
assess the need of other projects within the DCSM will truly be independent, given the likelihood that the
consultant will be a friend to the gas industry with inadequate experience assessing the possibilities of
electrification and demand-side solutions. We also cannot expect the consultant to be immune to being
influenced by the Companies own narrative about the need for more gas, one that has been thoroughly
discredited by no fewer than four reports by independent experts. Lastly, we are all too familiar with the
Companies’ past attempts to use “outside experts” to support its agenda in other ways, as with the report

It was because of the continued construction on widely opposed infrastructure during confidential
settlement negotiations—infrastructure that did not yet have rate recovery approval— coupled with these

of those negotiations, which, much like Case 19-G-0678, were clearly happening in bad faith. And as if it

made in the second, the PSC also made the mind-boggling decision to further erode the public’s faith in

$20 million towards clean energy, which was arguably the most important component of the settlement
fines and yet the biggest threat to the Companies’ business model; it also allows fine money to be treated

aspect of this is that PSC has allowed the Companies to frame this reappropriation of $20 million as
necessary to avoid rate increases on customers during Year 1 of the rate plan—at a time when they have
recklessly spent over $120 million on unneeded and unwanted infrastructure that, in large part, has no
cost recovery plan and that, in the case of the LNG vaporizers, might not even be built. If it wasn’t clear
before where the state’s priorities lie, it certainly is now.
Enabling Profit-Fueled Climate Denialism
While we are encouraged that the Companies’ have at least gestured in a positive direction—ending the
marketing of gas, coupling LPP replacement with studies of geothermal feasibility, referring customers to
Con Edison for electrification, studying the effects of climate policy on depreciation, considering Scope 3
emissions in its climate studies, and other things—we remind the Commission of the almost comedic
inadequacy of these concessions in the context of what is truly needed to address our climate problem:
ending the use of gas.
This sad incrementalism is clearly due to National Grid’s corporate mandate to maintain profits and
dividends for a privileged few at the expense of doing what is just and right for the many. And yet, as a
gas-only provider in the Downstate region, operating at a time when all experts agree that we must
electrify and do so as quickly as possible, National Grid is in trouble. Its assets risk becoming stranded
due to electrification, and it is clearly betting on hydrogen and RNG as future saviors that will give new
life to its obsolete infrastructure while keeping an outdated business model on life support, and all while
 sustaining financial gains for its investors.
Yet the recent report on carbon neutrality, co-commissioned by the City of New York and the utilities
themselves8, is a glaring testament to the denialism and desperation at the heart of this profit-driven
gamble. While placing hydrogen, biogenic RNG, and synthetic RNG at the center of all three possible
pathways to carbon neutrality, the report nonetheless—and on multiple occasions—emphasizes the
extreme unlikelihood of these technological solutions ever coming to fruition, not only because of
political opposition but because of excessive costs.  To be available, they would require, among other
things, commodity costs dropping precipitously, massive new pipelines to carry RNG into the city, and
invaluable renewable electricity being diverted into the production of costly “green hydrogen” for no
other reason than to prevent the assets of stubborn billion-dollar utilities from becoming stranded. All of

this, in turn, would likely force low-income gas customers to shoulder more and more of the costs of
maintaining increasingly obsolete infrastructure as affluent customers abandon it to electrify.
It is unconscionable that a state regulator tasked with protecting ratepayers would back a long-term
plan—rooted in such contingencies. The recklessness of National Grid in the face of not only climate
change but the basic requirement to protect its customers cannot be overstated. One need only flip through
the reports filed by the independent monitor in Case 19-G-0678 to understand the way that their extreme
corporate arrogance, rooted in a belief that the regulatory state will always err on the side of capital, has
translated into an extreme lack of preparedness to deal with changes to our energy economy that have
been a decade in the making.
Because electrification threatens the Companies’ customer base and assets and hydrogen and other
“green” fuels are fantasies for some distant future, they have chosen to do little in the present but stay the
backwards course—one that has no contingency plan because such a thing could never be profitable.
Nothing encapsulates that shameful position better than the words of a National Grid executive, quoted in
the monitor’s eighth quarterly report: “If Iroquois ExC [a crucial part of the Companies” long-term supply
plan] doesn’t go through, I don’t have a solution to that. And I don’t know that I’m going to come up with
one.”
In the absence of adequate solutions proposed by the Companies, it falls to you, the PSC, to come up with
them instead. Unless those solutions involve a concrete plan to reduce and phase out gas use, as was
accomplished in the NYSEG/RG&E case, then you, too, will be complicit in climate denialism by way of
supporting incrementalism. The solution will not be found in an even greater proliferation of new
proceedings and cases, kept isolated from one another when it is convenient and brought together when it
is not, that confuse bureaucratic can-kicking for bold action. And it certainly won’t be found in joint
proposals that would result in actually increasing the supply of gas to New York City at a time when we
should have gotten off of it yesterday.
We are tired of all of this. We are tired of technocratic fixes, fancy metrics, and outside experts
substituting for simply saying no to more fracked gas. We are tired of staff condescendingly casting aside
tens of thousands of public comments and records of overwhelming political opposition as though they
were merely the outpourings of naive people who don’t understand how the world works. We are tired of
claims about gas demand made by a multi-billion dollar company whose gas assets are at stake being
privileged over the opinions of numerous well-respected independent experts. We are tired of projects
being artificially segmented in these and other proceedings in ways that obscure their true impacts and
implications. In short, we are tired of the state getting in the way of a more democratically informed
energy system that truly represents the will of ratepayers.
Our demands are simple: we want a plan to get off gas and we want a commitment to build no new fossil
fuel infrastructure. And we implore you to reject this joint proposal on the grounds that it offers neither.

III. OVERVIEW OF EMBEDDED COST OF SERVICE STUDIES 
7 
Q. 
What is the purpose of the ECOS studies? 
8 
A. 
The cost to serve customers of any utility company generally consists of operating 
9 
expenses and return on rate base.  For the historical test period, these costs are contained 
10 
in the books and records of the company and are readily available to establish the overall 
11 
cost to serve the collective body of customers of the utility.  The cost to provide service 
12 
related to individual functions and to individual service classes can vary greatly based on 
13 
the facilities used to provide service and the level of demand placed on the system.  
14 
These costs to provide service to individual service classes, however, are not all readily 
15 
available from a company’s accounting system. 
16 
Historically, the ECOS studies were developed to assign or allocate each relevant 
17 
component of cost on an appropriate basis to service classifications to determine the cost 
18 
to serve the respective classes.  ECOS studies traditionally have been used as a guide in 
19 
revenue allocation and rate design to provide a measure of the relationship between the 
20 
revenues supplied by each service class and the cost of plant and expenses imposed on a 
21 
utility’s system by the customers in those classes.  These studies enable a company to 
22 
calculate a rate of return for each service class so that comparisons can be made between 
23

each class of service and the total system return.  The Revenue Allocation and Rate 
1 
Design Panel discusses how the Companies used the results of the ECOS studies as a 
2 
guide in establishing the proposed delivery service class revenue requirements for electric 
3 
and gas service. 
4 
 
The ECOS studies also serve an additional purpose.  As explained below, 
5 
functionalization has always been the first step in the cost of service process.  This part of 
6 
the process produces a logical breakdown of costs that facilitates classification of costs 
7 
into major categories of Customer-, Demand-, and Energy-related components prior to 
8 
the full allocation of costs to the various service classes.  With rate unbundling, the 
9 
functionalization step takes on heightened importance because the embedded costs of 
10 
each functional service will provide the basis for developing unbundled rates for the 
11 
Commission-designated competitive services, e.g., Supply Procurement (also referred to 
12 
as the “Merchant Function Charge” or “MFC”) and Bill Issuance and Payment 
13 
Processing (“BIPP”). 
14 
Q. 
Please provide an overview of the process for conducting ECOS studies. 
15 
A. 
The ECOS study process is essentially comprised of the following three steps:  1) 
16 
functionalization; 2) classification; and 3) allocation. 
17

What is the first step in the cost of service process? 
18

Starting with an historical revenue requirement, investments and expenses are separated 
19 
by the functional service categories that they support.  
20

21 
operating expenses in each Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (“FERC”) account to 
22 
a specific function.  For both the electric and gas ECOS studies, the Companies assigned

Q. 
What is the second step in the cost of service process? 
7 
A. 
The second step in the studies is classification, where functionalized costs are classified 
8 
based upon the characteristics of the type of service being provided – whether the costs 
9 
are primarily Customer-, Demand-, or Energy-related.  For example, electric 
10 
Transmission costs and gas Transmission costs are considered Demand-related, electric 
11 
hydro plant costs are considered Energy-related, and services, meters, and billing costs 
12 
are considered Customer-related. 
13 
Q. 
What is the third step in the ECOS process? 
14 
A. 
The third and final step is the allocation of the functionalized and classified costs to the 
15 
various service classes using a variety of allocation factors developed to reflect the cost 
16 
responsibility that each class causes or imposes on the system for each element being 
17 
allocated. 
18

using this application, the Companies are able to specifically model their operations, treat

IV. TREATMENT OF COMMON COSTS (ELECTRIC & GAS) 
1 
Q. 
Please describe the Companies’ methodology for functionalizing costs that cannot be 
2 
directly assigned to one of the functions. 
3 
A. 
As I previously discussed, such costs must be indirectly allocated on the basis of some 
4 
related pattern or group of accounts.  The categories of costs that were indirectly 
5 
allocated to the functions include General Plant, Administrative & General Expense 
6 
(“A&G”), Customer Care, and Uncollectibles. 
7 
Q. 
How did the Companies indirectly allocate general and common plant to the functions? 
8 
A. 
The majority of general and common plant accounts are allocated to each function 
9 
consistent with the functional distribution of labor costs to each account and function. 
10 
Q. 
How did the Companies indirectly allocate the costs recorded in the numerous A&G 
11 
expense accounts to each of the functions? 
12 
A. 
The Companies used a methodology that is in accordance with the Commission’s August 
13 
23, 2006 Order in Case 05-E-1222.  Under this methodology, A&G accounts are 
14 
reviewed and divided into groups based on those more closely related to either Labor or 
15 
to plant.   
16 
 
Expenses in FERC Accounts 920 (Administrative and General Salaries), 921 
17 
(Office Supplies and Expenses), 922 (Administrative Expenses Transferred – Credit), 925 
18 
(Injuries and Damages), 926 (Employee Pensions and Benefits), 929 (Duplicate Charges 
19 
– Credit), 930.2 (Miscellaneous General Expenses), 932 (Maintenance of General Plant 
20

Q. 
Please explain the electric minimum system studies. 
7 
A. 
The costs associated with a distribution system are related to both the peak amount of 
8 
load that the system is designed to deliver and the number of customers and premises that 
9 
it is designed to serve.  Consequently, it is appropriate to allocate a portion of the 
10 
distribution system costs on a demand-related basis and a portion on a customer-related 
11 
basis.  In order to classify a certain portion of the distribution system costs as demand-
12 
related or customer-related, a minimum system study was conducted which included an 
13 
analysis for poles, conductors, and line transformers.  The minimum system analysis 
14 
compares the cost of a hypothetical minimum system (i.e., a system sized to simply 
15 
connect and provide access to customers) to the total cost of the entire system.  The 
16 
minimum system cost represents the customer-related costs; whereas the total costs less 
17 
the minimum system costs represents the demand-related costs (i.e., total cost is split 
18 
between the customer component and the demand component). 
19 
The Primary and Secondary Analysis for conductors described above provided the 
20 
total cost of all primary and secondary conductors.  A hypothetical minimum system 
21 
replacement cost was calculated by taking the total circuit feet of conductor that related to 
22 
the primary system and multiplying it by the replacement cost of a minimum sized 
23

primary conductor to calculate a minimum system replacement cost for primary 
1 
conductors.  The minimum system replacement cost was then compared to the total 
2

The Primary and Secondary Analysis for poles described above provided a total

Greetings:
The Town of Saugerties hereby requests the submission by the Applicant of a Scoping Plan Related 
to Climate Change for review by all parties as part of the current rate case. The lack of such a Plan 
renders a comprehensive and reasonable review of the justification of a rate increase for 2021-22 
impossible, since the steps taken by the Applicant in the coming rate year, as with all parties and agencies 
affected, will likely be conditioned to a large degree by its response to anticipated Climate Change 
activities. 


--- Page 2 ---
The current application is likely to contain fallacious assumptions because no clear picture emerges 
concerning the Applicant's overall response to Climate Change. One wrong assumption concerns the lack 
of a need for a Climate Change Scoping Plan at a time when New York State, among other parties, is 
currently unrolling the most comprehensive law on the topic in the country. Clear targets need to be 
established on a parallel level with the response of  the New York State Climate Action Council 
(NYSCAC) in its creation of a Scoping Plan to meet 100% renewables targets under the Climate 
Leadership and Community Protection Act (CLCPA) of 2019. 
New York State's response to Climate Change is comparable to the analogy suggested by the United 
Nations as necessary to meet the 2050 global warming standard of 2-degree Centigrade increase, 
preferably closer to 1.5-degrees, in order to bring Climate Change under control. The UN's Emission Gap 
2020 report this month stated that reduced greenhouse gas emissions by 7 percent can be expected from 
this year's global response to the COVID pandemic (Emissions Gap 2020, UNEP DTU Partnership 
[Nairobi 2020], "Definitions,"  pp. 3-4, 38-39). Inger Andersen, the report director and UN Environmental 
Programme director, in her "Foreword" wrote that a more responsible percentage of improvement (from 
the previous year) is 25, not 7%. Using this figure, and considering the lateness of the world's response to 
Climate Change and how quickly and troublesome it is unfolding, it is reasonable to expect that the fiscal 
spending in the current Application should mirror similar ambitious goals in addressing Climate Change. 
Since the rebound from a slowed economy likely to happen once the COVID is brought under control 
(presumably in 2021) may exceed, through sheer economic enthusiasm, what might have been projected 
had COVID never happened, the prognosis for advancement in the Climate Change fight may be 
significantly reduced in the coming year, making us, by proportion, much worse off in five, ten or fifty 
years. That at least is the presumption we should be operating under, just as similarly valid concerns 
occupy the NYSCAC Panels that are creating the state Scoping Plan. 
There is some evidence of a green approach to energy in these Application pages, but nothing near 
the level required. What one relatively small energy distributor in North America does may not actually 
be defined by the global sense of things, yet a presumption of verisimilitude is in order during these trying 
times. In its rate request submissions for 2021-22, CHGE should demonstrate a 25% effort in achieving 
Climate Change reductions, starting in 2021; lacking an effective measurement tool, even 7% of a change 
seems unlikely in this case. Only through a formal Scoping Plan for Climate Change can this goal be 
demonstrated and measured.
Grid compatibility and the continued use of natural gas are areas strongly in need of planning 
delineation in the work toward 100% renewables. To its credit, Central Hudson has taken steps in this 
direction in its intenrtions, which purpose is defined as the need "to access resources necessary to achieve 
the commitments and the various things that we need to invest in as part of our system and our people"; 
CHGE's Executive Vice President and CFO Christopher Capone adds: "we do recognize the extraordinary 
times that we are in" (Capone, Procedural and Technical Conference, October 6, 2020). To its credit, 
Central Hudson was recently cited by the utility industry with resolving a sticky technical problem that 
challenged grid reliability in the new Distributed Energy Resources (DER) scenario of power flowing 
both ways. The need for the Applicant to modernize "our infrastructure. . .to make it more aligned with 
climate and energy leadership," as with utilities across the county, is acknowledged, yet the details of such 
an awareness are vague because no clear explanation of the changes needed to cope with Climate Change 
is delineated. By citing its efforts in these regards in singular context related to addressing operations, 
Central Hudson consistently misses the forest of change that may yield comprehensive results; a Plan for 
the future is needed.
A major grid problem exists in bringing both large- and small-solar farms into the mix, ones in the 
25-plus megawatt size as well as kilowatt-sized ones. In enabling Community Choice Aggregation (CCA) 
in 2016, the NYSDPS encouraged small localities to seek to manage their energy on their own in 
fostering a "wider market based deployment of clean energy" by creating "large scale renewables, and 


--- Page 3 ---
distributed energy resources (DERs)" on a grand scale (NYSDPS, Case 14-M-0224, Enabling CCA 
Programs, April 24, 2016). Large-scale renewables, defined as 25 megawatts or larger, are extremely 
difficult for localities to encourage because of the land space needed and likely citizen and zoning 
impediments, and impossible to achieve given the poor status of the energy grid, which still relies on 19th 
century technology to operate. Some communities will not be able to participate in large-scale solar 
development because they lack access to transformers needed to handle the new power generation 
required. In some areas of the CHGE territory, grid connections do not exist for anything larger than 
home-based solar, nor are changes planned that would fit within an adequate Scoping Plan. One of the 
solutions the Applicant is pursuing concerns allocating costs for major grid expansion among future 
applicants, so that all the cost is not borne by one as has been the case in the past. This is a fine 
advancement; unfortunately, it was recognized five years ago and still awaits rendering: a Climate Change 
Plan might have hastened that process. The Applicant needs to expand its grid capabilities at the pace 
needed to make the 2050 state goals, and a Plan is a proven mechanism for doing so in an organized, cost-
efficient manner.  
 
Without a long-term plan, there are likely to be fallacious assumptions in justifying the continuation 
of natural gas usage in the "short" term. Granted, the NYSCAC Panels appear to acknowledge that 
continued natural gas usage will be necessary while renewables are developed, but nothing in this rate 
case application suggests that CHGE might ultimately do without it. Indeed, 2020 was a banner year for 
promoting natural gas in the home. Leakages of methane from the natural gas systems, one of the 
nuisance areas in need of attention cited in the NYSCAC review, gets a piecemeal review by the 
Applicant in its attention to current leaks program management and budget difficulties. CHGE has a plan 
for addressing leakages each year, but has not met its own goals nor shown the potential for their long-
term elimination.  A natural gas component to a Climate Change plan should include a retirement plan for 
gas lines, starting with those that malfunction or repeatedly experience leakages. The claim that since 
methane, the principal atmospheric pollutant of natural gas use, has a lifespan of only twenty years that 
dissipates any concerns over natural gas's long-term effect on GHGs in the atmosphere is disingenuous in 
the assumption that its production today is justified. The continued pollution negates the beneficial 
impacts of renewables that should be replacing natural gas-powered energy. The years in which we 
concede the necessity of continuing with the use of natural gas just make matters worse in the pollution it 
causes in the meantime.
Central Hudson's does recommend natural gas progress in several ways immediately anticipated in 
the next year or so. Leakages, wrong piping and other reasons necessitate these labors, but this is an area 
"still under development" due to a lack of information issues, and the incremental capital costs under 
review are being maintained for future ratepayer compensation. EV light vehicle infrastructure support, 
although ambitious, also defers to the next rate term the growth of Level 2 infrastructure and heavy duty 
vehicle progress. These will be treated as new business investments when that time comes, their 
incremental costs similarly deferred to the next rate term.  (see Direct Testimony of the Climate and 
Leadership Panel, August 27, 2020) 
With the advances in research and development of reliable, long-term battery storage currently 
becoming more and more relevant, it appears that energy providers should be in the heat of the mix 
toward a Climate Change solution. CHGE is also a contributing player in the renewable energy it can 
(hopefully) provide to replace peaker plant needs for the New York metropolitan area, yet seems to be out 
of the picture in the long-term scope of its current filing. The rollout of the CLCPA makes it clear that 
battery storage will be a key component of a long-term Climate Change solution. The larger picture of an 
Applicant fully engaged in meeting the challenges of the future can benefit from battery storage research 
and development as expressing its good intentions toward meeting climate leadership needs.
President-elect Joseph Biden has made decarbonization of the national electric grid "a centerpiece of 
his economic plan"  (Al Gore, "Where I Find Hope," The New York Times, Sunday Review, December 
13, 2020). Major utilities elsewhere in the United States have demonstrated an intention to move toward 


--- Page 4 ---
100% renewables, but Central Hudson, in this filing at least, seems aloof to the idea that this means now. 
To some extent this is reasonable, since the testimony was developed before the CLCPA began to unfold, 
but now, once we are into it and are learning the scope of what is needed, a rate case without a clear plan 
for Climate Change is a non-starter. Extraordinary necessities for dealing with Climate Change are 
apparent across the board.  World policies and economies and the stellar mission of the NYSCAC are the 
bellwethers for every fossil-using industry to adjust its thinking. Central Hudson needs to more 
aggressively go after Climate Change with the same anxious and focused attention that NYSCAC is 
giving it. The talent the Applicant can bring to the table means they will have a meaningful impact if they 
join the effort with shirts rolled up, as New York State is doing, and go to work. 
The CLCPA rollout is assuming that natural gas is a necessary bridge to the future, yet embracing 
that assumption should not give a free pass to fossil-reliant companies to delay before acting. Reducing 
natural gas to extinction should be part of a long-term plan if for no other reason than to demonstrate to 
wary investors the need for cautious yet positive thinking. (See Matthew Bundyk, "Pacific Northwest 
poised to test 100% renewable storage as utilities weigh gas vs. storage," Utility Dive, December 17, 
2020). Scenic Hudson has suggested a potentially viable replacement of a $600 million 500 megawatt 
natural gas peaker plant proposed in a different utility region  by a five-acre battery storage facility 
capable of holding 190 megawatts of energy for $191 million. The assumption is based in part on trends 
and changes in technology that obviate the need for a huge addition in energy generation. ("Danskammer: 
Isn't It Worth Getting It Right?", Scenic Hudson, December 16, 2020) In New York City, the New York 
Power Authority is working with local advocacy groups and environmental interests to phase out peaker 
plants, which create harm by their emissions and proximity to disadvantaged neighborhoods. Central 
Hudson needs to develop the proper planning response to become a player in this dramatic shift to 
renewable energies. Adding renewable energy is the answer. The company has expressed its intention to 
play its part, and has demonstrated a huge increase in renewable project applications from two to 1200 
megawatts in size, but has not shown the comprehensive response needed in a reasonable Scoping Plan 
form.
Matthew Smith, the DPS utility analyst, while finding inflated costs throughout most of the 
vegetation management program, proposed a $2.7 million reduction in CHGE's projected $4.7 million 
cost for the first year of its storm hardening component. (Smith, Prepared Testimony of Staff Electric 
Reliability and Vegetation Management Panel, NYSDPS, pp. 23-24) These reductions are being 
challenged by the Applicant, yet surely the reductions that survive scrutiny here and elsewhere (thanks to 
staff and intervenors) will be such as to fund a Climate Change Scoping Plan as well as reduce the 
consumer's rate burden.  
A zero-carbon grid 30 years from now needs to be modeled through proper planning that considers 
and incorporates cost-effective, lower-carbon alternatives to current practices. In the case of natural gas, 
the term should be replacements instead of alternative, in keeping with the need to create a no-carbon 
world as part of the Climate Change response.  The same might be said for the Applicant's plan to remove 
100,000 "trouble" trees over eight years: replacements of those trees to benefit carbon sequestration can 
reduce GHGs and even save money if Central Hudson utilizes its many attractive, well-mown parcels for 
plantings throughout its five regions. A long-term Plan can throw a broader light on ideas that address 
positive expectations as well as addressing negative ones. 
Looking forward, the industry needs a quantum leap in modeling to capture the full operational 
value of long-duration battery storage that can ensure reliability under a wide range of complex, 
interconnected system conditions. Including a long-term Scoping Plan in its application will also send a 
message to its investors that the Applicant is not moving ahead with its head in the sand and instead looks 
forward to greater investment opportunities and procurements that come with the higher goals of Climate 
Change. It will also help to put a dent in the $170 billion shortfall in grid upgrades needed in the country 
by 2025. (See Scott Burger, Marco Ferrara, Roderick Go and Arne Olson, "Where to Start when Making 
Distribution Grid Investments," Utility Drive, December 23, 2020.)

Funding for a Scoping Plan should be available in the savings that staff and numerous intervenors 
are pursuing across the entire Application, while still leaving room for reductions in customer bills. The 
detailed attention given by staff and intervenors to cost estimates is demonstrating that significant savings 
are there, despite the Applicant's aggressive attempts to offset the cost savings claims. The Applicant is 
also likely to create savings simply by realizing a more efficient and focused course of action in carrying 
out its business as part of an overall Climate Change world, where resilience and efficiency are hallmark 
elements of prudent spending.
These are unusual times. 2020 a highly unusual year in its recognition of the fact that Climate 
Change is real, it is bad, we caused it, and we can do something about it. Developing a Climate Change 
Plan for Central Hudson to move forward with its response to how the world is changing is a cost-
effective, efficient and in the long term reliable response to a climate catastrophe that needs dramatic 
responses to be curtailed.
Respectfully submitted,
Vernon Benjamin

A. 
L2 chargers operate at between 208 and 240 volts, making them at least two times faster 
1 
than Level 1 charging, which is where power is drawn from a standard 120-volt wall 
2 
outlet.  L2 charging speeds vary depending on the size of the EV’s battery and the 
3 
vehicle’s maximum charge rate, ranging from as little as 12 miles per hour charging to 80 
4 
miles per hour charging.1   
5 
Q. 
Do you support the Companies’ proposed municipal curbside charging pilot? 
6 
A. 
Yes.  L2 chargers are widely considered the most common type of charger level used for 
7 
daily charging.  L2 chargers are versatile – they can be installed in homes, at workplaces, 
8 
and in public locations – and are generally far more affordable because power is provided 
9 
at a lower level over an extended period of time.  For these reasons, the availability of L2 
10 
chargers is very important for spurring confidence in adopting an EV, especially when 
11 
home or residential charging is not an option.  In its 2020 order establishing the Make-
12 
Ready Program, the Commission cited to New York State Department of Public Service 
13 
Staff’s assumption that 57 percent of EV drivers in the New York City (“NYC”) metro 
14 
area and 82 percent in rest of state will charge or will have the capability of charging at 
15 
home.  Based on this assumption, especially in the NYC metro area, L2 chargers will be 
16 
critical to ensure that drivers who do not have the capability to charge at home can drive 
17 
an EV with confidence.

Q. 
Do you have any recommended modifications to the proposed municipal curbside 
1 
charging pilot?

A. 
I have no recommended modifications, but a few points for consideration.  If approved, 
3 
the Companies should make sure that at least some of the L2s deployed as part of the

will be able to incorporate vehicle charging into their daily routine.

12 
in publicly accessible municipal-owned parking lots if adequate curbside charging cannot 
13 
be found.  This will still permit convenient public access, as envisioned by the municipal 
14 
curbside charging pilot.

Q.  
Can you elaborate on why make-ready costs pose a barrier for deployment of EV 
3

A. 
Electrification of the public transit segment of the MHD EV sector will result in public 
1 
health and overall operational cost benefits.  The replacement of diesel transit buses with 
2 
electric buses will provide many public health benefits to customers in the NYSEG and

4

13 
York City, p. 16.)  This will be very significant for public transit MHD EV fleets, as 
14 
these fleets often face constrained budgets and the inability to fully recoup investment 
15 
costs through fares and other charges to the public.

A.  
Utility investment in make-ready infrastructure can be justified by reference to the 
18 
established principle of line extension.  It is customary for utilities to cover the costs for 
19 
extending service lines to provide power supply up to designated distances or dollar 
20 
amounts.  Therefore, prudent investment in additional requisite electrical equipment up to

the point of load as proposed by the Company is justified to achieve important State

3

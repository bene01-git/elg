A. 
Background 
11 
Q. 
Please provide a few brief introductory comments concerning ECOS studies. 
12 
A. 
An ECOS study is often used as a tool in utility rate proceedings to apportion rate 
13 
base and operating expenses among the various customer classes. This data is 
14 
used in conjunction with existing revenues to estimate the overall system Rate of 
15 
Return (ROR) and the ROR earned from each service classification as defined in 
16 
the study. Sometimes, the focus is on historical revenues; other times forecasted 
17 
revenue increases for the Rate Year (pro-forma) are used to estimate the overall 
18 
system ROR (similar to what the Companies proposed in these cases). Regardless 
19 
of the specific approach that is used, the analyst must exercise discretion and 
20 
judgment in apportioning costs. Some of these judgments are discussed in the 
21 


--- Page 11 ---

UTILITY INTERVENTION UNIT

9 
 
National Association of Regulatory Utility Commissioners Gas Distribution Rate 
1 
Design Manual, published in June 1989 (NARUC Gas Manual). The NARUC Gas 
2 
Manual, on page 20, points out that “[t]here is no scientifically correct method of 
3 
making necessary allocations” and that a “certain amount of judgement must be 
4 
used in any cost of service study.” The NARUC Gas Manual offers a starting point 
5 
to develop ECOS studies and highlights areas of concern. Some of these 
6 
judgments have been the subject of much debate and controversy throughout the 
7 
last 40 years or more. 
8 
The end result of an ECOS study is to inform the overall system ROR and 
9 
individual service classification RORs. The ECOS study results are particularly 
10 
significant because of their potential impact on revenue allocation—how the rate 
11 
increases are assigned to the various customer classes. The ECOS results, or 
12 
output, are sometimes also used to support aspects of the rate design – particularly 
13 
the monthly customer charge – within individual SCs.   
14

Q. 
You mentioned that some of the judgments used within ECOS studies have been 
13 
controversial. Can you elaborate on this point with some examples? 
14 
A. 
Yes. One particularly controversial judgment has been the decision regarding the 
15 
assignment of customer costs—particularly whether costs associated with the 
16 
distribution system should be included as customer costs. For gas, the general 
17 
dispute is over the classification and allocation of distribution mains assigned to 
18 
FERC Account 376. Over the years, there have been differences in opinion among 
19 
intervenors and utilities on the optimal methodology to use.   
20 
Customer-related costs are assumed to be costs directly affected by the 
21 
number of customers served, irrespective of usage and demand. Examples of

costs as customer-related using some variation of a minimum system or a

Q. 
Can you briefly explain the revenue allocation process in a typical utility rate case? 
15 
A. 
Yes. The results of one or more ECOS studies guide decisions regarding how 
16 
proposed rate changes should be allocated across various service classifications.  
17 
The specific process can vary, but ECOS results are generally examined to see 
18 
whether certain classes have been identified as having a high ROR, which means 
19 
that service class has paid more than the costs they have been allocated, 
20 
sometimes referred to as “overearning.” If a high ROR is identified, an effort is 
21 


--- Page 22 ---
CASE 23-G-0225 and 23-G-0226 
 
UTILITY INTERVENTION UNIT 
 
DIRECT TESTIMONY OF DANIELLE PANKO    
20 
 
typically made to assign a somewhat smaller share of the rate increase or revenue 
1 
requirement to that service classification, thereby moving in the direction of greater 
2 
uniformity in the service classification RORs. Similarly, if certain classes are 
3 
identified as having a low ROR—meaning that the service class has paid less than 
4 
the costs that have been allocated to them, sometimes referred to as “overearning,” 
5 
a somewhat greater than average share of the rate increase or revenue burden 
6 
may be assigned to that class during the revenue allocation step. 
7 
In carrying out the revenue allocation process, tolerance bands are often 
8 
used around the system average ROR to decide whether the service classification 
9 
ROR deviates far enough from the average ROR to merit special treatment. The 
10 
width of the tolerance band (e.g., +-10%, 20%, 30%, etc.) can vary widely, but the 
11 
effect is generally the same—to ameliorate disputes concerning the ECOS results, 
12 
since service classifications that fall within the tolerance band generally receive 
13 
the same percentage change. An analyst must exercise judgment when 
14 
determining the size of the tolerance band, and this is sometimes a topic of dispute.  
15 
The tolerance band can be important because it helps determine which service 
16 
classifications will receive revenue changes that are less than, or more than, the 
17 
other service classifications. Finally, some restrictions may be imposed to exercise 
18 
further judgment in the outcome, such as to avoid extreme bill impacts or prevent 
19 
classes to receive revenue decreases (e.g., no class is given a decrease, no class 
20 
is given more than 150% of the average increase). Over the years in various

Basic Depreciation Terms and Concepts 
16 
Q. 
Please explain the terms “net salvage” and 
17 
“depreciation” as used in ratemaking. 
18 
A. 
Net salvage is any cash received for the asset, 
19 
less the cost to remove it.  Depreciation is a 
20 
method of recovering capital costs, less net 
21 
salvage, related to plant in service over the 
22 
expected life of the plant.   
23 
Q. 
Please define “average service life.” 
24 


--- Page 9 ---
Cases 23-E-0418 & 23-G-0419 
 
Mingdi Huang 
 
 
8 
 
A. 
ASL is the arithmetic average of the lives of 
1 
the units of property, represented by the area 
2 
under the survivor curve from age zero to the 
3 
maximum life, divided by 100 percent. 
4 
Q. 
What is a “survivor curve?” 
5 
A. 
A survivor curve is a graphical indication of 
6 
the percentage of original plant, by age, that 
7 
is still in service.  Known curves, such as Iowa 
8 
or h-curves, are fit to the observed curve to 
9 
help determine an appropriate ASL.  These known 
10 
curves include a mode, which describes the 
11 
relative height of the curve.  Low mode curves 
12 
have wide retirement patterns.  In other words, 
13 
the assets retire over a wide range of lives and 
14 
less retirements occur near the average service 
15 
life.  Curves with higher modes have much 
16 
narrower retirement patterns, with more 
17 
retirements occurring near the ASL.  The area 
18 
under the survivor curve is the ASL or whole 
19 
life.  The observed curves are generated from a 
20 
mortality study that examines the actual 
21 
retirement patterns over multiple years. 
22 
Q. 
Please define “depreciation rate.” 
23 
A. 
A depreciation rate is a percentage rate that is 
24 


--- Page 10 ---

Mingdi Huang 
 
 
9 
 
applied to the value of gross plant in service, 
1 
by account, in order to determine the annual 
2 
depreciation expense.  Depreciation rates are 
3 
made up of various independent variables 
4 
referred to as depreciation factors, such as 
5 
ASL, survivor curve and the net salvage factor.  
6 
Q. 
Please define “depreciation expense.” 
7 
A. 
Depreciation expense, in any given year, is the 
8 
product of gross plant in service multiplied by 
9 
the applicable depreciation rate.  Depreciation 
10 
expense allows for the recovery of an 
11 
investment, less net salvage, over the expected 
12 
life of the investment. 
13 
Q. 
Please define “net salvage factor.” 
14 
A. 
Net salvage factor refers to the component of 
15 
the depreciation rate that allows recovery of 
16 
the estimated net salvage.  It is expressed as a 
17 
percentage, based on the original cost of plant.  
18 
For example, let us assume that a unit of 
19 
property has a total installed cost of $1,000, 
20 
$0 estimated future salvage, and estimated costs 
21 
of removal of $500.  The net salvage factor is 
22 
calculated as follows: Rate = ($0 + (-$500)) / 
23 
$1,000.  This equates to a -0.50, or -50 percent 
24

10 
 
net salvage factor.  
1 
Q. 
Please define “book reserve.” 
2 
A. 
The book reserve is the continued accumulation 
3 
of the annual depreciation expense accruals, 
4 
less any retirements and cost of removal, plus 
5 
any salvage received.  The book reserve is 
6 
calculated by plant account and subtracted from 
7 
the gross plant, or original cost of the plant, 
8 
to calculate the net plant, or the remaining 
9 
plant balance that is not yet depreciated. 
10 
Q. 
Please define “theoretical reserve.” 
11 
A. 
The theoretical reserve is the amount of 
12 
depreciation expense that should have been 
13 
collected as of a particular date, given the 
14 
survivor curves, ASLs, and net salvage factors 
15 
used to determine the proposed depreciation 
16 
rates. 
17

A. 
Depreciation rates are calculated differently

depending on the depreciation system used.  Each 
20 
system is composed of a method, a procedure, and 
21 
a technique.

Q. 
Please explain the straight-line method. 
7 
A. 
The straight-line method recovers the cost of an 
8 
asset equally over its estimated useful life.  
9 
In other words, the amount recovered annually 
10 
through depreciation would not change over the 
11 
asset’s life. 
12 
Q. 
Please explain the ASL procedure. 
13 
A. 
In the ASL procedure, the depreciation rate is 
14 
based on the average life of the group and 
15 
applied to the remaining unrecovered balance.  
16 
This means that the cost of plant retired prior 
17 
to the average life is not fully recovered at 
18 
the time of retirement, while the cost of plant 
19 
retired after the average life is more than 
20 
fully recovered.  The costs not recovered before 
21 
the average life will be balanced out by the 
22 
costs recovered after the average life. 
23 
Q. 
Please explain how the depreciation rate is 
24

12 
 
calculated following the whole life technique. 
1 
A. 
Under the whole life technique, the depreciation 
2 
rate is calculated using the following formula: 
3 
 
Rate = (1-Net Salvage)/(ASL).  For example, if 
4 
the ASL for a plant account was 50 years and the 
5 
net salvage factor was negative 50 percent, then 
6 
the depreciation rate would be: R = (1-(-0.5)) / 
7 
50.  In such a case, the depreciation rate would 
8 
equal 0.03, or 3.0 percent.  The rate is then 
9 
multiplied by the gross plant to obtain the 
10 
depreciation expense for ratemaking purposes.

Q. 
Please explain the process of comparing the book 
3 
reserve to the theoretical reserve. 
4 
A. 
When a depreciation study is performed, the 
5 
current book reserve is compared to the proposed 
6 
theoretical reserve.  The proposed theoretical 
7 
reserve incorporates any proposed changes to the 
8 
survivor curves, ASLs, and net salvage factors.  
9 
Typically, if the difference between the book 
10 
reserve and the theoretical reserve is within a 
11 
margin of 10 percent, above or below, no 
12 
adjustment is made to amortize the over- or 
13 
under-accruals.  However, if the difference is 
14 
greater than 10 percent, an adjustment can be 
15 
made to amortize some or all of the difference 
16 
between the theoretical and book reserves.  If 
17 
the book reserve is larger than the theoretical 
18 
reserve, some or all of the difference can be 
19 
used for other ratemaking treatments, rather 
20 
than a reduction to depreciation expense.  The 
21 
use of a 10 percent margin and the rate 
22 
treatment are discretionary.  An adjustment is 
23 
often made because the difference is too large

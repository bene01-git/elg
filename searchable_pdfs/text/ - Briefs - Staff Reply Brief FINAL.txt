STAFF REPLY BRIEF 
 
I. 
INTRODUCTION 
 
 
In this Reply Brief, Department of Public Service Staff (Staff) responds to the 
arguments made by the parties in their respective Initial Briefs.  On April 6, 2020, ten parties to 
these proceedings filed Initial Briefs.1  In its own Initial Brief, Staff has already addressed many 
of the arguments made by parties in their respective Initial Briefs.  To that extent, Staff will not 
repeat those same arguments in this Reply Brief.2 
 
 
In their Initial Brief, the Companies seek revenue requirements far in excess of 
the amount necessary for them to provide safe, adequate, and reliable service during the Rate 
Year.  The rates proposed by the Companies are not just and reasonable.  At every turn, the 
Companies portray Staff’s recommendations as draconian.  However, a review of the record 
demonstrates that Staff’s recommendations provide reasonable revenues that will allow the 
Companies to provide safe, adequate, and reliable service during the Rate Year without unduly 
burdening customers. 
 
 
While many of the Companies arguments should be summarily rejected outright, 
a few overarching arguments merit discussion here.  First, with regard to myriad issues, the

2. Contractors 
a. Snow Removal Costs (KEDNY) 
 
 
Staff recommends using a three-year average of snow removal costs, plus 
inflation, to forecast KEDNY’s snow removal costs for the Rate Year.  Instead, KEDNY 
proposes to use only the Historic Test Year snow removal costs plus inflation.  Staff’s 
recommendation lowers KEDNY’s revenue requirement by $1.263 million.30 
 
 
In addition to the arguments Staff already addressed in its Initial Brief, the 
Companies’ argue that use of Historic Test Year alone to forecast Rate Year snow removal costs 
treats customers and KEDNY fairly over time.31  KEDNY reiterates that in the 2016 Rate 
Order,32 the rate allowance for snow removal expense was approximately $0.689 in each of the 
three rate years, while actual expenses in calendar years 2017 and 2018 (rate years one and two 
of that rate plan) were $1.8 million and $3.337 million, respectively, and that snow removal 
expense for calendar year 2019 (rate year three), as of September 2019, was $2.115 million.33  
Therefore, KEDNY claims that it did not fully recover its actual snow removal costs at any time 
during the rate plan adopted in the 2016 Rate Order.  KEDNY claims that utilizing Staff’s 
proposed three-year average in this case is inconsistent with the historical forecasting 
methodology and would continue the inequitable result of including a rate allowance that 
prohibits KEDNY from fully recovering its costs of this expense.

XIII. MANAGEMENT AUDIT COMPLIANCE 
 
 
PULP seeks to bring in information from an ongoing comprehensive management 
audit of the National Grid utilities (KEDNY, KEDLI, and Niagara Mohawk) into these rate 
proceedings.342  PULP’s proposal is without basis in the PSL, and reflects a misunderstanding of 
the interplay between management audits and the role of outside entities in management audits.  
Accordingly, PULP’s proposal should be rejected. 
 
 
PULP states:  “Historically, the scope, procedures and findings of utility 
management and operations audits have direct implications for the issues addressed in rate cases.”343  
With regard to the interplay between management audits and rate proceedings, PSL §66(19)(c) 
states in full: 
Upon the application of a gas or electric corporation for a major change in rates as 
defined in subdivision twelve of this section, the commission shall review that 
corporation's compliance with the directions and recommendations made 
previously by the commission, as a result of the most recently completed 
management and operations audit. The commission shall incorporate the findings 
of such review in its opinion or order, and such findings shall be enforceable by the 
commission. 
Thus, the utilities compliance with the results of prior audits is reviewed in rate proceedings.  
However, PULP seeks to discuss a currently ongoing audit in these rate proceedings.  The PSL 
does not provide for that.  Indeed, it would be, at best, an inefficient outcome, as both the audit 
and the rate proceedings are currently progressing on their own separate tracks which cannot, 
and should not, be meshed together.  PULP has cited not a single example of where the materials

of an ongoing management audit, i.e., one for which a final report has not yet been issued, have 
been substantively addressed in a rate proceeding. 
 
 
Further, PULP seems to misunderstand the role interested entities have in audits, 
when it requests that the “communications and information exchanged in Case 18-M-0195 
through April 6, 2020 … be made available to the parties and ALJs in these proceedings.”  A 
management audit is an “investigation” which the Commission has “discretion to have such 
audits performed by its staff, or by independent auditors.”  The PSL does not provide for 
interested third parties to participate in the performance of the audit/investigation.  Interested 
third parties may provide comments at any time in an audit, and are invited to do so by the 
Commission once the Audit’s report has been issued.  However PULP’s desire to intrude into an 
ongoing audit and investigation is not appropriate. 
 
 
Moreover, whatever its merits, PULP has used its Initial Brief to make an 
extensively broad discovery request.  PULP could have made such a request at any time earlier 
in these proceedings, but it chose not to do so.  While Staff likely would have objected to such a 
discovery request, there is a process for addressing discovery requests and objections.  That 
process is not the briefing stage of these rate proceedings. 
 
 
For the reasons stated above, the Commission should reject PULP’s proposal to 
inject the “communications and information exchanged” in the ongoing management audit into 
these rate proceedings.

III. 
ISSUES PRESENTED IN INITIAL TESTIMONIES 
10 
Q: Does a reduced role for combustion fuels eliminate the need for RNG? 
11 
A: No. RNG is an important near-term decarbonization strategy for applications which currently 
12 
utilize fossil-derived natural gas and, in the long-term, will be necessary in applications with 
13 
certain reliability requirements3 or which are not suitable for electrification.4 This concept is 
14 
substantiated by studies conducted by Energy and Environmental Economics (E3) for a number 
15 
of jurisdictions—including New York5 and California6—in which RNG is shown to be a 
16 
necessary decarbonization strategy, even in high-electrification scenarios. Furthermore, E3’s 
17 
high-electrification scenarios consistently show significant demand for fossil-derived natural gas 
18 
remaining through 2050,7 which should be decarbonized using renewable gaseous fuels wherever 
19 
possible pursuant to Climate Leadership and Community Protection Act (CLCPA) goals.8 
20 
 
21 
The aforementioned E3 studies were cited by multiple parties in this docket arguing against the 
22 
Company’s proposal. Notably, multiple parties use these studies to justify their assertion that, 
23 
due to reductions in the gas system necessary to achieve carbon-neutrality by 2050, RNG should 
24 
not be incentivized.9 This train of thought relates to similar assertions that RNG will not facilitate 
25 
adequate GHG reductions pursuant to reaching New York’s decarbonization goals, and that

Q: Does RNG use preclude the implementation of other energy decarbonization strategies? 
17 
A: No. As previously discussed, there is a clear role for RNG as a compliment to other strategies 
18 
in decarbonizing New York’s energy system. Importantly, pipeline-injected renewable gases can 
19 
be transferred between various end-uses over time based on the projected long-term need for 
20 
renewable gaseous fuels in various sectors. This means that incentivizing the use of RNG and 
21 
renewable hydrogen as decarbonization strategies for natural gas use in the building sector does 
22 
not stand in the way of future efforts to electrify the same end-uses. Furthermore, as previously 
23 
discussed, work conducted by E3 shows long-term RNG (and fossil-derived natural gas) use in 
24 
the building sector. Therefore, it is unquestionably prudent to incentivize RNG and renewable 
25 
hydrogen production in the near-term. 
26 
 
27 
Multiple parties argue that RNG performance should be assessed against all other energy 
28 
decarbonization strategies, not just fossil-derived natural gas, before it is incentivized.11  
29 
Similarly, other parties suggest that a hierarchy should be employed which prioritizes other 
30 
decarbonization strategies such as energy efficiency and electrification over RNG.12 We believe 
31 
such analysis will show that RNG can be a cost-effective strategy when compared on a $/metric 
32 
ton basis to other options. Therefore, in the near-term, RNG should be generally incentivized for 
33 
use in any natural gas application, including those which may ultimately be electrified because 
34 
there remains such a large demand for conventional gas and the RNG industry is still nascent. In 
35 
the long-term, RNG should be assessed against natural gas and other options in applications 
36 
which are not suitable for electrification.

Q: Does the industry support a comprehensive assessment of GHG emissions from RNG? 
20 
A: Yes. The RNG industry has long advocated for employing metrics to assess the GHG 
21 
emissions from each RNG production pathway. In particular, we believe that a lifecycle analysis 
22 
(LCA) is the most appropriate method of doing so because it accounts for all emissions benefits 
23 
and disbenefits17 from a given RNG production pathway. These various emissions categories are 
24 
then combined to produce a carbon intensity score (CI) for each production pathway. The most 
25 
common method of calculating RNG CI scores uses a version18 of the GREET model19 created 
26 
by Argonne National Lab, which is widely accepted among regulatory agencies and the scientific 
27 
community.

Based on this data, any type of RNG currently available for purchase in New York State (even 
8 
those which are low-carbon as opposed to carbon-neutral or carbon-negative) will provide 
9 
emission reduction benefits to National Grid’s customers. Furthermore, this data stands in direct 
10 
contradiction of the unsubstantiated claim that leakage from RNG projects will result in a higher 
11 
CI than conventional natural gas.21 While it would be technically possible to produce RNG with 
12 
a higher CI than conventional natural gas for a variety of reasons—due to leakage, energy 
13 
consumption,22 or other factors—this is not the current practical reality at real-world RNG 
14 
facilities in the U.S. today.23 Here it is also worth considering the extent to which electricity-
15 
based applications may also carry a high CI score depending on similar inputs.24 
16 
 
17 
In a discussion of current RNG CI scores it is also important to consider the inputs which may 
18 
change over time. For example, as New York’s electricity grid sees an increased amount of zero-
19 
carbon electricity generation, the CI for all RNG pathways which utilize grid electricity will 
20 
decrease. This level of transparency provides incentive for RNG producers to reduce their CI as 
21 
much as possible.

23

24 
would not be a viable alternative, unless system leakage rates are lower than in the flaring 
25 
counterfactual, and unless preventing methane formation is not a viable alternative25 is 
26 
problematic. This argument compares RNG to a first-best future world where zero GHG energy 
27 
and waste disposal options are ubiquitous, rather than to the more appropriate real-world 
28

29 
handling practices).

34

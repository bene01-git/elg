Refer to the Company’s Customer Operations Panel – Customer Relationship Management 
(CRM) system. 
1.     Describe the Company’s current method of managing customer relationships including

2.     Exhibit___CO-10, page 1, states that the future Customer Service System (CSS), will not be

following information: 
a.     A list of the customer interactions that the new CSS will capture, and the incremental 
interactions Company seeks to capture with the proposed CRM. 
b.     Has the Company developed a method to measure and or rank which interactions contain 
information frequently needed by Customer Service Representatives (CSR)?  If so, provide 
details.  If not, why not. 
c.     Provide a detailed description of CRM functionalities that overlap with the new CSS and the 
incremental functionalities the CRM will provide.  
d.     Does Oracle offer any CRM functionalities, add-ons or upgrades that can be implemented to 
the CSS? 
3.     Has the company completed any research on the cost of building an in-house solution? If so, 
provide results. If not, explain why it has not been considered as an option by the Company. 
4.     Provide the expected go-live date for the CRM?  
5.     In reference to page 31 of the Panel’s testimony, the Company states that when the CRM is 
fully developed, it will integrate with other Company systems.  Provide the following: 
a.     Is the initial go-live of the CRM for a partially developed system?  Provide details. 
b.     The timeframe for the fully developed CRM system and estimated annual capital and O&M 
cost to implement the fully developed CRM system. 
c.     The estimated ongoing capital and O&M cost after the fully developed CRM system has 
been implemented. 
 
 
Response 
 
1. The Company currently does not have a single platform or method for managing its 
customer relationships. Besides our current Customer Information System, soon to be 
replaced by Oracle’s Customer Care and Billing (CC&B) system, we rely on a variety of

disconnected homegrown and vendor-based platforms, tools and applications to manage 
our customer relationships. These include, but are not limited to: 
  
 Vendor Message Broadcast, for billing and payment updates and outage 
messaging;  
 The Work Information Notice System (WINS), for street closures messaging;  
 Vendor Possible Now, for digital customer communications preferences; 
 Vendor Pega, for field crews repair and mitigation appointments;  
 Customer Project Management System (CPMS), for Energy Services

Due to the decentralized nature of this ecosystem of platforms, and because not all the

possible to calculate the requested estimate of associated capital and O&M costs.

 Participation in special rate programs like Value Stack

providers in the field of CRMs, and we would expect them to be included in our Request 
for Proposal.

support, maintenance, and ongoing upgrades with new functionalities.

3. At page 87 of your testimony, you write, “While the Company’s current policy is a good 
step forward, it should be expanded to include days where the temperature hits 85 
degrees (including not terminating 1 calendar day before and 2 calendar days after 85-
degree days).”  
  
Please explain the factual basis for setting the temperature threshold for service terminations 
specifically at 85 degrees and provide any workpapers, analysis, or supporting documents in 
support of this conclusion. 
 
PULP’s Response: 
 
The effect of urban heat islands1 on temperatures in NYC and Westchester, as well as the 
measured and measurable increase in frequency of high temperatures/humidity levels, provides a 
reasonable rationale for reviewing the Company’s existing temperature threshold for service 
terminations. The effect of urban heat islands occurs due to the absorption and trapping of heat 
by asphalt and concrete, causing parts of NYC and Westchester to hit higher temperatures than 
more rural parts of NY State and other parts of the Company’s service territory with greater 
parkland, trees and vegetation, and less blacktop, among other factors increasing heat 
absorption.2 Heat index values, or the “feels like” temperatures attributed to relative humidity, 
can be increased by up to 15 degrees when factoring in exposure to full sunlight. 3 
 
1 See, https://www.epa.gov/heatislands; and see, https://www.dec.ny.gov/lands/30344.html. 
2 Id. “Structures such as buildings, roads, and other infrastructure absorb and re-emit the sun’s heat more than 
natural landscapes such as forests and water bodies. Urban areas, where these structures are highly concentrated and 
greenery is limited, become “islands” of higher temperatures relative to outlying areas. Daytime temperatures in 
urban areas are about 1–7°F higher than temperatures in outlying areas and nighttime temperatures are about 2-5°F 
higher.” 
3 See, https://www.weather.gov/arx/heat_index.

In order to quantify the heat island effects in New York City and whether such effects may have 
disparate impact on low-income neighborhoods and BIPOC neighborhoods,4 Columbia 
University has an ongoing research program titled “Addressing the Urban Heat Island through an 
Equity Lens: A Citizen Science Project.”5 The research is following a “citizen science approach” 
where residents are helping collect temperature related data. The heat disparities can be so 
extreme that it is easily visible through GIS mapping.6 
 
Based on Columbia’s and other research, PULP argues that because of urban heat islands in 
NYC, it is reasonable that temperatures between 85-93 degrees should be considered for a new 
policy threshold for not terminating electric service. In addition to the heat island effects alone, 
there are also the growing effects of climate change, which results in higher heat and factors 
including but not limited to, humidity making heat emergencies both more extreme, and 
particularly problematic for heat island zones.7  
 
As noted above briefly, due to climate change it’s also expected that there will be more frequent 
higher degree temperatures and that these temperatures will occur sooner in the spring/summer 
seasons than traditionally experienced.8 In the month of May 2021, NYC residents experienced 
(6) days where temperatures ranged from 84-93 degrees, and (4) in the month of May 2022.9 
High heat and humidity have the potential to have very dangerous health effects on people, for 
instance exasperating asthma,10 and causing heat stress. In particular, BIPOC communities face 
disproportional health risks “due to patterns of racial discrimination and segregation, 
concentrated poverty, and public and private disinvestment.”11 It is reasonable therefore to plan 
policies to avert such outcomes.12 
 
As described above, between rising temperatures/humidity, and urban heat islands, PULP 
therefore finds it reasonable for the Company re-evaluate its threshold for service terminations.

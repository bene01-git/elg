We write this reply comment after another week of wildfire smoke created hazardous air in New 
York State and amid the hottest days the planet has recorded in human history.1 These are 
reminders that the issues we raise in response to this Joint Proposal need to be addressed with the 
urgency demanded by the times in which we live. As such, we must emphasize to the 
Department of Public Service Staff (“Staff”), all signatory parties, and the Public Service 
Commission (“Commission”) that another three years delaying the application of the 2019 
Climate Leadership and Community Protection Act (“CLCPA”) mandates to investments made 
by NYSEG/RGE (“the Companies”) is unacceptable.  
 
 
1. Statements of Support by Both Staff and Companies Ignore a Key Requirement for 
Compliance on CLCPA 7(3) Regarding Disadvantaged Communities.  
 
When arguing that the Joint Proposal is compliant with CLCPA § 7(3), both Staff and the 
Companies selectively quote from the law. Both argue that the Joint Proposal complies with the 
law because it does not “disproportionately burden disadvantaged communities.” While the 
record supporting even this assertion is non-existent, it avoids the second part of CLCPA § 7(3) 
that states: “All state agencies, offices, authorities, and divisions shall also prioritize reductions 
of greenhouse gas emissions and co-pollutants in disadvantaged communities as identified 
pursuant to such subdivision 5 of section 75-0101 of the environmental conservation law.” 
 
When asked through information requests for which reductions of GHG emissions or pollutants 
reductions would benefit disadvantaged communities, Companies and Staff were unable to 
answer with any specificity, evidence, or analysis to demonstrate that disadvantaged 
communities (“DACs”) have been prioritized. 
 
This inadequacy is demonstrated in responses B and C of AGREE-40 (attached as exhibit A), 
directed at Companies and Staff. 
 
The Companies and Staff were asked:

b. What analysis did the signatory parties conduct on the increases or reductions to the

The Companies and Staff replied jointly:

Providing reliable service for all customers in a non-discriminatory way is an obligation the

Companies and Staff response to this information request, like their statements of support, only 
claim that the capital investments and programs will “not disproportionately burden 
disadvantaged communities.”  
 
The Companies and Staff have not quantified the outages or other markers of reliability and 
safety in regard to disadvantaged communities, so their claim on disproportionate burden has no 
evidentiary basis. Furthermore, the Companies and Staff have not quantified emissions 
reductions supposedly attributable to any of the elements of the Joint Proposal they claim will 
reduce emissions, and more specifically have not quantified those emissions impacts in DACs. 
Therefore, they cannot assess whether or not emissions and pollution reductions in 
disadvantaged communities have been prioritized.

The response that investments in the Clean Energy Transformation category “will create benefits 
for all New Yorkers” is plainly counter to the CLCPA, which mandates that state agencies, 
including through the approval of Joint Proposals as the Public Service Commission (PSC) has 
ordered, must “prioritize reductions of greenhouse gas emissions and co-pollutants in 
disadvantaged communities.” The Companies’ have not quantified the investments in DACs and 
thus cannot ensure that their investments are non-discriminatory and that they prioritize 
disadvantaged communities.  
 
We further take issue with the Companies’ and Staff’s objection that following the law on 
disadvantaged communities is “overly burdensome.”  The lack of analysis and specificity on 
these matters in the Joint Proposal shows exactly the type of disregard toward disadvantaged 
communities that the CLCPA § 7(3) was meant to ameliorate.  
 
The PSC has previously ordered that the CLCPA § 7(2) and CLCPA § 7(3) apply to rate cases.2 
As such, the PSC must reject the Joint Proposal for not complying with the CLCPA § 7(3).

2. The PSC Must Not Allow Three More Years of Non-Compliance with the CLCPA’s 
Emissions Mandates 
 
The statements of support for each of the Company and Staff claim that the Joint Proposal is 
consistent with the CLCPA § 7(2). These positions are based on out-of-date PSC orders, and they 
are at odds with Staff’s testimonial position on the Companies’ filing, the evidence, and the law.  
 
The Companies’ claim that the Joint Proposal is consistent with the CLCPA § 7(2) is based on a 
selective reading of the August 2021 KEDNY/KEDLI Order Approving Joint Proposal.3 With 
this 2021 Order as their evidence, the Companies argue that being “directionally consistent with 
the State’s emissions reduction goals” is equivalent to following the GHG emissions mandates of 
the CLCPA § 7(2).  
 
What the Companies and Staff fail to note is that the 2021 KEDNY/KEDLI Order is plainly 
written for its time. In the Order, the PSC makes note on several occasions that the CLCPA was 
passed during the proceeding.4 Similarly, the Companies in that case argued that the law’s recent 
passage means “implementation of the CLCPA is premature.”5 DPS further noted that the record 
for those proceedings were based “largely upon testimony and evidence filed prior to the 
CLCPA’s January 1, 2020 effective date” in arguing their support for the Joint Proposal. 
 
In finding that the 2019 KEDNY/KEDLI Joint Proposal was consistent with the CLCPA, the 
Commission wrote:  
 
We start our evaluation by noting that our application of Sections 7(2) and 7(3) to these 
rate proceedings is undertaken well prior to the full implementation of the CLCPA. The 
Companies’ April 2019 tariff filings pre-dated by three months the CLCPA’s passage and

Later in the Order, the Commission noted that it was only on December 30, 2020 that the 2030

The allowance of a minimum, “directional” compliance with the CLCPA in the KEDNY/KEDLI 
order was clearly tied to the law’s then-recent passage and the fact that the proceeding pre-dated 
that passage.  
 
Thus, in July 2023, we cannot have the same standard for compliance with the CLCPA as the 
PSC did when reviewing a rate case that began in 2019. Since the beginning of the 
KEDNY/KEDLI case in 2019, the path toward CLCPA compliance has been clarified and 
implementation of it is no longer premature. Likewise, the standard for compliance in rate cases 
must evolve.  
 
Since the KEDNY/KEDLI Order was written, the Climate Action Council’s draft Scoping Plan 
was released in December 2021 and the Final Scoping Plan was released in December 2022. The 
draft Scoping Plan included an “integration analysis” detailing sector by sector analysis of the 
greenhouse gas emissions of New York State and demonstrated the various pathways to the 
mandated emissions reductions. Even before the Scoping Plan was finalized, the direction for 
CLCPA compliance from utilities (major acceleration of energy efficiency and electrification 
and deep reductions in gas deliveries) was obvious from the integration analysis and the 
scenarios presented in the Draft.

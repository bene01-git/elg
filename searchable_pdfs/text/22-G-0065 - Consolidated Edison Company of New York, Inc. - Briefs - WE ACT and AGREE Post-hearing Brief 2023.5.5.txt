covered by the evidentiary hearing, and to responding to CECONY’s CLCPA Panel Rebuttal

A. Background 
On January 28, 2022, CECONY filed proposed changes to its electric and gas tariff 
schedules and proposed to increase its annual electric and gas delivery revenues.10 The direct 
testimony of CECONY’s CLCPA Panel described various investments and projects for the 
Commission’s consideration when assessing whether to approve the company’s proposed rate 
request.11 The CLCPA Panel asserted that “the Company’s rate filings are consistent with and 
otherwise comply with the requirements of the CLCPA applicable to utility rate cases.”12 Also, 
the Panel only described the CLCPA and Commission Orders implementing the statute in very 
broad terms.13 However, CECONY’s Rebuttal Testimony of its CLCPA Panel included legal 
conclusions in the form of novel interpretations of the CLCPA and selectively quoted and 
synthesized legal authorities over the course of close to a dozen pages.14 After creating the new 
legal standard, the Panel applied that standard to the facts of CECONY’s rate filing.15 On July 
15, 2022, the Joint Parties submitted a Motion to Strike improper legal arguments from 
CECONY’s CLCPA Rebuttal Testimony. CECONY filed a response to the Motion to Strike on 
July 25, 2022. Joint Parties renewed the Motion to Strike at the evidentiary hearing and objected 
to the admission of the CLCPA Panel Rebuttal Testimony into the record.16 The objection was 
overruled, and the renewed motion denied.17 WE ACT and AGREE urge the Commission to 
sustain the objection and grant the Motion to Strike.  
B. The Commission Must Grant the Motion to Strike. 
 
As the Joint Parties explain in their Motion to Strike, the extensive legal briefing 
contained in CECONY’s CLCPA Rebuttal Testimony runs afoul of the rule that expert testimony 
on issues of law is generally inadmissible.18 The Rebuttal Testimony of CECONY’s CLCPA 
Panel also violates the evidentiary principle prohibiting the introduction of unreliable evidence

i. 
Granting the Motion to Strike would promote fairness and New York State’s 
climate objectives without any prejudice to CECONY. 
 
Striking the portions of the CLCPA Rebuttal Testimony at issue promotes fairness and 
would in no way prejudice CECONY because the legal framework and analysis laid out in the 
CLCPA Rebuttal Testimony is substantially similar to the Company’s legal briefing filed on 
April 5, 2023.21 Should the Commission allow CECONY to ignore the evidentiary rules, 
intervening parties, especially smaller, less resourced stakeholders, may believe that utilities will 
receive preferential treatment in rate proceedings. This perception of bias could dissuade 
stakeholders with important insights from partaking in rate proceedings at all. Allowing 
CECONY to file legal conclusions within pre-filed expert testimony, while most other 
intervening parties refrained from such actions, may create a perception of inequity. By striking 
portions of the CLCPA Rebuttal Testimony from the record, the Commission ensures equity, 
prevents a repetition of these violations in future proceedings, and ensures that the public is not 
misled about the scope and meaning of New York’s landmark climate law.  
ii. 
The evidentiary hearing ruling denying the Motion to Strike is inconsistent with the 
rules of evidence and precedential decisions before the Commission. 
 
Judge Moreno erred by admitting the entirety of CECONY’s CLCPA Rebuttal Testimony 
into the record as factual testimony. ALJ Moreno essentially argued that allowing the CLCPA

III. 
THE JOINT PARTIES’ PROPOSAL TO REMEDY DISPROPORTIONATE 
BURDENS ON DISADVANTAGED COMMUNITIES SHOULD BE ADOPTED; 
SUCH A REMEDY WILL NOT INTERFERE WITH ANY GENERIC 
PROCEEDINGS. 
In its Statement of Opposition to the Joint Proposal, the Joint Parties explained why the 
Commission should modify the Joint Proposal “to ensure that the Disadvantaged Communities 
report [included in the Joint Proposal] is actually and expeditiously used to relieve 
disproportionate burdens and prioritize emissions reductions in disadvantaged communities.”35  
The Joint Parties proposed that CECONY use the data it collects on DACs, as a result of the 
DAC Annual Report, and remedy any disproportionate burdens (should any burdens occur) 
resulting from the proposed programs in the Joint Proposal.36  
In its Reply to Statements in Opposition, Staff stated “[w]hile staff agrees that any 
disproportionate burdens should be remedied as swiftly as possible, specific requirements to 
relieve disproportionate burdens would tie the Commission’s hands in how and when to address 
any such disproportionate burdens.”37 On cross-examination, Counsel for PULP asked Staff

inconsistent with those mandates.43 By contrast, the language of Section 7(3) is categorical: the 
legislature included no language allowing agencies to justify disproportionate burden on 
DACs.44 “[W]here the Legislature ‘includes particular language in one section of a statute but

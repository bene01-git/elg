PRELIMINARY STATEMENT 
 
In accordance with the June 4, 2023, Ruling on Schedule, the Public Utility Law Project 
(“PULP”) hereby submits this Statement in Reply to the Initial Statements on the Joint Proposal 
(“JP”) filed on June 27, 2023, in the above captioned proceedings. PULP argues that New York 
State Electric & Gas Corporation (“NYSEG”) and Rochester Gas and Electric Corporation 
(“RG&E”) (or the “Companies”) and the Signatory Parties to the JP, have failed to meet their 
burden of proving that the JP is in the public interest. 
In PULP’s Statement in Opposition (“SIO”) to the JP, our organization identified several 
concerns with the JP, which when considered, outweigh the potential benefits of the JP. These 
items included the overall unaffordability of the proposed rates; the JP not being supported by a 
majority of parties; the three-year fixed 9.2% return on equity (“ROE”) and associated earnings 
sharing mechanism (“ESM”) proposed by the signatory parties are not in the public’s interest; 
the lack of a specific commitment in the JP to increased outreach efforts to specifically help the 
Companies’ residential customers deal with the rate increases; the Companies’ Disadvantaged 
Communities (“DACs”) Report lacks the opportunity for public engagement, such as an annual 
meeting with stakeholders, while also failing to report on additional items such as outages in 
DACs; and the lack of adequate, immediate relief to specifically assist DACs and low-income 
households with the increased energy burden created by the new rates.1  
PULP argues herein that neither the Statements in Support (“SIS”), nor the Statements in 
Partial Support and Partial Opposition, justify the substantial rate increases included in the JP 
and believe that they continue to outweigh the other proposals included, thereby making the JP 
not in the public interest. Moreover, PULP remains concerned that there has been no public 
report nor finalization of the Department of Public Service’s investigation into NYSEG and 
RG&E’s billing issues.2 As a result, PULP once again urges the Administrative Law Judges 
(“ALJs”) and the Public Service Commission (“PSC” or “Commission”) to reject the JP. The 
cases should be remanded for the purpose of establishing temporary rates pending litigation. The

B. THE STATEMENTS IN SUPPORT FILED FAIL TO JUSTIFY THAT THE 9.2% 
RETURN ON EQUITY IS IN THE PUBLIC INTEREST. 
PULP remains concerned that the fixed 9.2% ROE is ultimately not in the public interest. 
When reviewing the SIS, both Department Staff and the Companies argue that 9.2% is a fair 
compromise.18 The Companies start by making the assertion that the JP “strikes a fair balance,” 
the Companies make an effort to justify the 9.2% ROE because, “...the Joint Proposal includes a 
Return on Equity (“ROE”) that is lower than the ROE sought by the Companies in their 
testimony.”19 While true that the Companies originally requested a 10.2 % ROE and 9.2% is 
lower, PULP believes that having an ROE that is “lower” than what the Companies originally 
requested is not a good enough justification to award the 9.2% ROE for three years without 
annual updates that take into account changes in financial and economic conditions that affect 
the Companies’ cost of capital.  
Later in its SIS, the Companies make two additional arguments. One being the need to 
consider the cost of capital, especially in light of the need to meet the State’s clean energy 
goals.20 The other argument made involves comparing the proposed 9.2% ROE found in the JP 
with ROEs from three other investor-owned utilities, Orange and Rockland, Corning Natural 
Gas, and the pending JP in Consolidated Edison.21  
PULP finds these justifications to be unpersuasive in this day and age. Due to the 
increase in economic pressures both inside the rate case and out, this business-as-usual approach 
to setting and justifying the ROE is not in the public interest. The fact that one utility company 
has an ROE that is similar to another company’s ROE should not be overly persuasive. As 
discussed in PULP’s SIO, we do not believe negotiated ROEs set for the entirety of a multi-year 
rate plan are in the public interest as the ROE has substantial impact on rates. Due to increased 
costs and financial pressure on rate payers, PULP believes that maintaining the status quo with 
the setting of the ROE is a missed opportunity. PULP believes that the proposal we recommend 
in our SIO, that includes an annual resetting of the ROE, has a substantial opportunity for saving 
rate payers considerably.22 We call on the ALJs and the Commission to not allow a “business as

C. THERE HAS BEEN NO INDICATION THAT THE JP INCLUDES ANY NEW 
EFFORTS TO SPECIFICALLY TARGET AND ASSIST LOW-INCOME 
HOUSEHOLDS WITH THEIR INCREASE IN ENERGY BURDEN 
 
The Companies’ SIS describe measures included in the JP that will “significantly 
expands outreach efforts”23 in relation to the Energy Affordability Program (“EAP”), but much 
of what is included in the JP is not new and are aspects the Companies are already undertaking to 
some degree. For instance, the Companies discuss filing outreach plans that: 
“at a minimum: (1) Outbound Calling Campaigns; (2) Bill Messages; (3) Energy Lines 
 
Bill Inserts; (4) Website Postings; (5) E-mails; and (6) Interactive Voice Response 
 
messaging. The Companies will also expand community, agency and municipal outreach 
 
intended to ensure new groups of vulnerable populations are aware of internal and 
 
external assistance programs and payment options.”24 
The Companies do commit to analyzing EAP participation by county and with the use of 
census data, then work on increasing outreach in identified communities.25 However, PULP 
argues that where the Companies are indicating that they will do “more outreach” there should 
be specific targets in place to ensure that more than passive monitoring is occurring, but that 
there are specific targets in place for the Companies to achieve.26 For instance, there should be 
proactive growth in EAP enrollment and if such growth is not occurring during the rate plan, 
there should be measures in place that direct the Companies to do more. Moreover, when re-
reviewing JP Appendix P, the Companies use the term “continue” in multiple locations, 
confirming that these are actions the Companies are already undertaking, rather than being 
new.27

that the Companies will do specific, targeted, outreach to low-income households solely based

D. STATEMENTS FILED DO NOT ADEQUATELY IDENTIFY THE BENEFITS 
FOR DACS AND SHOULD BE REFORMED IN A NUMBER OF WAYS 
 
i. The Disadvantage Communities Report is not enough to ensure benefits to 
DACs nor enough to prevent disproportionate burdens on DACs.  
In its SIS, Department Staff describe the Disadvantaged Communities Report (hereinafter 
“Report”) included in the JP as being, “...consistent with, and in furtherance of, the objectives of 
the CLCPA, as well as the State’s goals, pertaining to both clean energy and disadvantaged 
communities.”28 The Companies describe the Report included in the JP as going, “...beyond legal 
requirements and file an annual report on the impact of various programs in disadvantaged 
communities, including: (1) clean energy spending; (2) Electric Vehicle Make-Ready program; 
(3) demand response; and (4) DERs.”29 PULP, in contrast, sees the Report as being the bare 
minimum in the effort to comply with the Climate Leadership and Community Protection Act 
(“CLCPA”) and we also believe that the reporting requirement created by the JP, itself requires 
more guardrails than what is proposed in the JP.

While the Report will provide the Commission with annual data, the real question is how 
the Commission will ensure that the Companies use the data to modify existing programs and 
implement new actions that will benefit DACs. Collecting data is not enough. There should be 
guardrails in place set by the JP that sets action to be taken after the annual report is filed to 
ensure that the report is reviewed, and action directed from the report itself. To help ensure that 
the data will not just be reviewed, but also that action is directed from it, there should be a public 
component to this process that will facilitate discussion, engagement, and future action. The JP 
should require the posting of the annual report in multiple public locations, from DMM to the 
Companies’ websites. There should also be at least one meeting per annual report for the purpose 
of engaging the public in discussions about the data found and how the Companies can improve 
their DAC related work. There should also be accurate notice and multiple forums for 
participation to account for the public’s varying ability to participate in meetings due to work/life 
schedules and access to technology, such as internet access.  
Such action is not foreign to the Department/Commission and the utilities. While PULP 
cautions that a similar Report was included in the pending Consolidated Edison JP, though we do 
not believe that Report goes far enough, it does include the requirement of an annual meeting 
with stakeholders following the filing of the annual DAC report.30 This is an important aspect 
that will help ensure public engagement and increase the chances that the data collected by the 
Report can be used to create better conditions for DACs. As a result, PULP urges the ALJs and 
the Commission to require greater avenues for public participation, including an annual meeting 
and the public posting of the Report in multiple locations, to make the passive reporting 
requirement a progressive process that has a greater chance of resulting in better programs and 
efforts to help DACs.

iii. The Statements in Support Fail to Adequately Address Disproportionate 
Burdens Placed on DACs due to the proposed rates.  
CLCPA Section 7(3) requires all State agencies to ensure that their decisions will not 
“disproportionately burden disadvantaged communities.”33 PULP believes that disproportionate 
burdens include economic effects, and thereby, the increase in energy burden caused by the 
substantial rate increase is a factor that must be considered by the ALJs and the Commission 
when reviewing the JP.  
In the Company’s SIS they argue that there are several provisions included in the JP that 
address affordability, which PULP similarly discussed above in Section 3.34 PULP remains 
concerned that the JP does not offer any new outreach and education based on the increase in 
rates themselves and that this economic impact will result in disproportionate burdens on DACs 
and that neither the JP, nor the Statements in Support, adequately address. There should be 
specific, direct outreach to DACs based on the increase in rates to alert these communities based 
on the billing changes coming their way, and from there the Companies can turn to existing 
programs such as EAP and referrals to NYSERDA for energy efficiency programs to assist 
DACs.

CONCLUSION 
 
PULP’s review of the SIS and/or partial support fails to relieve our concerns that the JP is 
not in the public interest. The sheer rate increases themselves and the fixed 9.2% ROE raise 
significant affordability concerns, especially for low-income households and DACs. Moreover, 
while there are customer service protections in place by the JP, some are new, but many were 
already in existence and none will provide direct, targeted outreach and education based solely 
on the rate increases themselves, which PULP believes is absolutely necessary to prepare the 
ratepayers for the increases proposed in the JP. Moreover, a close review of “who” the Signatory 
Parties are and why, also contributes to PULP’s evaluation that the JP is not in the public 
interest. As a result, PULP once again urges the ALJs and the Commission to remand these cases 
for the purpose of establishing temporary rates pending litigation. The temporary rate order 
should contain the modified programs and practices included in PULP’s Statement in Opposition 
to the JP and the herein Reply Statement.

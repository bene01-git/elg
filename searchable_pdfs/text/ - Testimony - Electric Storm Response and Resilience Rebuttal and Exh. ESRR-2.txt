Communication Triggers 
The main cause of duplication and overlap between the ONIM and ETR protocols are the 
“triggers” or outage thresholds for communication activities. The ETR protocols are 
triggered when there are 5,000 customers out of service in an operating area for more 
than 30 minutes or 20,000 customers out of service companywide for more than 30 
minutes (ETR Protocol, p. 1). The ONIM on the other hand contains six separate outage 
thresholds each with a significantly higher customer outage count and each with a 
“waiting period” of between one and three hours before the trigger occurs1. In addition, 
the ONIM does not have an outage threshold for Company operating areas (see ONIM 
II). And finally, unlike the ETR Protocol requirements, certain communication activities 
are not required under the ONIM depending on the outage threshold classification.  
Simply put, the ONIM requirements are duplicative because, in most circumstances, the 
ETR Protocols are triggered well before the ONIM requirements and, therefore, the ETR 
communication activities are often performed before the ONIM triggers are met.

Two elements of the ONIM make these duplicative notifications have questionable value. 
First, the ONIM focuses on an initial, one-time notification in a defined time period. For 
example, under the ONIM if 40K customers are out of service for two hours, the 
Company has two hours to complete a communication activity. Similar performance time 
frames exist for the five other outage thresholds. 
Second, for communication under Sections IV(1), IV(2), IV(4) and IV(6), if applicable to 
a particular outage, Con Edison is required to communicate the cause of the outage, the 
geographic area(s) affected, the estimated number of customers affected, and the 
estimated time of restoration, but only “to the extent available.”  Since most ONIM 
notifications are in response to major storms (e.g., outages in more than one load area), 
most of the required information will not be available rendering the communication 
practically valueless. For example, during a major storm, 40K customers could lose 
service for two hours, but the storm would be ongoing, and the full scope of the 
geographic area, the number of affected customers, and the estimated time of restoration 
would not be available. Under the ONIM, Con Edison would be required to issue 
communications, with little to no information, in two hours or face financial penalties.  
Alternatively, the ETR Protocols establish a start of restoration time and require 
communications (every 6 hours in some instances) based on the start of restoration. The 
timing of these communications adds value to customers, emergency response 
organizations and other stakeholders. The duplicative ONIM notifications do not.

Please explain the origin of visual curve fitting. 
7 
A.
Visual curve–fitting was employed before the advent of computers.  Prior to the 
8 
availability of mechanized systems, a series of survivor proportions obtained from an 
9 
observed life table was typically plotted on graph paper and overlaid with 
10 
correspondingly scaled graphs of survivor curves such as the Iowa–type curves.  The 
11 
type curves were drawn with various average service lives such that both the dispersion 
12 
and average service life of the observed proportion surviving could be selected from a 
13 
visual inspection of which curve appeared to best “fit” the data.  A computerized 
14 
version of the same technique has since replaced manual plotting of points and fitting 
15 
to survivor curves.  The type curves (such as Iowa) used in such an analysis can be 
16 
scaled to any average service life, thereby providing a description of both the dispersion 
17 
(i.e., distribution of retirements over time) and average service life exhibited by the 
18 
fitted data.  The “best fitting” curve, however, is still decided by a visual inspection of

What method does Foster Associates use in conducting statistical service–life 
5 
studies? 
6 
A.
The statistical method used by Foster Associates is an application of inferential 
7 
statistics in which hazard rates are graduated rather than survivorship data points fitted 
8 
“visually” to a survivor curve.  This method draws inferences and predictions about 
9 
population service–life parameters based on an analysis of samples drawn from the 
10 
parent population.  Projection lives and projection curves are population parameters 
11 
“inferred” from a statistical analysis of the underlying forces of retirement described 
12 
by probability distributions.  A projection life is an estimate of mean service life of the 
13 
population from which retirements are observed as a random sample.  The fundamental 
14 
probability distribution of interest in estimating the service life of industrial property is 
15 
called a hazard function.  This function, which is also used in reliability theory, 
16 
describes the conditional probability of retirement (called a hazard rate) during an age 
17 
interval given survival to the beginning of the interval.  For example, the probability 
18 
that plant which has been in service, say for five years, will be retired during the sixth 
19 
year is a conditional probability of retirement.  In other words, the probability is 
20 
conditioned upon having achieved an age of five years.  The objective of a statistical 
21 
analysis of plant retirements is to identify the form of an equation that best describes 
22 
the conditional probabilities of retirement, where the form of the equation is dictated

Q.
Are there other reasons to prefer the hazard function statistical techniques used 
4 
by Foster Associates over the visual survivorship functions relied upon by Staff? 
5 
A.
Yes.  The analysis techniques used by Foster Associates overcome a “chaining” 
6 
problem of visual curve fitting.  Each successive point (i.e., proportion surviving) 
7 
plotted against a survivor curve is dependent upon the points plotted for prior age 
8 
intervals.  One or more anomalous or irregular retirements, therefore, will dictate the 
9 
value of points plotted for subsequent age intervals.  Fitting survivor curves to the 
10 
observed proportion surviving will seldom produce an accurate description of the 
11 
underlying forces of mortality or prove a sound estimate of projections lives. 
12 
13 
In short, the statistical methods Foster Associates uses maximize the informational 
14 
content of the data and minimize the influence of extraneous events by analyzing the 
15 
underlying forces of retirement at the level of independent hazard rates.  Absent the use 
16 
of more powerful statistical techniques, however, life analysis simply becomes an 
17 
exercise in trying to visually fit a curve to an oddly shaped array of data points.

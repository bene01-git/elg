Q. 
Is the statistical analysis the only basis for the service life estimates in the 
1 
depreciation study? 
2 
A. 
No.  While the statistical analysis is an important component of the “life analysis” 
3 
process of depreciation studies, it is only a tool in determining the life 
4 
characteristics that a utility’s assets have experienced historically.  The goal of the 
5 
depreciation study is to estimate the service lives of the assets that are currently in 
6 
service, not those that were retired in the past.  Thus, the process is to develop 
7 
informed estimates of what will occur in the future, not simply to determine what 
8 
has occurred in the past and assume it will recur.   
9 
Q. 
Please continue. 
10 
A. 
The full process of determining the service life estimates is referred to as “life 
11 
estimation.”  Factors other than the statistical analysis should be considered, both 
12 
because the historical statistical data are not always definitive (i.e., often more than 
13 
one survivor curve will represent a similar fit to an original life table, as usually 
14 
only a “stub curve”2 is available for the analysis) and because the historical data 
15 
may not be reflective of future experience (e.g., if the Company were planning to 
16 
replace more assets in the future than it has historically).  
17 
 
2  A “stub curve” means that the original life table developed from the historical data is not a complete curve, 
i.e. it does not reach 0% surviving.  Because the retirement rate method generally will only produce stub 
curves for many accounts, there is typically an aspect of forecasting or projecting future experience in the 
statistical analysis (even if no other factors are considered).

NET SALVAGE ACCRUALS AND NET SALVAGE COSTS SHOULD NOT BE 
1 
EXPECTED TO BE THE SAME 
2 
 
3 
Q. 
Please explain the distinction between net salvage costs and net salvage 
4 
accruals. 
5 
A. 
Net salvage costs represent the dollars that the Company has expended on net 
6 
salvage (i.e., gross salvage received less cost of removal expended).  These costs 
7 
are related to assets that have already been retired and are no longer in service.  In 
8 
contrast, the depreciation accruals for net salvage are accruals related to costs that 
9 
have not yet been incurred.  These accruals are for the future net salvage costs for 
10 
assets that are currently in service.  Because net salvage accruals relate to the assets 
11 
currently in service, whereas net salvage costs are for assets that have been retired 
12 
and are no longer providing service, net salvage accruals and net salvage costs 
13 
should not be expected to be the same or even be of a similar dollar level to one 
14 
another. 
15 
Q. 
Please provide an example to demonstrate that net salvage accruals and net 
16 
salvage costs should not be expected to be the same. 
17 
A. 
Consider a single asset that has a ten-year service life.  When the asset will be 
18 
retired, it will cost $1,000 to remove from service and there will be no gross salvage.  
19 
The net salvage is, therefore, negative $1,000 ($0 in gross salvage less $1,000 in 
20 
cost of removal).  The net salvage accruals for this asset will be $100 in each of the 
21 
ten years the asset will be in service (i.e., the $1,000 net salvage cost divided by ten 
22

years).  However, the net salvage cost will not be incurred until year ten, at which 
1 
time the entire $1,000 is expended.  Thus, for years one through nine, the net 
2 
salvage accruals will be larger than any net salvage costs ($100 compared to $0 in 
3 
net salvage costs), but in year ten the net salvage costs will be higher than the net 
4 
salvage accruals ($1,000 compared to $100 in net salvage accruals).  Over the full 
5 
ten-year period, the net salvage accruals will sum to the net salvage cost, fully 
6 
recovering this cost over the life of the asset.  However, by allocating the net 
7 
salvage costs over the life of the asset, customers pay an equitable portion of the 
8 
cost of the asset in each year of its service life. 
9 
 
 
Depreciation accounting is intended to equitably spread the costs of an 
10 
asset, including net salvage, over the period of time the asset will be in service.  
11 
Because net salvage costs are only expended at the end of the asset’s life, but net 
12 
salvage accruals are spread evenly over the asset’s life, the timing of net salvage 
13 
accruals and net salvage costs should be expected to be different.

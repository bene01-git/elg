(Staff)  in response to the following: 1) Mr. Bob Wyman’s (Wyman) opposition to the gas 
portion of the Joint Proposal related to his alternative proposals regarding gas plant depreciation, 
the reduction of the weighted cost of capital (WACOC), and limiting investments in gas capital

Under the Settlement Guidelines, the Commission, when considering whether the Joint 
Proposal is in the public interest, the document should be considered as a whole, with each 
individual section providing support and balance to the others.  As a whole, there can be no 
question that the Joint Proposal satisfies the public interest standard expressed in the Settlement 
Guidelines as we explained in Staff’s Statement in Support.13 
PULP’s claims that the Joint Proposal is contrary to the Settlement Guidelines is misplaced.  
First, the Settlement Guidelines do not require that the Joint Proposal reflect accommodation 
among all the parties.  The order adopting the Settlement Guidelines states that a desirable joint 
proposal should: strive for a balance among ratepayer protection; fairness to investors; and the 
long-term viability of the utility.  Furthermore, a joint proposal should be consistent with sound 
environmental, social and economic policies of the Agency and the State.  Finally, it should also 
produce results within the range of reasonable results from a Commission decision after a fully 
litigated process.14  In its order adopting the Settlement Guidelines, the Commission rejected 
adding an additional factor, whether the Joint Proposal is contested.15   
Apparently, PULP is of the belief that any opposition to the Joint Proposal, or particular 
aspects of it, are prima facia evidence that the Joint Proposal does not meet the requirements of 
the Settlement Guidelines.  The Commission’s order adopting the guidelines rejects this idea, 
stating that it would give weight to the fact that all parties executed the Joint Proposal, but that 
absence of an objection does not relieve it of its obligation under the PSL to make an 
independent assessment of a joint proposal and whether it is in the public interest.16  Similarly, 
opposition in and of itself is not evidence that a joint proposal is not in the public interest.  The 
Commission must, in any instance of a joint proposal, make its own assessment based on the

b.  
Affordability of Rate Plan 
The Joint Proposal recommends increasing electric revenues on a levelized basis by 
$457.5 million in each rate year or by 4.2 percent, 4.0 percent, and 3.8 percent on a total bill 
basis in RY1, RY2, and RY3, respectively.19  For the gas business the Joint Proposal 
recommends increasing revenues on a levelized basis by $187.2 million in each rate year or by 
6.7 percent, 6.3 percent, and 5.9 percent on a total bill basis in RY1, RY2, and RY3, 
respectively.20  
PULP, Sane, and AARP all contend that the increases recommended in the Joint Proposal 
are unaffordable.21   Furthermore, PULP raised concerns regarding the bill impacts of low-
income households eligible for, but not enrolled in, the Company’s Energy Assistance Plan22 and 
Sane claims the Joint Proposal does not offer any additional resources to mitigate the bill impact 
of low and fixed income customers.23  Finally, AARP noted that the recommended increases are 
not inclusive of the various surcharges included on customer bills, collected to fund mandated 
programs emanating from decisions in other forums or that are designed to shield shareholders 
from impacts of deviations from forecasts or to provide financial rewards.24 
The increases recommended in the Joint Proposal are significantly lower than the 
amounts initially sought by the Company.  As indicated in Staff’s Statement in Support the

1.  
Six-Percent Energy Burden and File Matching Concerns are 
Misplaced 
WE ACT/AGREE claim that low-income customers that are already at the Commission’s 
six percent target energy burden level would exceed that threshold as a result of the rate 
increases provided for in the Joint Proposal.35  They also point out the file match issue where the 
Company failed to receive matching data from Westchester County Department of Social 
Services (DSS) for low-income program enrollment for 2021 and beginning of 2022.36  WE 
ACT/AGREE state that the Joint Proposal does not address the issue or include provisions for 
improvement.37 
Regarding  WE ACT/AGREE’s for first concern regarding low-income customers 
exceeding the six percent energy burden, the Commission directed in its Order Adopting Energy 
Affordability Policy Modifications and Directing Utility Filings (Phase 2 Order) that utilities file 
revised Energy Affordability Policy discounts that account for any rate increases whenever a 
utility files tariff amendments in compliance with a new rate plan.38  The revised discounts will 
protect low-income customers from any lag in EAP program updates between when new rates 
become effective and the annual update cycle which occurs on December 1 of each year.  To 
address the concern regarding improvements to the file match process, the Joint Proposal 
includes the provision that the Company will develop internal controls for management to be 
notified of completed file matches each quarter.  Also, for transparency, the Company will notify 
and update stakeholders through the Energy Affordability Policy Working Group and on 
submitted monthly reports of completed file matches.39  Therefore, these concerns raised by WE 
ACT /AGREE have already been addressed by the Commission and the Joint Proposal.

its service territories, so the adoption of RNG offsets the need for incremental pipeline supplied 
gas to meet these customer demands, thus, decreasing the Company’s pipeline reliance.

2. Gas Issues 
a.  
Depreciation 
 
In the Wyman Statement, Mr. Wyman objects to the continuation of the use of straight-
line depreciation and proposes instead to switch to unit-of-production (UoP) depreciation.   Mr. 
Wyman argues that the use of straight-line depreciation is inappropriate in times of declining gas 
demand, as straight-line depreciation would recover an equal amount of cost of an asset over 
each year the asset is in service.  Therefore, as demand falls, the same amount of cost would 
need to be recovered over a smaller customer base, or less total usage, leading to higher base 
rates. 
UoP depreciation, conversely, recovers an equal amount of cost over each unit of 
commodity delivered to customers, which means that the amount recovered each year would 
scale with demand and that base rates would not see an increase due to depreciation even if 


--- Page 40 ---
Cases 22-E-0064 and 22-G-0065 
38 
 
demand declines.  The use of UoP depreciation would result in an increase in depreciation 
expense collected immediately after the change to UoP depreciation, but Wyman proposes to 
offset this increase through adjusting the equity ratio of the Weighted Cost of Capital to take on 
more debt.    
In addition, Mr. Wyman also argues that the Company should be limited regarding the 
gas capital assets it may deploy on its gas distribution system to only those required for safe and 
reliable service  but provides no explanation or justification as to how the Joint Proposal fails in 
this regard.   NYGEO takes a similar position on depreciation, arguing that the Company should 
implement an alternative to straight-line depreciation such as UoP depreciation.   
While Mr. Wyman’s characterizations of straight-line depreciation and UoP depreciation 
are accurate, there are multiple reasons why the adoption of UoP depreciation is inappropriate in 
to use for the Company’s gas business.  First, it is premature to adopt UoP depreciation without 
further guidance from the Commission on how to address depreciation in light of the CLCPA.  
Second, while the effects of potential declining gas demand on depreciation is a concern for all 
utilities, at the moment, there is no process currently developed to accurately forecast out the 
potential loss of customers or declining usage over such long periods of time.  The proper 
calculation of UoP depreciation rates would necessitate a reasonably accurate forecast of gas 
demand, as well as future capital expenses needed to operate the gas system properly and safely, 
until the end of service for the Company’s gas system, both of which far exceed the forecasts 
reflected in the Joint Proposal and would extend beyond the Company’s long term gas plan that 
is expected to be filed later this year.  Therefore, at this juncture, it is prohibitively difficult to 
forecast, with any degree of accuracy, the total accumulated gas sales and total amount of future 
capital expenses to the extent necessary for UoP depreciation with the information currently 
available.  Additionally, at no point in testimony or in his Statement of Opposition did Mr. 
Wyman propose specific depreciation rates or the increased revenue requirement and associated 
bill impacts to customers resulting from the immediate change to UoP depreciation.  For these 
reasons, the Joint Proposal’s support of the continued use of straight-line depreciation for this 
proceeding is appropriate.

Average authorized ROE for electric nudges up but drops for 
gas in 2022
Friday, February 3, 2023 9:00 AM ET 
By Lisa Fontanella 
Market Intelligence
The average authorized return on equity for electric utilities approved in cases decided during 2022 rebounded from 
2021, which was the lowest annual average in RRA's rate case database comprising all major rate cases decided since 
1980. Despite the rise, however, the average authorized ROE for electric utilities in 2022 remained near historic lows 
and was the third-lowest annual average on record. 
For gas utilities, the average authorized ROE in 2022 fell to the second-lowest annual average on record. 
The average ROE authorized for electric utilities rose to 9.54% for rate cases decided 
in 2022 from the 9.38% average for cases decided in 2021. There were 53 electric 
ROE determinations reflected in the calculations for 2022 versus 55 in 2021.  
The average ROE authorized for gas utilities was 9.53% for cases decided in 2022, 
slightly lower than the 9.56% average observed in 2021. There were 33 gas rate case 
decisions that included an ROE determination during 2022 versus 43 in 2021. 
The electric data set includes several limited-issue rider cases. Historically, the ROEs 
authorized in limited-issue rider cases were meaningfully higher than those approved 
in general rate cases, driven primarily by incentives allowed in Virginia for certain 
types of generation investment. These premiums have largely expired, narrowing the 
gap between the average ROE in the rider cases and general rate cases. Excluding 
rider cases, the average authorized ROE for electric cases was 9.52% in 2022 versus 
9.39% in 2021. By contrast, limited issue riders have not had much impact on average 
ROEs in the gas sector, as most of the gas riders rely on ROEs approved in a 
previous base rate case. 
In 2022, the median ROE authorized in all electric utility rate cases was 9.50% versus 
9.38% in 2021; for gas utilities, the metric was 9.60% in both 2022 and 2021.

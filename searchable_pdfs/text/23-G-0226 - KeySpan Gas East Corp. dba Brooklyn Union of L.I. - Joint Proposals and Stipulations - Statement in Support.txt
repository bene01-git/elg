II.
The Parties Claiming That The Bill Impacts Arising From The JP Are Too High
Unreasonably Ignore The Record And Circumstances Of This Case
While several opponents of the JP claim that the proposed rate impacts are too high,5 these
assertions unreasonably ignore both the record of this case and the circumstances that predated it.  
As discussed in the Companies’ SIS (at 18-23), the rate increases proposed in the JP reflect a 
reasonable compromise of positions advocated by the Companies, Staff, NYC, and other parties 
who submitted testimony.  Moreover, many of the cost pressures that are driving the revenue 
requirements proposed in the JP arise from factors that are beyond the Companies’ control, 
including the impact of inflation, the Federal Reserve’s response to inflation (e.g., raising interest 
rates), the need to comply with federal and state pipeline safety mandates, and property tax 
increases.6   
In addition, it must be recognized that the revenue increases proposed in the JP are driven 
in significant part by the fact that the Companies’ current rate plans were negotiated during the 
COVID-19 Pandemic and went to great lengths to minimize rate increases.7  As Commission Chair 
Rory M. Christian recognized in an editorial concerning the Commission’s most recent rate 
decision for New York State Electric and Gas Corporation (“NYSEG”): 
Last time rates were set for NYSEG was at the height of the COVID-19 pandemic.  
In 2020, with no vaccine yet on the horizon and unemployment soaring, the PSC 
deliberately put off rate increases that had been proposed by several parties who 
had reached a rate agreement with the company.  At that time of great uncertainty,

VI.
PULP’s Proposal For An Annual ROE Reopener Is Contrary To Commission Policy
And Precedent And Should Be Rejected
PULP asserts that the Commission should either establish a mechanism for recalculating
the return on equity (“ROE”) on an annual basis during the term of the JP or modify the ROE to 
reset it at the lower of 9.35 percent or the result of Staff’s ROE model run with the most recent 
available data.44  PULP’s proposal should be rejected for several reasons. 
First, PULP’s proposal is premised on the erroneous notion that there is a Staff ROE model 
that has been or could be adopted by the Commission45 as the means of setting the ROE in utility 
rate cases without knowing the impact of that decision on the ultimate rates charged by the utility 
or its continued financial integrity.  This premise runs afoul of the most basic legal tenets that 
govern ratemaking; namely (i) that it is the result reached, not the methodology employed that 
determines whether the end result of ratemaking is just and reasonable and (ii) that the ROE should 
be commensurate with returns on investments in other enterprises having corresponding risks and 
should be sufficient to assure confidence in the financial integrity of the enterprise, so as to 
maintain its credit and to attract capital.46  As neither the Commission, PULP itself, nor any other

I. INTRODUCTION
Alliance for a Green Economy, Fossil Free Tompkins, Campaign for Renewable Energy,
Ratepayer and Community Intervenors, and Climate Solutions Accelerator, oppose the Joint
Proposal (“JP”) in the above referenced proceeding filed by NYSEG/RG&E (the “Companies”),
the Department of Public Service Staff (“Staff”) and a small number of other full and partial
signatories on June 14, 2023. This filing outlines the reasons for our opposition to the Joint
Proposal.
We take no pleasure in writing this statement of opposition. Our organizations have committed
countless hours as active participants in this case. We filed testimony, actively pushed back
against continued extensions and the make-whole agreements that will result in rate
compression harming consumers, and filed a motion raising alarm about the inadequate
information provided by the Companies in this case (See Exhibit A). The inadequacy of the
Companies’ filing demonstrated a blatant disregard for the Companies’ customers and for the
regulations and standards enforced by the New York Public Service Commision (“Commission”).
Throughout, we also actively participated in settlement negotiations in the hopes that despite
the unacceptable rate hikes being requested, the shortcomings in the Companies’ case, and the

constituents and the planet. Regrettably, this is not the outcome that was achieved.
As community-based non-profit and public interest groups working throughout the Companies’
service territories on issues of consumer advocacy, energy justice, and renewable energy, we
have a direct connection to the communities most impacted by the utilities’ rates and
programming. We have brought the various concerns of our constituents to bear on the

Our organizations are joined by the other public interest organizations, filing separately, that
participated in these proceedings and also oppose the Joint Proposal. Collectively we represent
the concerns of tens of thousands of NYSEG and RG&E customers.
II. STANDARD OF REVIEW
According to the Commission’s settlement procedures and guidelines1, the standard of
review for Joint Proposals is as follows:
Procedural soundness, of course, is necessary but not sufficient, and the factors to be
considered in the ensuing substantive review do not lend themselves to codification. As
the comments suggest, they include (1) the settlement's consistency with law and with
the regulatory, economic, social, and environmental policies of the Commission and the
State; (2) whether the result compares favorably with the likely result of full litigation and
is within the range of reasonable outcomes; (3) whether the settlement strikes a fair
balance among the interests of ratepayers and investors and the long-term soundness of
the utility; (4) the existence of a rational basis for decision; (5) the completeness of the
record; and (6) whether the settlement is contested.

The first four of the foregoing factors are themselves elements of the public interest
standard. The fifth and sixth, in contrast, simply guide us in our assessment. We would
give weight, for example, to the fact that a settlement has been agreed to by all parties,
including normally adversarial ones, but the absence of objection would not relieve us of
our obligation to form an independent judgment that the settlement is in the public
interest. Similarly, the less developed the record, the greater the burden on the
settlement's proponents to show that the result compares favorably to the likely result of
full litigation.
The Joint Proposal fails to meet each of these standards of review for the reasons discussed
below.

IV. THE JOINT PROPOSAL IS NOT CONSISTENT WITH LAW AND WITH THE
REGULATORY, ECONOMIC, SOCIAL, AND ENVIRONMENTAL POLICIES OF
THE COMMISSION AND THE STATE
The Joint Proposal does not comply with New York’s landmark climate law, the Climate
Leadership and Community Protection Act (“CLCPA”), which requires all state agencies,
including the Public Service Commission and Department of Public Service, to assess and
implement strategies and regulations to achieve a 40% reduction in greenhouse gas emissions
from 1990 levels by 2030, and an 85% reduction in greenhouse gas emissions from 1990 levels
and net zero emissions across all sectors by 2050. Below we outline the multiple sections of the
CLCPA and subsequent Scoping Plan developed by the New York Climate Action Council
(“CAC”).
Utility rate case proposals before the Public Service Commission are required to demonstrate
compliance with the requirements of the CLCPA, as does any settlement proposal. This is clear
from the law and was reinforced by Commission Order in cases 19-G-0309 and 19-G-0310.5
a.
The Joint Proposal does not comply with Section 7(2) of CLCPA, which states:
In considering and issuing permits, licenses, and other administrative approvals
and decisions, including but not limited to the execution of grants, loans, and
contracts, all state agencies, offices, authorities, and divisions shall consider

The Scoping Plan also recommends a new building and energy code which will require new
buildings and renovations to be all-electric and highly efficient.8 This recommendation was
implemented in the Advanced Building Codes and Equipment Standards Act of 20229 and the
All-Electric Building Act of 2023,10 which prohibits fossil fueled systems in new construction
starting in 2025 for small buildings and 2028 for large buildings. The Scoping Plan also
recommends prohibiting replacement of fossil fuel heating, cooling, and hot water systems at
the end of their useful lives beginning in 2030 for residential-sized equipment and 2035 for
commercial-sized equipment, and 2035 for fossil fuel appliances for cooking and clothes
drying.11 This means that by 2050, nearly every building in the state will be all-electric, and DPS
must plan for this to ensure an orderly, reliable, and just transition.
The Scoping Plan states that the DPS should lead the development of a coordinated plan that,
among other things, “meets greenhouse gas (‘GHG’) emission reduction targets” using the

reduce GHG emissions by 2030 and by 2050, as required to contribute to achieving the
statewide GHG emission limits established in the Climate Act.
●
Include utility-specific and system-wide forecasts for the reduction in gas sales and
decreasing numbers of gas customers connected to the gas system over time, as large
numbers of customers transition to electrification and community thermal for heating, hot
water, and other energy end uses.

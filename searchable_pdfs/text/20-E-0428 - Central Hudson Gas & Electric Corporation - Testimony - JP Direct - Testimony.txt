2. ELECTRIC ECOS STUDY 
Q 
WHAT IS AN EMBEDDED CLASS COST-OF-SERVICE STUDY? 
1 
A 
An ECOS study is an analysis used to determine each class’s responsibility for a 
2 
utility’s costs.  Thus, it determines whether the revenues a class generates cover the 
3 
class’s cost of service.  An ECOS study separates the utility's total costs into portions 
4 
incurred on behalf of the various customer groups.  Most of a utility's costs are incurred 
5 
to jointly serve many customers.  For purposes of revenue allocation and rate design, 
6 
customers are grouped into homogeneous classes according to their usage patterns 
7 
and service characteristics.  The procedures typically used in an ECOS study are 
8 
described in more detail in Exhibit ___ (JP-3).   
9 
Q

Q 
WHAT IS THE MATCHING PRINCIPLE? 
14 
A 
The Matching Principle is one of the cornerstones of ratemaking.  In essence, the 
15 
Matching Principle means that all ratemaking components (e.g., rate base, revenues, 
16 
expenses, allocation factors, billing determinants) should be based on the same set of 
17 
assumptions (i.e., a test year).  This will provide a consistent and realistic measure of 
18 
the utility’s revenue requirements, as well as the costs incurred to serve the utility’s 
19 
various service classes.  As applied to ECOS Studies, the Matching Principle means 
20 
that the same assumptions should be used in determining both the costs and the 
21 
revenues.

Q 
WHAT IS CLASS REVENUE ALLOCATION? 
1 
A 
Class revenue allocation is the process of determining how any delivery revenue 
2 
change approved by the Commission should be spread to each service class served 
3 
by the utility.   
4 
Q 
HOW SHOULD ANY CHANGE IN DELIVERY REVENUES APPROVED IN THIS 
5 
PROCEEDING BE SPREAD AMONG THE VARIOUS SERVICE CLASSES SERVED 
6 
BY CHG&E? 
7 
A 
Delivery revenues should reflect the actual cost of providing service as closely as 
8 
practicable.  As a general rule, rates should be set at cost.  However, regulators 
9 
sometimes limit the immediate movement to cost based on other considerations, such 
10 
as gradualism concerns.  Gradualism is a concept that the Commission historically 
11 
has used to prevent a class from receiving an overly-large rate increase.  Under this 
12 
practice, the movement to cost-of-service is made gradually, rather than all at once.   
13 
Q

14 
IN DETERMINING HOW ANY BASE REVENUE CHANGE SHOULD BE 
15 
ALLOCATED? 
16 
A 
Cost-based rates will send the proper price signals to customers.  The other reasons 
17 
for adhering to cost-of-service principles are equity, engineering efficiency (cost-
18 
minimization), stability and conservation.

Q 
WHY ARE COST-BASED RATES EQUITABLE? 
1 
A 
Rates that primarily reflect cost-of-service considerations are equitable because each 
2 
customer pays what it actually costs the utility to serve that customer – no more and 
3 
no less.  If rates are not based on cost, then some customers must pay part of the cost 
4 
of providing service to other customers, which is inequitable. 
5 
Q 
HOW DO COST-BASED RATES PROMOTE ENGINEERING EFFICIENCY? 
6 
A 
With respect to engineering efficiency, when rates are designed so that customer, 
7 
demand and/or energy charges are properly reflected in the rate structure, customers 
8 
are provided with the proper incentive to minimize their costs which will, in turn, 
9 
minimize the costs to the utility. 
10 
Q 
HOW CAN COST-BASED RATES PROVIDE STABILITY? 
11 
A 
When rates are tied closely to cost, the utility's earnings are stabilized because 
12 
changes in customer use patterns result in parallel changes in revenues and 
13 
expenses.  If rates are not based on cost, then an increase in usage by subsidized 
14 
classes or a decrease in usage by classes providing subsidies would adversely affect 
15 
the utility’s earnings.  That is because, as subsidized classes grow, a utility’s revenue 
16 
shortfall also will grow over time.  Conversely, as an overcharged customer class 
17 
grows, its customers become increasingly important to utility earnings, while those 
18 
customers are simultaneously given greater incentives to reduce their use of the 
19 
utility’s system.   
20 
Q 
HOW DO COST-BASED RATES ENCOURAGE CONSERVATION? 
21 
A 
By providing customers with balanced price signals against which to make 
22 
consumption decisions, cost-based rates encourage conservation (of both peak day

Q 
SHOULD AN RDM ADJUSTMENT BE TRIGGERED AS A RESULT OF THE GAIN 
1 
OR LOSS OF CUSTOMERS? 
2 
A 
No.  RDMs are designed to cushion the impact of energy efficiency programs on a 
3 
utility’s earnings, although it also reduces a utility’s risks associated with variations in 
4 
weather and business cycles.  An RDM should not be designed to recognize gains or 
5 
losses in the number of customers.  Customers come and go — the gain or loss of 
6 
customers is a risk that all delivery utilities should assume.  This is not a risk that 
7 
should be borne by customers.  This is especially true where the classes at issue have 
8 
an extremely-low number of customers.   
9 
I would note that in the current National Grid electric and gas rate proceeding, 
10 
the Staff Electric and Gas Rates Panel opposed extension of the utility’s gas RDM to 
11 
SC-5 and SC-8 firm transportation service classes because they had so few 
12 
customers.  Specifically, that Panel testified that: 
13 
…we recommend that the Company not expand the gas RDM to SC-5 
14 
and SC-8 classes due to the limited number of customers in those 
15 
service classes.  Since there are so few customers in these classes, 
16 
and since these customers may not always have consistent loads … 
17 
there is a risk of large RDM imbalances which could be detrimental to 
18 
the customers in these service classes.  Additionally, the delivery 
19 
service revenues to these customer classes make up only a small 
20 
portion of the Company’s gas delivery revenues and the Company has 
21 
been at risk for the revenue variations of these customers.15  
22 
In comparison to National Grid’s SC-5 and SC-8 (gas), Central Hudson’s SC-13 
23 
(electric) and SC-11 (gas) have far fewer numbers of customers.

A. 
Term 
 
The Joint Proposal advances three-year electric and gas rate plans, encompassing 
the period July 1, 2021 through March 31, 2024.  (JP at 7.)5  The Joint Proposal also contains 
provisions addressing the proposed adoption thereof subsequent to the start of Rate Year 1.  (See 
JP at 9-10, App. B.) 
 
Multiple Intervenors supports the term of the Joint Proposal.  Initially, Multiple 
Intervenors notes that, based on Central Hudson’s filing of electric and gas rate cases herein on 
August 27, 2020, and the Commission’s statutory, 11-month ratemaking cycle, it is questionable 
whether Central Hudson ordinarily would be entitled to rate relief as early as July 1, 2021, were 
this proceeding to be litigated.  That noted, starting Rate Year 1 on July 1, 2021, is warranted in 
this instance.  Central Hudson historically has utilized rate periods that run from July through June, 
and maintaining such schedule arguably is desirable, for at least certain parties, so long as there 
are no material disadvantages for doing so.  In this proceeding, Multiple Intervenors contends that 
the negotiated electric and gas revenue requirements equitably account for the “early” start date 
for Rate Year 1, and also notes that other parties representing customers (e.g., PULP, UIU) 
similarly support the Joint Proposal, including but not limited to the proposed term.

Multiple Intervenors further contends that under most – but perhaps not all – 
circumstances, three-year terms constitute the “sweet spot” for negotiated resolutions of utility rate 
proceedings.  In the litigation context, Commission rate decisions only are in effect for a minimum 
period of one year, which allows utilities to file new rate cases very shortly (i.e., within one month) 
following the conclusion of their prior rate proceedings.  In the settlement context, however, Joint 
Proposals routinely include provisions whereby the subject utility agrees to “stay-out” or refrain 
from filing for new rates to become effective prior to the conclusion of the settlement term, absent 
exigent circumstances.  (See, e.g., JP at 71-73.) 
 
While Multiple Intervenors asserts that no settlement is preferable to a bad 
settlement, where a Joint Proposal is in the public interest, a negotiated, multi-year rate plan 
provides certain potential advantages over a fully-litigated rate proceeding.  From the utility’s 
perspective, increased revenue certainty is provided, allowing it to focus on operating as efficiently 
as possible without repeated, resource-intensive forays into the rate-setting process.  From 
customers’ perspectives, multi-year rate settlements can provide increased rate certainty.  Other 
benefits of multi-year rate plans to utilities, customers, and regulators include, but are not 
necessarily limited to: the avoidance of annual or near-annual rate case litigation; the opportunity 
to resolve certain issues creatively in a manner for which litigation often is not conducive; and the 
ability to “levelize” and/or moderate near-term rate impacts over a longer period of time.  Thus, 
Multiple Intervenors has long supported efforts to negotiate multi-year rate plans with utilities, 
while recognizing that any Joint Proposal must be evaluated on its own merits, based on the facts 
and the circumstances of the individual rate proceeding in which it was crafted.  In certain 
circumstances – but not those present here – resolving a rate proceeding based on a fully-litigated 
record may be necessary and/or more consistent with the public interest. 


--- Page 9 ---
7 
 
It also should be noted that comprehensive settlements of utility rate proceedings 
require extensive commitments of time and resources by active parties, often well in excess of 
what otherwise would be required under “normal” rate case litigation.  Consequently, it is 
questionable whether rate settlements shorter than three years in duration are “worth” the increased 
effort involved. 
 
On the other hand, because settlements rely, by necessity, on multi-year forecasts 
and projections of various cost elements, numbers of customers, quantities of deliveries, etc., they 
often present an increased level of risk and uncertainty – on all sides – compared to annual rate 
case litigation.  It is fair to state that the longer the rate plan, the greater the risks that the forecasts 
and the projections underlying the plan will prove to be materially inaccurate.  Thus, there is a 
definite balance between negotiating rate plans that are (i) not so short in duration such that they 
fail to justify the increased time and resources required, and (ii) not so long in duration that they 
impose undesirable and/or unreasonable risks on the subject utility and/or its customers. 
 
In Multiple Intervenors’ opinion, three-year rate plans often reflect the “sweet spot” 
of this balancing effort, while recognizing that, depending on the circumstances, negotiated rate 
plans of shorter or longer durations also may satisfy the public interest standard.

C. 
Application of Net Regulatory Liabilities as Moderators 
 
From Multiple Intervenors’ perspective, one of the major issues in this proceeding 
is the treatment of net regulatory liabilities (i.e., customer credits) and ensuring the expeditious 
return of all or a material portion of those amounts to customers in the form of rate moderators.  
For the reasons set forth below, the application of net regulatory liabilities advanced in the Joint 
Proposal is fair, equitable, and in the public interest. 
 
Central Hudson currently owes customers a sizeable amount of money in the form 
of net regulatory liabilities.  Pursuant to the Joint Proposal, the Signatories agreed that in order to 
achieve moderation: (a) $9.5 million and $21.5 million of regulatory liabilities would be returned 
to electric customers in the form of bill credits during Rate Year 2 and Rate Year 3, respectively; 
and (b) $0.8 million, $3.2 million, and $5.6 million of regulatory liabilities would be returned to 
gas customers in the form of bills credits in Rate Year 1, Rate Year 2, and Rate Year 3, respectively.  
(JP at 9.)  Thus, $40.6 million in regulatory liabilities (i.e., $31.0 million electric plus $9.6 million 
gas) would be returned to customers in the form of rate moderators during the three-year settlement 
term.  Additionally, because there is a negotiated $3.1 million electric revenue requirement

revenue requirement is allocated amongst the utility’s different service classes, rate design refers 
to how rates within a particular class, or subclass, are designed to produce the targeted level of 
revenues for that class or subclass.  Thus, the resolution of large non-residential rate design issues, 
for instance, does not affect other types of customers. 
 
As detailed in the Preliminary Statement hereto, with respect to electric rate design,

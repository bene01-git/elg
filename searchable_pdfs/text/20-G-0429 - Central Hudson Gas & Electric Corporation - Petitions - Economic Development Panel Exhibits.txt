An Overview of the Mid-Hudson Valley 
From the Great Recession to 2030 
The Great Recession technically ended in June 2009, but everyday Americans have continued 
to feel its lingering impact in their personal lives. In the Mid-Hudson Valley, the impact from 
the recession has shined a light on the region's existing economic and demographic fault lines 
as well as those that developed in the aftermath of the financial crisis. The study area's population 
growth is stagnant, and its effects can be seen in macro issues such as empty desks in classrooms, 
to micro issues, like declining registration for Little League or the size of high school football 
teams, from fewer residents to fill the regional workforce and the challenge to our capacity to 
support the Valley's aging population. 
The economy, which is in the midst of the longest expansion ever, is not working for everyone. 
There are fewer well-paying jobs than there were before the recession, and an increase in poorly 
compensated employment that cannot support a family. This makes it more difficult for people 
to afford the high cost of living and housing that has always been a challenge for the Mid-
Hudson Valley. 
In 2018, Central Hudson Gas & Electric Corp. asked Hudson Valley Pattern for Progress to 
explore two simple questions: What was the nature of the Mid-Hudson Valley's recovery from 
the Great Recession, and what do the next 10 years hold. The answers to those questions are 
found in the following pages. The study area for this report included the seven counties of 
Columbia, Dutchess, Greene, Orange, Putnam, Sullivan and Ulster. 
Following is a summary of Part One of the report. The full version of Part One is available 
online at hvoutofalignment.com or by emailing outofalignment@pfprogress.org. Part Two will 
be issued later this year and will include strategies and recommendations to bring the Mid-
Hudson Valley closer to alignment in the coming decade. 
It is essential that we work together to address these issues and develop strategies to modify our 
economy. We must not be afraid to take risks, innovate and look for new regional solutions, to 
improve the quality of life for residents in the Mid-Hudson Valley.

Low or stagnant population growth is evident in the Mid-Hudson Valley. Rural counties 
farthest frorn Nevv York City have seen the sharpest decline while those that are closer have 
been flat or seen minor .increases. Given that population density is often related to economic 
growth, this trend will be a challenge to policymakers as they struggle to retain and attract 
residents, grow the economy and workforce, and deliver and pay for services. 
While six of the seven counties in the study area grew modestly between 2000 and 2010, all 
but Orange County saw a decline in population between 2010 and 2017, the latest year for 
which data is available. Just about half of Orange's 1.4% growth can be attributed to Kiryas Joel, 
a village composed primarily of members of the Hasidic Jewish Satmar community, which 
typically have large farnihes and where the median age is 13.8, far below the County's median 
of36.9. The rural counties of Columbia and Greene shrank by 2.6% and 2.9%, while Dutchess, 
Putnam and Ulster lost 0.6%, 0.2% and 1.3% of their residents respectively. 
This is occurring even as the study area continues to age. In six of the seven counties, residents 
55 and older make up 30% or more of the population. That number is expected to rise to 35% 
by 2030. Every county other than Orange has a median age above 42, with Columbia having 
the highest median age at 47. The national median age is 38. 
The outlook for the next 10 years is even more concerning, especially as the study area's total 
fertility rate - arguably the single most important demographic indicator - fell to 1.76 in 
2016, slightly below the U.S. rate of 1.80, and much below the replacement rate of2.1. In three 
counties, Columbia, Greene and Ulster, deaths outpace births from 2010 to 2018. Population 
projections by Cornell University's Program on Applied Demographics (PAD) show that 
between 2015 and 2030, the Valley will add only 2,403 people, an increase of 0.2%, or an 
annual growth of0.01%. For all practical purposes, that infinitesimal growth is no growth, and 
it has wide-ranging implications for the workforce, the economy and the Valley's wellbeing. 
A flat population growth rate combined with family formation being delayed and younger 
couples choosing to have fewer kids has led to the decline in K-12 population. Of the 65 school 
districts in the study area, only seven have seen an increase in student numbers since 2000. The 
study area has 26,485 fewer students since 2000, meaning it has lost 14.5% of its students. As a 
result, taxpayers are paying more to educate a decreasing number of children. Currently, school 
districts in the study area spend an average of $28,264 on educating each student, higher than 
the state average of $25,611, which itself is the highest in the nation. 
The Mid-Hudson Valley also isn't getting value for its money. In the 2017 / 18 school year, only 
3W% of 8th graders in the study area were considered proficient in English Language Arts (ELA)

In 2014, the last date for which data is available, only 40% the study area's high school graduates

though the study area's pupil-teacher ratio, a popular measure of quality of education a district

Enrollment projections for 2028/29 are even more alarming. The Cornell Program on Applied 
Demographics estimates that the Mid-Hudson Valley will lose an additional 20,571 students by 
2028/29, or 13.2% of enrollment, close to the percentage decline that occurred between 
2000/01 and 2018/19, but in approximately half the time. Columbia, Greene and Ulster 
are projected to lose a third or more of their enrollment if the declines are calculated from the 
base year of 2000/01 to 2028/29. In the study area, Pattern projects spending to increase to 
$5.375 billion, up from $4.4 billion in 2018/19, a 27% increase. Correspondingly, per pupil 
spending for the study area is projected to climb to $39,686, an increase of$11,422, or 40.4%. 
In the 10 years since the Great Recession, unemployment rates in the study area have now 
reached levels that are comparable with those in 2000 and 2007. With such low rates (3.8% at 
the end of2018), wages should rise, but they have not in any meaningful way. Though median 
household incomes appear to have risen in all seven counties between 2010 and 2017, when 
the numbers are adjusted for inflation, they actually show a decrease in every county except 
Greene. A review of data also shows that lower income households have experienced the 
sharpest drop in income while the top 20%, have seen their income rise. The household poverty 
rate in the study area rose from 8.9% in 2010 to 10.2% in 2017. But those numbers tell only 
part of the story. When another measure - households with employed individuals who are 
above the federal poverty level but still unable to make ends meet - is applied, it shows that 
31% of the study area's population is living paycheck to paycheck. 
A contributing factor to this economic hardship lies in the fundamental shift that occurred in 
the study area's employment composition between 2008 and 2017. Higher-paying jobs in 
sectors such as manufacturing, finance, K-12 education and information services, that had 
anchored the middle class in the past, fell. And while more than 12,000 new jobs were created, 
they were mainly in food, administration and waste services sectors, which pay low wages. This 
brings into focus one of the dilemmas facing the Mid-Hudson Valley - the highest paying 
industries are losing jobs while the sectors that are growing pay less than what it costs to live 
here.

The study area's proximity to New York City, one of the most expensive markets to live or do 
business in, has meant that the cost of necessities like housing, food, childcare, transportation 
and healthcare is higher. However, a cost-of-living comparison with another neighboring 
region and mid-sized cities in the South that compete to draw millennials and the general 
vvorkforce shows that it is more expensive for a family of four to live in the Mid-Hudson Valley. 
This disparity can be attributed to higher housing costs, higher taxes, and the cost of childcare, 
which is almost tvvice what it costs in comparative areas. 
Sonic of the reasons for high housing costs is the lack of inventory that is affordable, a mismatch 
between what is available, what is needed, and high property taxes. The Mid-Hudson Va11ey 
has some of the highest "all-in" tax rates in the state and its counties were among the top 10 
with the fastest growing tax rates between 2008-2017. As a result, rents arc simply unaffordable 
for tens of thousands of study area residents. The average monthly gap between what it costs to 
rent and what renters can affi)fd. to pay is $727 per month. The high cost ofli ving is one of the 
reasons why nearly half of the study area's adults 18-34 Jive at home ,vith their parents. 
Pattern's year-long study into the Mid-Hudson Valley has revealed an economy that is seriously 
out of alignn1cnt with its demographics. In another 10 years, we will have 26% fewer children 
in school than we did at the start of the millennium, but we will pay 40% more to educate 
them. The population is aging but we have not adjusted to being an old.er region. The quality 
of jobs we create results in at ]east a third of our population struggling to make ends meet. The 
cost of living contributes to nearly half our young adults living at home with their parents. 
Young working-age people arc leaving the area at the same time that baby boomcrs arc retiring, 
creating a challenge for employers to fi]] available jobs. 
While all of these findings arc concerning, the most troubling is our demographic future. Flat 
growth in our overall population, far fewer children and a grovving aging population seems to 
be our destiny. This will have a far-reaching impact on our economy, education system, 
workforce, health care, housing market, tax base, and government services. Given these trends, 
we nrnst work hard to diversify and strengthen our economy and change how we live so that 
we can secure the future while retaining the sense of place that makes the Mid-Hudson Valley 
such an attractive area to live.

2019: The Best of Times? 
The current economy is often described as possibly the best the U.S. has ever seen. Even 
accounting for hyperbole, we are, indeed, in a period ofremarkable growth. The U.S. economy 
has been expanding for more than a 100 consecutive months and most economic indicators are 
robust. But to a large section of the U.S. population, it doesn't feel so. 
At the turn of the millennium, the unemployment rate was approximately where it is today, 
and people felt confident about the economy. The U.S. had a balanced budget; indeed, even a 
surplus. The middle class did not feel pinched. Many workers had generous benefits, including 
employer-paid healthcare. High deductible health plans were virtually unknown as was the gig 
economy. Most middle class workers received an annual raise and expected to retire on a 
pension. 
In July 2019, even though the national unemployment rate stood at 3.7%, wage growth has 
been anemic. Employee benefits have been slowly eroding. People are making larger 
contributions to their employer-provided health care. Many workers, especially younger 
people, work multiple jobs with few benefits. A majority of Americans are not saving and cannot 
afford to retire. The country is $22 trillion in debt. Student loan debt has grown from $640 
billion in 2008 to slightly over $1.5 trillion in 2019. 2 Today, the top 1% of Americans own 
more wealth than the bottom 90%. To many Americans, including many in the Mid-Hudson 
Valley, the economy may look good on paper, but they do not personally benefit from that 
growth. 
In a hearing before Congress in July 2019, Federal Reserve Chairman Jerome Powell pushed 
back on the widespread notion that the job market was hot. 3 The 3% wage and benefit growth 
being seen, he said, barely covered productivity increases and inflation. In July 2019, the Fed 
cut interest rates by .25% in response, and followed up with a similar cut in September. It may 
consider further rate cuts in the coming months. 4 
An interest rate cut is typically utilized by the Federal Reserve to spur economic growth. It is 
not clear why the central bank would take this action if the economy is so robust. If it cuts rates

In the 1960s, New York was the most populous state 
in the nation. By 2018, it had dropped to fourth place 
and earned the dubious distinction of being the fourth slowest growing state since 1960. It grew 
more than West Virginia but not as much as North Dakota. 5 One measure of the slowing 
growth is that New York had 45 congressional districts; it now has only 27 and is projected to 
lose one or two more after the 2020 Census. 6 
Declining population in the Mid-Hudson Valley has been felt after the Great Recession. 
Between 2000 and 2010, none of the seven counties in the study area experienced a drop in 
population. Though Columbia's population in the first decade of the millennium remained 
mostly unchanged, all other counties saw their population grow by between 2.1 % and 9.2%. 
Orange County grew the most in that decade, with its 9.2% population increase fueled by 
families fleeing high rent areas in New York City, Westchester and northern New Jersey for 
more affordable homes in the exurbs. The dramatic growth of the ultra-Orthodox Hasidic 
community also contributed, with the Village of Kiryas Joel, where virtually everyone is a 
member of the Satmar Hasidic sect, accounting for almost 25% of the growth in Orange County 
between 2000 and 2010 while being only 5% of the County's population in 2010. In that 
period, Dutchess County grew by 6.2%, and Sullivan and Putnam by 4.8% and 4.1 % 
respectively. 
From 2010 to 2017 Columbia and Greene counties witnessed the largest percentage population 
decline of 2.6% and 2.9% respectively. Sullivan lost 2.3% of its population while Ulster lost 
1.3%, and Dutchess and Putnam contracted by 0.6% and 0.2% respectively. Orange, which had 
experienced the largest percentage growth in the previous decade, saw its population increase 
by a modest 1.4%. New York State's population growth for the same period was 2.4%, which 
can be contributed to the growth of New York City.

The Way Forward 
Given the myriad of complex challenges, any discussion of where the Mid-Hudson Valley is 
headed as a region cannot move forward without acknowledging the increased interplay of 
economic and social issues that influence the competing ways different stakeholders wish the 
region to grow. 
For example, in the Village of Warwick, crowds are so great that parking cannot be found 
during apple picking season, and traffic and noise has grown to the point that some residents 
want to limit the inflow of people. Yet school enrollment tells another story about the Village's 
growth. The Village appears full, but the school population continues to drop. 
The cities of Newburgh and Poughkeepsie share similarities. Concern about crime - real and 
perceived - combines with the narrative that the cities are "coming back." Yet poverty and 
vacant buildings remain. Fifty years ago, a Pattern report pushed for these and other cities to be 
developed as centers of the Mid-Hudson Valley. It did not happen. The dialogue today must 
change from "the potential for revitalization" to "they are being fully revitalized." 
While Westchester is not part of the study area, it is an example of how a county has grown, 
albeit at a slow pace. It grows despite its property taxes being among the highest in the country. 
Part of the reason is its proximity to New York City and the availability of mass transit. Many 
school districts in the county are considered excellent, so it draws wealthy new residents who 
view the taxes as "tuition" or the price for quality schooling. While each county in the study 
area can point to maybe one of two school districts that are truly quality, approximately 15 in 
Westchester are among the state's highest ranked. 
In Part 2 of this study the funders challenged Pattern to plan for a better future without relying 
on policy changes from either New York State or the federal government. Longstanding 
complaints such as "home rule" which limit regional planning or state mandates that cause 
counties to bear the full burden of development are likely to continue and are beyond the scope 
of the study area to implement. This limitation is important in understanding that Pattern is 
bound to seek local solutions. As part of encouraging a new dialogue, we will seek to engage 
stakeholders throughout the Valley to help in the formation of strategies and recommendations 
on how to achieve a better future. While we do not envision a single silver bullet to bring the 
region into alignment we are optimistic that by working together we can make the next decade 
one which can assist the lives of all residents throughout the Mid-Hudson Valley.

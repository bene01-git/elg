I.
Introduction
In this post-hearing brief, the environmental and community parties urge the Public
Service Commission (“Commission”) to reject the Joint Proposal filed in the above referenced
proceeding by National Grid’s downstate companies “KEDNY” and “KEDLI” (“the
Companies”), the Department of Public Service Staff (“Staff”), and other parties. The Joint
Proposal violates Section 7 of the Climate Leadership and Community Protection Act
(“CLCPA”) and therefore does not meet the standard of review for settlements in rate cases. The
Commission is obligated to fully comply with the CLCPA in this rate proceeding and in all rate
proceedings when approving new gas infrastructure projects, no matter the purported
justification for the projects.
Sections 7(1) and 7(2) of the CLCPA state:
All state agencies shall assess and implement strategies to reduce their greenhouse gas
emissions. In considering and issuing permits, licenses, and other administrative
approvals and decisions, including but not limited to the execution of grants, loans, and
contracts, all state agencies, offices, authorities, and divisions shall consider whether such
decisions are inconsistent with or will interfere with the attainment of the statewide
greenhouse gas emissions limits established in article 75 of the environmental
conservation law. Where such decisions are deemed to be inconsistent with or will
interfere with the attainment of the statewide greenhouse gas emissions limits, each
agency, office, authority, or division shall provide a detailed statement of justification as
to why such limits/criteria may not be met, and identify alternatives or green-house gas
mitigation measures to be required where such project is located.
Section 7(3) of the CLCPA states:
In considering and issuing permits, licenses, and other administrative approvals and
decisions, including but not limited to the execution of grants, loans, and contracts,
pursuant to article 75 of the environmental conservation law, all state agencies, offices,
authorities, and divisions shall not disproportionately burden disadvantaged communities
as identified pursuant to subdivision 5 of section 75-0101 of the environmental
conservation law. All state agencies, offices, authorities, and divisions shall also prioritize
reductions of greenhouse gas emissions and co-pollutants in disadvantaged communities
as identified pursuant to such subdivision 5 of section 75-0101 of the environmental
conservation law.

II.
DPS Staff’s claim that the Commission’s adoption of a rate order “may not fall
within the actions identified by CLCPA §§7(2) and 7(3)” has no basis and is clearly
inconsistent with the plain language of the statute.
DPS Staff attempted to distinguish between “merely set[ting] rates” and issuing permits
for construction and operation of projects, stating that perhaps Section 7 of the CLCPA does not
apply to rate cases. The Commission must reject this interpretation of the CLCPA, which has no
basis.
First, Section 7 explicitly places its requirements on “all state agencies, offices,
authorities, and divisions” (emphasis added). The Department of Public Service (“DPS”) and the
Commission are state agencies.
Second, Section 7 is intended to be broad and expansive in the scope of decisions it
covers, stating: “In considering and issuing permits, licenses, and other administrative approvals
and decisions, including but not limited to the execution of grants, loans, and contracts...”
(emphasis added). Rate recovery approval is an administrative approval. The use of the phrases
“and other” as well as “including but not limited to” indicate an intention to cover any kind of
decision, and the use of “grants, loans, and contracts” as examples indicates an intention to
include decisions—like rate recovery approval—that involve money.
Third, Staff’s attempted distinction between rate recovery approval and project approval
is a false one. In many instances, DPS is the only state agency to review a project and therefore
rate recovery approval or denial is tantamount to project approval or denial. For the Metropolitan
Reliability Infrastructure (MRI) Pipeline, for example, DPS was the only state agency that
reviewed the project.1 In initial sanction papers for the MRI Pipeline, the Companies noted under
“key issues” that “[t]his is a major capital project that will undoubtedly receive significant
scrutiny from the NY PSC, and as such a strategy for rate recovery is required.”2 When DPS staff
questioned if Phase 5 of the MRI Pipeline was a prudent investment and recommended that “the
Commission require KEDNY to pause work,”3 National Grid paused construction. Furthermore,
the Independent Monitor in Case 19-G-0678 stated in his first quarterly report that prospects for
the completion of construction of the MRI Pipeline “remain outstanding” since the project was
still under review in this rate case, indicating that the Commission’s authority to approve rate
recovery has clear implications for the construction and operation of projects. Indeed,

III.
DPS Staff have not fulfilled their legal obligations under the CLCPA.
Having established that CLCPA Section 7 clearly applies to rate case approvals, we now
turn to the question of whether or not DPS Staff have fulfilled their obligations under CLCPA
Section 7. Specifically, the Commission is required to find that a decision adopting the Joint
Proposal (1) would not interfere with the attainment of required greenhouse gas emissions limits;
and (2) would not disproportionately burden disadvantaged communities. The Joint Proposal
contains no such finding, and therefore the Commission has no basis upon which to make such a
finding.

evidentiary hearing, Staff made no effort to analyze the greenhouse gas emissions increases
associated with the Companies’ proposed infrastructure5 or growth in gas sales. Therefore, Staff
cannot make a finding either way as to whether a decision to approve the Joint Proposal would or

But all of this is beside the point, because Section 7(3) of the CLCPA unequivocally

That is, the plain language of Section 7 of the CLCPA makes clear that all state agencies,
including the Commission, have affirmative obligations to (1) ensure that disadvantaged
communities are not disproportionately burdened and (2) prioritize disadvantaged communities
for reductions of emissions and pollutants. Staff, by their own admission, made no attempt to
fulfill either of these obligations. The law assigns these obligations to state agencies, not to
intervening parties; thus, Staff’s (incorrect) belief that “there is no evidence [of disproportionate
burden] in these cases” does not relieve Staff of their independent obligation to thoroughly assess
potential impacts on disadvantaged communities.

C. Neither Staff nor the Companies have considered the full lifecycle emissions of the
Companies’ proposed gas infrastructure, as required by the CLCPA.

The CLCPA requires a comprehensive, economy-wide evaluation of greenhouse gas
emissions, including combustion-related emissions,13 and there are metrics to quantify those
emissions.

B. Staff’s narrow conception of “safe” service is inconsistent with the CLCPA’s conception
of “safety,” which includes the climate change, pollution, and other health impacts of
greenhouse gas emissions.
In testimony elicited on cross-examination at the July 21, 2021 evidentiary hearing, DPS
Staff were unwilling or unable to elaborate on the factors that Staff consider when determining a
utility company’s compliance with its obligation to provide “safe” service, suggesting that Staff
are continuing to use a very narrow definition of “safe” that fails to include any potential safety
and public health impacts to disadvantaged communities from emissions of fossil fuel projects
such as the Greenpoint LNG Vaporizers and MRI Pipeline. As Sane Energy Project16 and other
parties in this case have argued, the Commission must reassess the meaning of “safe and
reliable,” including by considering the implications of the ongoing and increasingly urgent
climate crisis, which was not in consideration when the Public Service Law was written. The
CLCPA also includes discussion of safety, stating that “[a]ctions undertaken by New York state
to mitigate greenhouse gas emissions should prioritize the safety and health of disadvantaged
communities” (emphasis added). This conception of safety—one that includes risks from climate
change, pollution, and other impacts of fracked gas infrastructure—must be considered by DPS
Staff and the Commission when evaluating the Companies’ compliance with its obligation to
provide safe and reliable service.
In this rate case,17 as well as in other proceedings before the Commission, Staff has stated
that increased gas use will be necessary for record cold days, specifically referencing the winter
storms that crippled the Texas energy system this year. Staff’s argument is only valid if the
climate impacts of extracting, transporting, delivering, and combusting fracked gas are ignored.
UN weather experts confirmed the extreme cold in Texas was caused by a polar vortex resulting
from warmer temperatures in the Arctic caused by global warming.18 Staff are essentially arguing
that rate recovery should be approved for more fracked gas projects without acknowledging the
climate impacts of these projects, which cause increased occurrences of precisely the extreme
weather events that Staff cite to justify approval of the projects.

C. Staff’s suggestion that only DEC is responsible for examining greenhouse gas emissions
for the Companies’ proposed infrastructure has no basis.
In testimony elicited on cross-examination during the June 21, 2021 evidentiary hearing,
DPS Staff testified not only that they have done no analysis of greenhouse gas emissions for any
project in the Joint Proposal, but also that “it is DEC’s duty to look at greenhouse gas emissions
for permitting.”19 As noted above, the CLCPA clearly imposes its mandates upon “all state
agencies,” including the Commission.
Furthermore, only a handful of projects in the Joint Proposal undergo DEC review for
permits, leaving the vast majority of the capital investment in fossil fuel projects unassessed for
compliance with the CLCPA if DPS fails to undertake this assessment.
DPS is arguably the agency best in position to assess compliance with the CLCPA
because rate cases show the entirety of utility plans. DEC often receives applications for projects
that have been illegally segmented from other projects, and these applications do not include in
their scope the full lifecycle greenhouse gas emissions from the gas delivered by utilities.
V. The CLCPA took effect on January 1, 2020, the Commission has an obligation to
implement the CLCPA based on the plain language of the statute, and other state agencies
have already been applying the CLCPA in their determinations.
The Department of Public Service and the Commission must apply the plain language of
the CLCPA to this rate case. Any interpretation of the CLCPA that suggests that these state
agencies must await further guidance from the Climate Action Council, Climate Justice Working
Group, or others before proceeding with implementation would thwart the clear intent of the
New York State Assembly and is not suggested by the plain language of the statute. The legal
effectiveness of the CLCPA was not made subject to implementing regulations, and the plain
language of the CLCPA indicates the legislature’s acute awareness of the increasing urgency of
the climate crisis and an intent to take immediate measures to curb the crisis. (See, e.g.,
Schneider v. Navient Sols., LLC, No. 16-CV-6760 CJS, at *5 (W.D.N.Y. June 7, 2018)
(“Although [the statute in question] gave [the relevant agency] nine months to prescribe
implementing regulations, such fact does not mean the statute did not become effective as soon
as it was signed by the President”).

Project as it is currently envisioned is inconsistent with the energy and climate
policies, laws, and goals of the State (emphasis added).
The Commission should take particular note of DEC’s determination on NESE because it
sets a clear example of how another state agency has applied the CLCPA to a proposal similar to
those at issue in this case. In fact, DEC’s findings on NESE could be applied nearly verbatim to
the Long Term Capacity Capital Projects at issue in this rate case, as those projects are meant to
deliver the exact same fuel to the same region.

There are substantial greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions which are currently associated
with the existing and proposed uses. Based on the information currently available, it
appears that the proposed project would be inconsistent with or would interfere
with the attainment of the Statewide GHG emission limits established in the Climate

Pursuant to Section 7(2) of the Climate Act, if an agency's decision would be
inconsistent with or would interfere with the Statewide GHG emission limits, then
the agency must also: (1) provide a detailed statement of justification of the project
notwithstanding inconsistency with the Climate Act; and (2) identify alternatives or
GHG mitigation measures to be required. Based on the information provided to
date, the Department is not currently able to satisfy either of these two elements
with respect to the proposed project absent additional information. (Emphasis
added).
Thus, other state agencies have already proceeded with applying Section 7 of the CLCPA
to their determinations, as the statute requires. The Commission must now follow suit and fulfill
its obligations.
VI. Conclusion
For all of the aforementioned reasons, we urge the Commission to reject the Joint
Proposal filed in this proceeding. The Commission should reject DPS Staff’s argument that
Section 7 of the CLCPA “may” not apply to this proceeding, as the plain language of the statute
makes clear that all decisions of all state agencies are subject to the CLCPA. The Commission
should also find that DPS Staff have not fulfilled two of their key obligations under the CLCPA,
failing to either (1) assess greenhouse gas emissions increases associated with the projects
contained in the Joint Proposal; or (2) assess the projects’ potential disproportionate burdens on
disadvantaged communities. Without these assessments, the Commission is in no position to
determine whether or not the Joint Proposal is CLCPA-compliant. Finally, the Commission
should reject DPS Staff’s suggestion that the Public Service Law and the CLCPA are somehow
fundamentally incompatible with one another; indeed, the Commission is obligated to meet the
requirements of both laws, and the Companies have already advanced a viable option (the
no-infrastructure option) that would ensure safe and reliable service as well as compliance with
New York State’s landmark climate legislation.

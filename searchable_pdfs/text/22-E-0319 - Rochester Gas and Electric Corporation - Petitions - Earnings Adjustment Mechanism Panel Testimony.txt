II. OVERVIEW OF TESTIMONY 
1 
Q. 
Please provide an overview of the Panel’s testimony. 
2 
A. 
Earnings Adjustment Mechanisms (“EAMs”) establish metrics and targets with financial 
3 
rewards that are designed to align utility interests with New York State energy policy 
4 
goals, such as the Climate Leadership and Community Protection Act (“CLCPA”), and 
5 
thus are a powerful tool in the fight against climate change.  
6 
EAMs encourage the utilities to greatly promote and expand their focus in areas 
7 
such as energy efficiency, beneficial electrification (“BE”), distributed energy resource 
8 
(“DER”) utilization, and load reduction, all to the benefit of customers and society as a 
9 
whole.  The Companies’ existing EAMs resulted in increased electric vehicle (“EV”) and 
10 
heat pump uptake in our service territories, along with increased distributed generation 
11 
(“DG”) solar interconnection and progress toward energy efficiency goals.    
12 
We highlight lessons learned from the design and implementation of our current 
13 
EAMs.  Namely, that we were most successful in meeting EAM targets where we had 
14 
direct control or substantial input and control into the achievement of EAM targets.   
15 
We propose 14 EAMs for the Rate Year (consisting of both new EAMs and 
16 
modifications to certain existing EAMs) that reflect our learnings and are consistent with 
17 
EAMs recently approved for other New York utilities.  The proposed EAMs are 
18 
categorized into four sub-groups: Energy Efficiency, DER Utilization, Peak Load 
19 
Reduction, and BE, with a focus on environmental justice and low- and moderate-income 
20 
customers where applicable.

IV. PURPOSE AND DESIGN OF EAMS 
13 
Q. 
Before addressing the Companies’ current EAMs, please discuss the general purpose and 
14 
design of an EAM. 
15 
A. 
An EAM is an incremental performance incentive mechanism that allows Companies to 
16 
earn an incentive in return for achieving certain beneficial objectives.  EAMs establish 
17 
metrics and targets with financial rewards that are designed to align utility interests with 
18 
New York State energy policy goals, including but not limited to the goals set forth in the 
19 
CLCPA.    
20 
The overarching goal of EAMs is to provide the Companies with an additional 
21 
incentive to support important areas, such as energy efficiency, BE, DER utilization, and 
22 
load reduction, which directly benefit our customers and society as a whole.  The 
23

incentive is in the form of positive revenue adjustments (“PRAs”).  At their most 
1 
fundamental level, EAMs are designed to provide an incentive for utilities to focus efforts 
2 
and incremental spending in areas that further regulatory or legislative initiatives where 
3 
timeliness and success are critical, like initiatives related to the CLCPA.     
4 
Q. 
Please explain how EAMs should be designed. 
5 
A. 
EAMs should be designed to align utility interests with a particular goal (e.g., energy 
6 
efficiency savings, DER MWH connected).  To promote progress towards the identified 
7 
goal, EAM targets should be set that are achievable, but require incremental utility effort 
8 
to attain.  Upon achieving these targets – and working towards these goals – the EAM 
9 
should provide a financial incentive that reflects the effort and funds expended in order to 
10 
achieve the target.  
11 
Q. 
Why are these design elements important to proper functioning of EAMs? 
12 
A. 
Properly designed EAMs incent the Companies to direct their energies and funds in a 
13 
certain direction that will help achieve identified goals and deliver customer benefits, 
14 
such as success with meeting the goals of the CLCPA.  To function properly, the targets 
15 
should be achievable.  While they should not be easy to achieve, setting EAM targets at a 
16 
level that is unattainable or extremely time- or cost-intensive to achieve would make it 
17 
unlikely for a utility to devote significant incremental resources or effort towards 
18 
achieving the EAM.  The target level and financial incentive must consider the level of 
19 
effort and costs that a utility must expend in reaching a target.  Maximum targets may be 
20 
set as an aspirational or “stretch” goal, but ultimately targets must be achievable for 
21 
EAMs to provide proper incentives for utility action.

Q. 
Have the Companies identified any other important factors for successful EAMs? 
1 
A. 
Yes.  EAMs should be designed such that the Companies have direct control, or 
2 
substantial control, over the outcome sought to be achieved and are not overly dependent 
3 
on circumstances or actions of parties outside of the Companies’ control. 
4 
Q. 
Would the Panel please provide some examples illustrating why EAMs should be 
5 
designed in such a way that the Companies have substantial control? 
6 
A. 
Yes.  An example of a program where the Companies have substantial control is in the 
7 
Energy Efficiency area.  The Electric Share the Savings (“ESTS”), Gas Share the Savings 
8 
(“GSTS”), and Heat Pump Share the Savings (“HPSTS”) programs are driven by the 
9 
Companies’ energy efficiency spending and programs.  There is a strong relationship 
10 
between how much the Companies spend on these programs and the market penetration 
11 
of energy efficiency products into the customer population.  There is also a correlation, 
12 
though to a lesser extent, to the amount of effort in the Coordinated Electric System 
13 
Interconnection Review (“CESIR”) process and the ability to connect DERs as part of the 
14 
DER Utilization EAM.  Conversely, the Companies do not have much direct control over 
15 
the electric and gas coincident peaks since only some of the energy efficiency programs 
16 
are designed to reduce peak load and there is not a significant amount of direct load 
17 
control on individual customers.  Additionally, the BE goal as designed in the last Rate 
18 
Plan is highly dependent on electric vehicle sales incentives that were offered by the New 
19 
York State Energy Research and Development Authority (“NYSERDA”).  Electric 
20 
vehicle sales are also highly dependent on exogenous factors out of the Companies’ 
21 
control, as demonstrated by decreased electric vehicle sales resulting from computer chip

Q. 
Are there instances where it is acceptable to have an EAM where the Companies do not

4 
A. 
Yes.  EAMs may still be appropriate where utilities do not have direct or substantial 
5

11 
demand, and thus on CO2 reduction.

Q. 
Why is system load factor a better measure than peak load reduction? 
1 
A. 
Load factors are a ratio of annual load to the system peak load.  Specifically, Load Factor 
2 
= (Annual System Load / (System Peak Load x 8760 hours).  The closer the Load Factor 
3 
approaches 100% (or a ratio of 1.0), then the system peak does not deviate much from the 
4 
overall average load.  A high load factor has tremendous benefits for the electric system – 
5 
it benefits both the supply of electricity and the transmission and distribution systems.  A 
6 
high load factor will put less stress on the system since the peak will not be much 
7 
different than “normal” or “typical” operations.  As a result, the transmission and 
8 
distribution systems will not have to be sized for large swings in loads.  Likewise, the 
9 
supply of electricity will not require as much peaking load, which will help to reduce 
10 
electric supply costs to customers.  Further, some of the BE, such as electric vehicles, can 
11 
be encouraged to charge during off-peak hours and thus fill in the load “valleys” to raise 
12 
the overall system load factor.    
13

14 
A. 
The Companies currently measure the hourly loads with the NYISO.  This data will be 
15

16 
Q. 
How does the System Load Factor EAM benefit customers? 
17 
A. 
By increasing the system load factor, distribution system costs can be moderated.  
18 
Distribution systems are sized to handle peak demands, and if the load factor is high, then 
19 
the cost to size the system in excess of the average goes down.  In essence, the benefit is 
20 
long-run cost containment.  From a supply perspective, electric supply costs during peak 
21 
periods can be significantly higher than the average price.  A higher load factor will

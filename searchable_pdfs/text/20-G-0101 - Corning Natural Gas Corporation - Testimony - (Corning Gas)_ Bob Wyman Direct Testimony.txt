Q. Does acceleration of cost-recovery conform to established principles of utility cost
9
allocation?
10
A. It is a well established principle of utility cost allocation that the asset’s costs should
11
be paid by those who beneﬁt from the use of those assets. In this case, if cost recovery
12
for the Company’s ﬁxed assets is not accelerated, costs will be shifted from customers
13
who either reduce or eliminate their gas use to an increasingly dwindling number of
14
unfortunate customers who remain reliant on the Company’s gas service. If we do not
15
reduce the ASLs which drive the rate of cost recovery, we will ﬁnd that gas customers
16
who cannot or have not eliminated their use of gas will be required to pay the costs of
17
gas assets they are no longer using. This would be inequitable and would not conform
18
to accepted principles for utlity cost allocation.
19
Q. Explain why ﬁxed costs are such a problem when sales decline.
20
A. If sales decline but ﬁxed costs remain ﬁxed, then the ﬁxed costs will be recovered
21
from a smaller number of units sold. When sales decline, the per-unit cost recovery
22
of ﬁxed costs must increase.

For example, if gas consumption were to decline by 80% between now and 2050, then
1
the per-unit cost recovery for an asset installed or in use today but still in use in 2050
2
would be 500% greater than it is today.10 This is because, in 2050, the ﬁxed costs
3
would be spread over just 20% of the number of units sold in today. If the decline in
4
sales by 2050 is only 50%, then the per-unit cost would increase by a still substantial
5
200%.
6
Q. What is the likely result of the per-unit cost increases that will be driven by reduced
7
gas sales?
8
A. The result will be a gas “death-spiral.”
9
As gas’ per-unit allocation of ﬁxed costs increases, alternatives to gas will become
10
more competitive and those ratepayers who have the capital or credit needed to in-
11
vest in alternatives to gas, such as heat pumps, will abandon gas and ﬁnd shelter
12
with the cheaper alternatives. However, by abandoning gas, they will drive gas sales
13
even lower and that will increase the per-unit costs of gas to the point where even
14
more ratepayers decide to abandon gas. The cycle then repeats. As the per-unit costs
15
increase, gas sales will decrease even faster, forcing the per-unit costs increases to
16
accelerate. This is referred to as a “death-spiral.”
17
In time, we will ﬁnd that gas will become known as the “poor-man’s fuel” since the
18
remaining gas users will increasingly be those who can’t afford to abandon it — no
19
matter how much they might wish to. Low and moderate income ratepayers, who
20
have the most limited ﬁnancial resources and the greatest need for more affordable
21
energy, will end up carrying the burden of the stranded gas assets left behind by those
22
who are ﬁnancially more fortunate.

Q. If cost recovery is accelerated, won’t this create windfall proﬁts for the Company’s
1
Shareholders?
2
A. No. Accelerated cost recovery does not create a proﬁt for shareholders. It simply
3
converts the shareholders’ share of asset values into an equivalent share of more liq-
4
uid capital. If anything, one might argue that shareholders would prefer that cost-
5
recovery not be accelerated since doing so reduces the amount of equity which is
6
subject to the Company’s allowed return on equity. However, failing to accelerate
7
cost-recovery will result in stranded assets and, if the magnitude of stranded assets
8
grows too great, there may be efforts to limit the Company’s ability to fully recover
9
the costs of stranded assets. Thus, shareholders are better off getting their capital
10
returned to them in a form that allows it to be reinvested in non-gas assets that are
11
more likely to deliver acceptable returns over time.
12
Q. Is it fair to existing customers to accelerate cost-recovery now and thus increase their
13
rates?
14
A. Yes. In fact, many gas customers should be pleased to see this increase since the
15
proposed increase is less than the increase that would be required if delayed to a
16
future year.
17
It is inevitable that the Company will be required to accelerate cost-recovery to avoid
18
the accumulation of stranded assets. The cost increases must eventually be imposed
19
– the question is ”when,” not “if.” However, the longer the delay before those in-
20
creases are imposed, the greater those increases must be since there will be less time
21
to accomplish the required cost recovery. Thus, by accelerating cost recovery now,
22
sooner rather than later, the impact on customers is reduced.
23

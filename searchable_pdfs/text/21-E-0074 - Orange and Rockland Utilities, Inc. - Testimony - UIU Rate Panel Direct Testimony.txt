Background  
16 
Q. 
Would you provide a few brief introductory comments concerning ECOS studies? 
17 
A. 
An ECOS study is often used as a tool in utility rate proceedings to apportion rate 
18 
base and operating expenses among the various customer classes.  This data is 
19 
used in conjunction with existing revenues to estimate the overall system ROR and 
20 
ROR earned from each service classification as defined in the study.  Sometimes, 
21 


--- Page 11 ---
CASE 21-E-0074 and 21-G-0073  
 
 
             DIRECT TESTIMONY OF  
 
   
UIU RATE PANEL 
10 
the focus is on historical revenues; other times forecasted costs and revenue 
1 
increases for the Rate Year (pro-forma) are used to estimate the overall system 
2 
ROR.  And sometimes the RORs from both the pro-forma and historical ECOS are 
3 
considered.  Regardless of the specific approach that is used, the analyst must 
4 
exercise discretion and judgement in apportioning costs. For example, the National 
5 
Association of Regulatory Utility Commissioners Gas Distribution Rate Design 
6 
Manual, published in June 1989, (the NARUC Gas Manual) discusses the general 
7 
judgements that can be used in an ECOS model.  That manual states on its page 
8 
twenty that “[t]here is no scientifically correct method of making necessary 
9 
allocations.  A certain amount of judgments must be used in any cost of service 
10 
study.  Consequently, cost allocation studies should only be utilized as a general 
11 
guide or a starting point for revenue allocation and rate design.”  In New York, 
12 
ECOS study results are typically used as an initial guide to assign costs and 
13 
apportion the revenue increase to the various customer classes.  Below, we will 
14 
briefly discuss some of our concerns with respect to the ECOS studies provided 
15 
by the Company. The first issue we discuss is the Company’s proposed allocation 
16 
methodology for AMI costs.

2 
Q. 
Can you briefly explain the revenue allocation process in a typical utility rate case? 
3 
A. 
Yes.  The results of one or more ECOS studies are used to help guide decisions 
4 
regarding how proposed rate changes should be spread across the various service 
5 
classifications.  The specific process can vary, but ECOS results are generally 
6 
examined to see whether certain classes have been identified as having a high 
7 
ROR – meaning they are paying more than the costs that have been allocated to 
8 
them (sometimes referred to as “overearning”).  If a high ROR is identified in the 
9 
ECOS study (or studies), an effort is typically made to assign a somewhat smaller 
10 
share of the rate increase or revenue requirement to that service classification, 
11 
thereby moving in the direction of greater uniformity in the service classification 
12 
RORs.  Similarly, if certain classes are identified as having a low ROR (paying less 
13 
than the costs that have been allocated to them, which is sometimes described as 
14 
“underearning”), a somewhat greater than average share of the rate increase or 
15 
revenue burden may be assigned to that class during the revenue allocation step.  
16 
Service classifications with a ROR that is close to the overall system average will 
17 
typically receive a very similar, or identical, percentage increase.66 
18

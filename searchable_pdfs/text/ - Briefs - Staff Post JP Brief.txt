STAFF POST-HEARING BRIEF 
 
I. 
INTRODUCTION 
 
 
In a July 6 ruling,1 Administrative Law Judges (ALJs) Maureen Leary and James 
Costello authorized parties to file post-hearing briefs regarding two narrow and limited issues 
concerning the Climate Leadership and Community Protection Act (CLCPA),2 in the above-
captioned cases regarding The Brooklyn Union Gas Company d/b/a National Grid NY (KEDNY) 
and KeySpan Gas East Corporation d/b/a National Grid (KEDLI) (collectively, the Companies).  
In this Post-Hearing Brief, Staff will address the applicability of the CLCPA, specifically, as to 
whether (1) the Public Service Commission (Commission) is required to make a finding under 
section 7 of the CLCPA that adoption of the Joint Proposal will not interfere with the attainment 
of required Greenhouse Gas (GHG) emission limits and/or goals requiring the reduction thereof, 
and will not disproportionately burden disadvantaged communities; and (2) whether the 
Commission must consider the CLCPA in a rate proceeding when approving new gas 
infrastructure projects to address reliability requirements. 
First, based on the language of the statute, Staff believes that §7(2) of the CLCPA 
can be read as being inapplicable to the Commission’s exercise of its ratemaking authority.

Second, Staff also believes that CLCPA §7(3) can be interpreted as being 
inapplicable to the Commission’s exercise of its ratemaking authority.  CLCPA §7(3) shares a 
similar statutory construction to §7(2), thus the same analysis applies.  In addition, the Climate 
Action Council (CAC) has yet to finalize criteria to define disadvantaged communities; thus, it is 
unclear what analysis would be required at this time under CLCPA §7(3), assuming it does apply 
to the Commission’s exercise of its ratemaking authority.  Moreover, assuming CLCPA §7(3) 
applies to the Commission’s review of the Joint Proposal, a review of the record in these 
proceedings supports a finding that the Commission’s adoption of the terms of the Joint Proposal 
would not disproportionally burden disadvantaged communities.  Finally, the record in this case 
demonstrates that the Joint Proposal seeks to mitigate or avoid potential burdensome impacts by 
prioritizing energy efficiency and demand response efforts. 
Third, Staff notes that the effective date of the CLCPA post-dates the filing of the 
instant cases, and the vast majority of the record developed for administrative litigation purposes 
in these proceedings.  Therefore, even if CLCPA §§7(2) and (3) apply to rate cases, the analysis 
required by the statute could not have been considered in the instant rate cases.  The effective 
date of the CLCPA after the filing of the vast majority of pre-filed testimony by the Companies, 
Staff, and intervenors, and the on-going nature of the CAC’s efforts to issue recommendations 
and guidance militate against applying CLCPA §§7(2) and (3) to these proceedings, if they apply 
to the Commission’s exercise of its ratemaking authority in general.

III. 
ARGUMENT 
A. Whether or not Section 7(2) of the CLCPA Applies to the Commission’s Exercise of 
its Ratemaking Authority 
Section 7(2) of the CLCPA focus on permitting, licensing, or financing of 
particular projects.  Accordingly, it appears that this provision does not apply to the 
Commission’s exercise of its general ratemaking authority under the PSL §66(12).  Section 7(2) 
of the CLCPA states: 
In considering and issuing permits, licenses, and other administrative 
approvals and decisions, including but not limited to the execution of grants, 
loans, and contracts, all state agencies, officers, authorities, and divisions, 
shall consider whether such decisions are inconsistent with or will interfere 
with the attainment of the statewide greenhouse gas emissions limits 
established in article 75 of the environmental conservation law. Where such 
decisions are deemed to be inconsistent with or will interfere with the 
attainment of the statewide greenhouse gas emissions limits, each agency, 
office, authority, or division shall provide a detailed statement of 
justification as to why such limits/criteria may not be met, and identify 
alternatives or greenhouse gas mitigation measures to be required where 
such project is located.  (Emphasis added).

As a threshold issue, the Legislature specifically did not use the word “all” to 
apply to the terms “permits, licenses, and other administrative approvals and decisions, including 
but not limited to the execution of grants, loans, and contracts.”  Instead, the Legislature 
provided a list of non-exclusive approvals and decisions subject to the required analysis; i.e. 
permits, licenses, and execution of grants, loans, and contracts.  None of these examples relate to 
the Commission’s authority to approve rates.  This interpretation is supported by the last 
sentence of CLCPA §7(2), which ties the mitigation measures to the location of “such project.”  
The legislature’s use of the term “project” demonstrates its intent to apply the requirements of 
§7(2) pertain to the permitting, licensing, approval and/or execution of grants, loans, and 
contracts associated with specific projects.  Again, rate cases do not involve any of these types of 
agency actions. 
To be clear, the Commission, through its ratemaking authority, adopts an 
appropriate level of rates to be recovered from a utility’s customers to fund the provision of safe, 
adequate, and reliable service.  As clearly explained during the June 21 evidentiary hearing, the 
Joint Proposal does not authorize construction or “the execution of grants, loans, and contracts” 
for any specific project or projects.  Instead, the Joint Proposal provides a structure for rate 
recovery.9  Should a project fail to receive the necessary permitting by another agency, such as 
NYSDEC or the New York City Department of Environmental Protection (NYCDEP), the 
projects would not be built and cost recovery for the construction of those projects would not 
occur.10  Moreover, it would be redundant to understand CLCPA §7(2) to require both NYSDEC 
and the Commission to separately conduct the same analysis of GHG emissions, which would 
result if the Commission adopted the positions of the Movants with regard to capital expenditure 
projects such as Vaporizers 13 and 14 at the Greenpoint liquified natural gas (LNG) facility. 
The Sponsor’s Memo for S.6599 further supports this interpretation of CLCPA 
§7(2).  Specifically, the Sponsor’s Memo notes that CLCPA §§7 through 12 “provide for 
additional authority for state agencies to promulgate greenhouse gas regulations and require the 
Department of Environmental Conservation to consider climate change in permitting

B. Whether or not Section 7(3) of the CLCPA Applies to the Commission’s Exercise of 
its Ratemaking Authority 
Section 7(3) of the CLCPA shares a similar statutory construction to §7(2) and, as 
discussed above, is at a minimum ambiguous as to whether it places specific requirements on the 
Commission’s exercise of its ratemaking authority.  Since §7(3) contains the same language as 
§7(2), as described above, pertaining to permitting and licensing, Staff relies on the discussion 
above to explain why it appears the Commission could determine that CLCPA §7(3) does not 
apply to the Commission’s exercise of its ratemaking authority. 
CLCPA §7(3) does not require any specific finding, it merely states that decisions 
to which it applies “shall not disproportionately burden disadvantaged communities as identified 
pursuant to subdivision 5 of section 75-0101 of the environmental conservation law.”  In turn, 
§75-0101(5) states “‘Disadvantaged communities’ means communities that bear burdens of 
negative public health effects, environmental pollution, impacts of climate change, and possess 
certain socioeconomic criteria, or comprise high-concentrations of low- and moderate- income 
households, as identified pursuant to section 75-0111 of this article.”  Finally, §75-0111(1)(b) 
states that the Climate Justice Working Group “in consultation with the department, the 
departments of health and labor, the New York state energy and research development authority, 
and the environmental justice advisory group, will establish criteria to identify disadvantaged

C. The Effective Date of the CLCPA Post-dates the Filing of the Instant Cases 
As noted above, these proceedings began with the filing of tariff leaves, 
testimony, and exhibits by the Companies on April 30, 2019.  On June 5, 2019 ALJs Leary and 
Costello issued a ruling providing a procedural schedule for these proceedings to meet the PSL’s 
11-month suspension period requirements.18  Over the course of 2019, Staff, the Companies, and 
intervening parties complied with the relevant procedural dates through September 2019 at 
which point the parties commenced settlement discussions on September 24, 2019.19  On 
September 27, 2019, ALJs Costello and Leary issued a ruling that the evidentiary hearings would 
be postponed to allow for settlement negotiations.20  Ultimately, the evidentiary hearings were 
postponed until February 2020, and were conducted on multiple days between February 10, 2020 
and February 25, 2020.21  By January 1, 2020, the following rounds of pre-filed testimony had 
been submitted:  Companies’ direct testimony, Companies’ first supplemental testimony, 
Companies’ corrections and updates testimony, Staff’s and intervenors’ direct testimony, all 
parties’ rebuttal testimony, and Companies’ second supplemental testimonies.  There was only 
one additional round of supplemental testimony filed in January 2020.  The evidentiary hearings 
were based on this pre-filed testimony.  Thus, a large majority of the record in these proceedings 
had been developed prior to January 1, 2020.  Furthermore, after the February 2020 evidentiary 
hearings, the litigation track of these proceedings transitioned to the briefing phase, not the 
submission of additional evidence.

B. 
 
REVENUE ALLOCATION 
1 
We largely support the Company’s revenue allocation approach, but recommend 
2 
against placing exclusive reliance on the minimum system approach; and we also have 
3 
some concerns related to the proposed rate impact mitigation.  
4 
 
5 
C. 
 
RATE DESIGN 
6 
We recommend concentrating revenue recovery on the per kWh and therm rates, 
7 
and phasing out declining block rates, in order to provide a stronger price signal to 
8 
encourage energy efficiency. We believe this approach will more effectively advance the 
9 
State’s policy goals. We also recommend that plans be initiated for a possible transition 
10 
away from the existing time of use rates to a well-designed Critical Peak Pricing rate. 
11 
  
12 
D. 
 
LOAD RESEARCH 
13 
We believe the opportunity exists to develop better, more granular load research, 
14 
using AMI data. We recommend initiating a small-scale, relatively inexpensive pilot 
15 
program to pursue this opportunity—one that builds on the Companies’ existing in-house 
16 
data research and analysis capabilities.

Q. 
Can you briefly explain the revenue allocation process in a typical utility rate case? 
15 
A. 
Yes. The results of one or more ECOS studies are typically used to help guide decisions 
16 
regarding how proposed rate changes should be spread across the various service 
17 
classifications. The specific process can vary, but ECOS results are generally examined to 
18 
see whether certain classes have been identified as having a high rate of return—meaning 
19 
they are paying more than the costs that have been allocated to them (sometimes referred 
20 
to as “overearning”). If a high RROR is identified in the ECOS study (or studies), an effort 
21 
is typically made to assign a somewhat smaller share of the rate increase or revenue 
22 


--- Page 34 ---
22-E-0317, 22-G-0318,

requirement to that service classification, thereby moving in the direction of greater 
1 
uniformity in the service classification RRORs. Similarly, if certain classes are identified 
2 
as having a low RROR (paying less than the costs that have been allocated to them, which 
3 
is sometimes described as “underearning”), a somewhat greater than average share of the 
4 
rate increase or revenue burden may be assigned to that class during the revenue allocation 
5 
step. Service classifications with an RROR that is close to the overall system average will 
6 
typically receive a very similar, or identical, percentage increase. 
7 
In carrying out the revenue allocation process, tolerance bands are often used 
8 
around the system average RROR to decide whether the service classification ROR 
9 
deviates far enough from the average RROR to merit special treatment. The tolerance band 
10 
typically has the effect of ameliorating disputes concerning the ECOS methodology, 
11 
because service classifications that fall within the tolerance band generally receive the 
12 
same percentage change, and because rate changes tend to be moderated for classes that 
13 
fall just outside the tolerance band. 
14 
The typical process just described is not the only one that has been used in New 
15 
York, and the impact of even this typical process can vary depending on important details 
16 
that are not always handled consistently. For instance, the tolerance band could be set as 
17

18

19

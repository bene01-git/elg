Q. 
What does an OFO mean in practical terms? 
1 
A. 
The specific effect of an OFO varies based on what type of OFO is issued and when it is 
2 
received, but generally, the practical effect of an OFO is that the Company limits the 
3 
availability of balancing gas services supplied by the Company to its customers with 
4 
interruptible service.  The Company creates a lower and/or upper band limiting usage to 
5 
within a certain percentage from the customers’ scheduled gas deliveries, also known as 
6 
gas nominations.  
7 
Q.  
What are the different types of OFOs? 
8 
A.  
First, there are hourly OFOs.  An hourly OFO sets a fixed amount of gas that a consumer, 
9 
such as NYPA, may use per hour based on its total daily gas nomination with the local 
10 
distribution company, in this case the Company.  For example, under a 1/24th rule, if the 
11 
total daily gas nomination is 24,000 dth, the hourly burn limit is 1,000 dth. 
12 
Second, there are over-burn OFOs which limit NYPA to a fixed percentage not to be 
13 
exceeded over its gas nomination.  Generators cannot exceed a specified fixed percentage 
14 
above their daily gas nomination.  Over-burn OFOs often cause an increase in demand 
15 
and can cause market prices to rise as generator strategy is adjusted to include risk 
16 
premiums to offset the cost of under-utilized gas purchased to manage the OFO. 
17 
The third category, an under-burn OFO, limits the quantity of unused gas the generator 
18 
can leave in the pipeline.  The generator must keep unused gas within the fixed 
19 
percentage of the daily gas nomination.  Third, under-burn OFOs will cause generators to 
20 
limit the quantity of gas purchased to avoid the risk of not utilizing the gas nomination 
21 
within the tolerance of the OFO and not be in violation.  This will result in inefficiency 
22 


--- Page 6 ---
Direct Testimony of Gas Operations Panel 
New York Power Authority 
Case 25-G-0073 
 
 
6 
 
and higher operational costs as the generator will manage usage of excess gas through the 
1 
balancing agreement over-burn tiers, which usually include a premium on the price of gas 
2 
above the index.  
3 
The final category is a banded OFO, which imposes both over-burn and under-burn fixed 
4 
limits, requiring adherence to a specific fixed percentage of burning additional gas either 
5 
above or below the total daily nomination.  Banded OFOs severely restrict the deviation 
6 
from the total daily gas nomination.  As a result, to avoid violation of the banded OFO 
7 
restrictions on gas usage, a revised bid strategy is utilized to minimize risk exposure. This 
8 
can ensure more predicable burns but may result in missed revenue opportunities and 
9 
increased operational costs.  While switching to oil at dual-fuel units is not always 
10 
necessary, it may be considered based on gas capacity. In addition, the generator may be 
11 
unable to use the balancing agreement with the Company, which can result in higher 
12 
costs if the plants cannot balance their gas usage effectively.

Q. 
Why doesn’t NYPA pay for non-interruptible gas delivery service? 
9 
A. 
Non-interruptible, or firm, service is cost prohibitive for our needs.  NYPA uses the fuel 
10 
to generate electricity and sells the electricity on the wholesale electricity markets, 
11 
administered by the NYISO.  If we were to use firm service, the higher delivery cost 
12 
would need to be added to our bids into the NYISO markets.  Not only would these costs 
13 
be so high as to prevent us from winning any bids, but as the current bidding model limits 
14 
what a generator can recover or represent for fuel costs, the costs would be so high that 
15 
they would prevent us from even entering the NYISO auction.  The result would be 
16 
unacceptable for operation of a generation unit—in fact, there are no power generators in 
17 
New York City that pay for firm gas service.

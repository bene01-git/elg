Projected Distribution Vegetation Management 1st Cycle and Steady State Spend 
NYSEG 2023-2033 
 
For the Rate Year beginning May 1, 2023, NYSEG proposes the following three modifications to its 
current program: 
1) Cycle Funding – NYSEG is requesting $39.9 million total in the Rate Year (with 3% CPI 
adjustment for subsequent years) for its cycle program to address the contract bid cost increases 
on the NYSEG system. 
2) Reclamation Funding – NYSEG is requesting $24.9 million in Rate Year (with 3% CPI 
adjustment for subsequent years) which would allow NYSEG to reclaim its entire system within 
five-years. 
3) Danger Tree Removal Funding – NYSEG is requesting to continue its Danger Tree Programs 
with inflationary adjustments only which will total $10.8 million in the Rate Year (with 3% CPI 
adjustment for subsequent years).

EAB And Other Infestations/Biological Threats to NY Forests 
It is estimated that in New York State ash trees (fraxinus spp.) comprise approximately eight percent of 
the total trees within the state. Emerald Ash Borer (Agrilus planipennis Fairmaire) or EAB poses a 
significant risk to all of New York’s estimated 900 million ash trees 1 Since EAB’s initial discovery in 
Cattaraugus County in July 2009, its existence has now been confirmed in all New York counties except: 
Essex, Hamilton, Lewis, and Washington. This stands in stark comparison to the 11 counties with known 
infestations when last reported in the 2019 rate case. The rate of spread has been swift and the risk to the 
NYSEG and RG&E electrical grid cannot be understated. 
Consumers Energy in the state of Michigan has seen substantial increases in outages related to EAB 
damaged trees. In addition, outage minutes are also increasing due to the catastrophic nature of these 
outages. Consumers Energy estimates a 150 percent increase in total tree-caused outages due to EAB 
related tree outages over the next five to seven years. The customer outage impact is estimated to be 
approximately $18 million per year for the next 10-years in the form of outage restoration costs and lost 
revenues and effect approximately one million additional customers per year. Consumers Energy

Trees become infested when adult beetles lay eggs on the bark, which hatch into larvae that bore into the 
tree. The larvae tunnel in the phloem layer (between bark and wood) and disrupt the movement of water 
and nutrients, eventually killing the tree. Tree mortality can be swift, commonly occurring within one to 
three years. From an electric utility standpoint, this poses obvious risks to service reliability. Ash trees 
that succumb to EAB are often subject to mechanical failure at the root plate, resulting in the whole tree 
hinging over at the base. Mechanical failure of this magnitude can cause severe damage to the utility 
infrastructure, causing conductors to fail, broken poles, and other hardware damage.  
Dead ash trees are extremely hazardous to remove since they cannot be safely climbed. Therefore, quick 
and decisive action must be taken to remove these trees prior to tree death. Dead ash trees can also be 
much more expensive to remove since safety issues generally require higher levels of expertise and 
alternative removal techniques to affect their removal. 
 
Potential Impact to Facilities 
 
The most recent vegetation workload study conducted by Lakeside Environmental Consultants, LLC. 
(ECI) in 2010 estimated that approximately 10.5 percent of the total 2.427 million trees under and 
immediately adjacent to NYSEG’s overhead distribution system were ash species. Approximately 10.9 
percent of the total 400,000 trees on the RG&E system were ash species. Therefore, approximately 
299,000 ash trees may pose a significant risk to the safety and reliability of the electrical infrastructure. 
Since the 2010 ECI study did not account for trees outside of the current line corridors, it is estimated that 
the total ash tree population that must be addressed could easily be double that amount.  
In studies performed recently by ECI, the cost of deferral (the “do nothing approach”) in removing ash 
trees far exceeds the proactive removal costs due to increased removal costs (i.e., dead trees cannot be 
safely climbed), outages resulting in major equipment damage, and customer dissatisfaction.

Other Biological Threats 
 
Emerald Ash Borer is not the only threat to the health of New York’s trees. Several other insects and 
diseases pose a significant threat and potential mortality to the trees within the NYSEG and RG&E 
footprint. While the potential mortality rate may not match the staggering 96 percent death rate of ash 
trees infested with EAB, it is significant enough to cause concern among utilities owners due to the 
potential of facility damage caused by failing trees. These pests/diseases include:

•

•

VIII. REVENUE ALLOCATION AND RATE DESIGN ISSUES  
UIU witness Ms. Danielle Panko submitted direct testimony and exhibits offering

A. ECOS Study  
The Companies’ proposed hypothetical minimum system ECOS study should not serve as the 
sole basis for revenue allocation in these rate cases. Instead, the Commission should consider the 
results of both types of pro-forma ECOS studies presented by the KEDNY and KEDLI in their 
respective rate filing,12 for the reasons set forth below. By considering both sets of ECOS models, 
the Commission will have broader context, be able to more accurately evaluate the extent to which 
specific service classes are currently over- or under-contributing to the revenue requirement, and 
have necessary tools to neutralize flaws or questionable assumptions that may be apparent in any 
single study. Additionally, to ensure that multiple types of ECOS studies are available for 
consideration by the parties in the next rate cases, the Companies should be required to once again

Model 1 (“Minimum System”)

This model is controversial because of its hypothetical nature that introduces a customer-related 
component and the inherently subjective judgements and assumptions that are used in developing 
it. As a preliminary matter, the premise of a minimum system which assigns distribution main 
costs as customer-related is controversial. The issue is that the minimum system introduces a 
customer-related component which tends to over allocate costs to small customers (which are the 
majority of the customers) thereby most of the costs would be allocated to those customers.

Model 2 (“100% Demand Approach”)  
Compared to Model 1 (Minimum System), Model 2 only changes the classification of FERC 
Account 376 (Distribution Mains) costs. This model classifies all of the distribution main costs in 
FERC Account 376 as demand-related and none as customer-related. This is a large account with 
potentially significant cost impact on small customers. The decision to classify main costs as 100% 
demand related is recognized within the “basic system theory” approach described in the NARUC 
Manual (Ex. 631 p. 23).21 UIU recommends that Model 2 (100% Demand Approach) be considered 
in these rate cases because it will help illustrate a range of reasonable outcomes relating to this 
large account, which will equip the Commission with more objective information upon which to 
make an informed decision on cost allocation. As an initial matter, Model 2 is a sound theoretical 
approach that is within the range of reasonable outcomes for ECOS results. For the reasons

In Model 2, because distribution mains are classified as 100% demand related, the costs

appropriately linked to the specific customers in each service classification.

3. Flaws in the Minimum System Approach 
As the NARUC manual discusses, there is a certain amount of judgment used in any cost of 
service study, and these judgments may lead to different results.41 While some of these judgments 
are relatively non-controversial, a classic example of a highly disputed judgment is the decision to 
classify some of the costs of distribution mains as being “customer-related,” even though mains 
are shared by many different customers, and the costs cannot be specifically traced to specific 
customers (2/13/20 Tr. p. 1432).42  This judgment has been debated for decades. (2/13/20 Tr. p. 
18).

There is no direct way to calculate the customer-related costs of distribution mains.  As such,

customer-related costs.  This method is highly controversial as distribution mains are primarily 
built to meet demand and the costs associated with distribution mains are dependent upon factors 
such as the geography, not the number of customers. Ms. Panko aptly noted, “[D]istribution . . . is 
built to satisfy the demand of the system on its peak design day. It doesn’t necessarily mean that 
it’s equally distributed among customers, or at least the cost.” (2/13/20 Tr. p. 1432).   
As an illustrative example, longer mains are required to reach more widely dispersed 
customers.  The correlation between miles of mains and number of customers is limited, because 
many other important variables influence the length of the mains required to reach any given 
location or a specific geographic area, such as population density and characteristics of the housing 
stock. As a basic matter, longer mains are needed to serve larger, more widely separated buildings, 
while shorter mains will suffice if an area consists of smaller, more closely spaced buildings.  
In rebuttal testimony, New York City witness Mr. Baudino makes the unsupported argument 
that “the number of customers connected to the distribution system is another important causative 
factor in distribution main investment.” (2/25/20 Tr. p. 5568).   Yet, no statistical evidence was 
provided in the Mr. Baudino’s testimony to support this claim. In reality, the minimum system 
approach rests on the mere assumption that result of hypothetical computations can appropriately 
be characterized as “customer-related” costs. Yet, in practice, the “customer-related” category is 
effectively a dumping ground for costs that do not necessarily vary with the size of the pipe. These 
costs also do not necessarily vary with the number of customers – like the cost of trenching and 
the cost of re-routing traffic while mains are being installed. There is no justification for requiring

actually designed. Furthermore, the hypothetical assumption of 2-inch plastic mains is purely

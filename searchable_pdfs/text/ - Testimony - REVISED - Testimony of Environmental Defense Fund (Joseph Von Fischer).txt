Q. What are the criteria for finding a leak/elevated methane reading? 
25

A. We characterized methane concentrations as elevated by comparing individual data points with 
1 
average background concentrations.  We found background methane levels were highly variable 
2 
within and among cities and in time, and thus found a fixed threshold suboptimal. Instead, we 
3 
defined local background methane concentrations as a moving average long enough for the 
4 
baseline itself to be unaffected by large natural gas leaks, yet small enough to reflect local 
5 
variations.  Controlled release experiments described above indicated that a two-minute window 
6 
optimized this tradeoff; for example, a large (40 L min-1) leak caused methane to be elevated for 
7 
40 m or about 5 seconds at a typical 40 km h-1 driving speed, and so a large leak would have less 
8 
than 5% impact on the baseline methane concentration over a 2 minute period. As noted above, 
9 
leaks are binned into low, medium and high categories, depending on leak flow rate – that is, the 
10 
magnitude of elevated concentrations observed over time. 
11 
We developed a protocol for flagging methane readings as significantly above 
12 
background based on typical instrument precision and results from controlled release 
13 
experiments. The threshold for defining elevated methane concentrations was greater than 10% or 
14 
greater than 4 SD above background, whichever is greater. In an examination of data collected in 
15 
urban areas, we found that the 10% threshold was invoked for more than 90% of readings as 
16 
compared to the 4 SD threshold; the latter only defined the threshold in areas with high 
17 
background and/or highly variable methane readings.

Q. Please explain the stochastic nature of leak observation as part of this study. What are the 
21 
implications?    
22 
A. The probability of observing a leak from low-pressure natural gas pipes appears to be influenced 
23 
by leak magnitude, with larger leaks observed more frequently.  As a consequence, the data that 
24 
we have collected reflects a sample of the natural gas leaks in any given city, not a 
25

16-G-0059

comprehensive inventory, because even when leaks are persistent, they are not observed all the 
1 
time.  Atmospheric stability, wind speed and direction, traffic effects and other factors can 
2 
prevent a sampling vehicle from detecting a natural gas plume.  Observation of the plume is thus 
3 
an unpredictable event to some degree, but this stochasticity can be greatly reduced with 
4 
increased observations.  
5 
There are several important implications of the stochastic nature of methane plume 
6 
detection.  First, our survey of natural gas leaks is a sample and not a complete census.  The 
7 
probability of observing all of the leaks in a city rises with sampling effort, with 8 or 9 
8 
observations of a stretch of road required to measure at least 90% of the true population of leaks.  
9 
In comparison, routine utility leak surveys are intended to be a complete census, though it seems 
10 
likely that those efforts also miss some leaks.  When we compared our leak maps with utilities’, 
11 
we consistently identified leaks that the utilities were unaware of, and vice versa. Second, these 
12 
stochastic effects and our requirement of two or more observations suggest that our estimates of 
13 
total leak frequency and magnitude should be considered underestimates of the true rates.

Q. Please describe the procedure used for attribution of leaks to PSE&G infrastructure under 
6 
this leak quantification pilot project.  
7 
A. To attribute an elevated reading to a location, a series of buffering and consolidation procedures 
8 
are used, which are graphically represented in sequential order in Figure 1 of Exhibit III. First, the 
9 
series of points representing an elevated methane reading is buffered using a 20 meter buffer 
10 
(Steps 1 and 2). This buffer distance represents the average width of a plume of gas at the 
11 
distances from curbside likely to be encountered while driving a city street, as identified by the 
12 
controlled release testing described above. Each 20 meter buffer is then consolidated into a single 
13 
point, that is, the centroid (Step 3), each of which is again buffered using a 20 meter buffer (Step 
14 
4). Overlapping buffers (hereafter an “Observed Reading Cluster” or “ORC”) are then merged 
15 
into a single buffer, the centroid of which is the location assigned to a “verified leak” (Steps 5, 6 
16 
and 7). A verified leak is constituted of at least two overlapping buffers; in other words, a leak 
17 
must have been detected on at least two separate sampling drives for it to constitute a verified 
18 
leak.  
19 
Any verified leak with an associated ORC intersecting the utility’s pipe is attributed to 
20 
that pipe. In instances where an ORC intersects multiple pipes, such as pipeline intersections, a 
21 
leak cannot be attributed to a specific utility pipe segment, but can still generally be attributed to 
22 
the utility’s infrastructure. The size of the mapped ORCs represents the relative uncertainty of 
23 
verified leak locations. The larger the plume of leaked gas, and the resulting ORC, the greater the 
24 
uncertainty of the actual leak location.

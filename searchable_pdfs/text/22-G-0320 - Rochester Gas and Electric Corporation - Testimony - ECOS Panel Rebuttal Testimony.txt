II. ALLOCATION OF ELECTRIC TRANSMISSION COSTS 
3 
Q. 
Do other parties have any recommendations regarding the allocation of transmission 
4 
costs in the electric ECOS studies? 
5 
A. 
Yes.  MI recommends NYSEG allocate its transmission costs using the average of the 
6 
two summer coincident peaks (“2SCP”) and that RG&E allocate its transmission costs 
7 
using a 1 Coincident Peak (“1CP”) allocator.1  The Staff Electric Rates Panel 
8 
recommends using a 2 Coincident Peak average (“2CP”) (average of the summer and 
9 
winter peaks) to allocate NYSEG’s transmission costs.2 
10 
Q. 
Do you agree with these parties’ recommendations regarding the allocation of 
11 
transmission costs? 
12 
A. 
No.  Both MI and the Staff Electric Rates Panel recommendations reflect faulty 
13 
assumptions that one or two peak months’ higher demands drive all transmission 
14 
investment and that the remaining months are not relevant to the planning and operation 
15 
of the transmission system. 
16 
Q. 
What drives transmission system investment? 
17 
A. 
An integrated transmission system is essentially a network that incorporates a variety of 
18 
voltages, interconnections and energy resources over a large geographic area.  This 
19 
network serves a varied level of load throughout the year and the service territory.  In 
20 
addition, to ensure that a reasonable level of reliability is maintained throughout the year,

Q. 
Are the costs of the distribution system related to demand only? 
10 
A. 
No.  Distribution system costs are incurred to move electricity from generation and 
11 
transmission facilities to individual customers that are distributed geographically 
12 
throughout the service territory of a utility.  A significant portion of those costs are 
13 
incurred regardless of the peak demand of the customers.  Increases or decreases in 
14 
demand alone do not result in proportional increases or decreases in the number of poles 
15 
and miles of conductors required to distribute electricity geographically.  For example, if 
16 
the load of customers in the Companies’ service territory was significantly reduced, but 
17 
the number of customers was unchanged, we would not expect the number of poles and 
18 
miles of wires to be reduced by the same proportion.  As a result, the costs associated 
19 
with a distribution system relate to both the peak amount of load the system is designed

Q. 
At page 20, the UIU Rate Panel states “A new utility pole does not necessarily need to be 
3 
installed when a new customer connects to the grid, nor will the cost of poles be reduced 
4 
if a customer moves to another service area.”  Do you agree with this statement? 
5 
A. 
No.  There are two fundamental issues with this statement.  First, the fact that an addition 
6 
or a reduction of one customer may not require an addition or reduction of new poles 
7 
does not imply that 100% of the cost of the poles are related to demand.  As previously 
8 
stated, increases or decreases in demand do not proportionally affect the number of poles 
9 
required in a service territory.  For these reasons, it is unreasonable to assume that the 
10 
costs of poles are 100% demand-related.  The minimum system on the other hand 
11 
recognizes that a portion of the distribution system is customer-related and a portion is 
12 
demand-related.  Second, by focusing solely on the impact of adding a new customer, 
13 
UIU fails to recognize the fact that much of the distribution system already in place 
14 
(before the hypothetical new customer is added) was designed to serve the minimum 
15 
requirements of existing customers and their geographic dispersion.  The point of 
16 
conducting a minimum system study is to isolate the portion of the distribution system 
17 
that does not vary with demand.

Q. 
Are the costs of the distribution mains related to demand only? 
1 
A. 
No.  Distribution mains perform two functions; providing for the customers’ peak usage 
2 
and to connect the customer to the system.  Therefore, it is appropriate to assign some 
3 
level of costs to the customer function.  There is a minimum size of distribution mains 
4 
that is required to deliver natural gas to all customers that are actually on the Companies’ 
5 
system, and the costs of this minimum system is unrelated to either peak demands or total 
6 
consumption. 
7 
Q. 
Is the classification of distribution mains as demand- and customer-related an accepted 
8 
gas utility practice? 
9 
A. 
There are several recognized methods for the classification and allocation of distribution 
10 
mains.  The NARUC Gas Distribution Rate Design Manual recognizes, at page 22, that 
11 
“a portion of the costs associated with the distribution system may be included as 
12 
customer cost.”10  This practice of classifying mains between customer and demand is 
13 
utilized by many utilities throughout the United States. 
14 
Q. 
Are you aware of other utilities in New York that classify a portion of their distribution 
15 
mains costs as being customer-related? 
16 
A. 
Yes.  Every single investor-owned gas utility in New York State classifies a portion of 
17 
their distribution costs as being customer-related.

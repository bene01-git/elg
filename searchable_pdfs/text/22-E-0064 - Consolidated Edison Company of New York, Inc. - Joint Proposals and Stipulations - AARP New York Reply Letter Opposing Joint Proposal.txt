Dear Secretary Phillips and Judges Moreno and Costello: 
 
AARP New York hereby submits reply comments to the statements supporting and opposing the 
settlement in the Con Edison electric and rate proceedings (Joint Proposal or JP).  
 
The assertions and factual analyses of opponents and supporters of the Joint Proposal reinforce 
AARP New York’s conclusion that many of the key recommendations contained in the JP are 
contrary to the public interest as well as statutory and regulatory policies1 and promote rates that 
are per se unjust and unreasonable. The focus of this reply is on several parties’ statements 
pertaining to the Commission’s standard of review of joint proposals, a topic which we discussed 
in our initial letter.  Before turning to that subject, we address parties’ statements on other topics 
of interest to AARP New York.

Residential Delivery Bill Impacts Not Identified  
The Settling Parties’ respective filings continued their practice of not providing the impacts of 
the JP’s gas and electric revenue requirements on the delivery portion of average-use residential 
electric, gas heat and gas cooking-only customers’ bills.  Nevertheless, the City of New York 
confirmed AARP New York’s view that actual bill impacts will be substantially higher than 
suggested in the body of the JP and its appendices, stating: 
 
When it evaluates Con Edison’s request for rate increases in these proceedings, 
as well as other utilities’ requests for rate increases in their respective rate cases, 
it is important for the Commission to examine costs and impacts holistically, 
and to take into account all contributors to customers’ monthly bills.  
Unfortunately, in these proceedings, the City was unable to obtain or present a 
full picture of the bill impacts for the next three years. While the lack of such 
information is not fatal to a finding that the Joint Proposal produces just and 
reasonable rates, the Commission should require in future proceedings that it 
be presented with a comprehensive summary of all charges to be included on 
customers’ bills and of the associated bill impacts. While the increases each 
year have been moderated as a result of the parties’ negotiations and 
compromises, they nevertheless are substantial. Inasmuch as the increases 
compound, the total impact over the three-year term is very substantial.2 
 
Con Edison agreed that actual bill increases will be higher than suggested in the JP, stating:  
“The Joint Proposal also continues accounting mechanisms and other rate provisions that the 
Commission has found just and reasonable and in the public interest and includes new provisions 
that allow the Company to recover costs incurred for customers on a timely basis, thereby 
mitigating future base rate increases.”3 
 
The City of New York also complained that “Con Edison needs to better control its spending, 
and it needs to reconsider its long-range plans to reduce the cost of future work. According to its 
Electric Long-Range Plan, the Company plans to spend more than $53 billion in electric system 
capital improvements through 2031. [footnote omitted] That level of spending is more than 
double its current annual capital investment level. The Company also plans to make gas-related 
capital investments of about $13 billion over the same time period. [footnote omitted]  Neither 
of these spending levels are reasonable, and they will place massive burdens on 
customers.”4 
 
The Settling Parties continue the misleading practice of comparing the JP’s revenue requirement 
recovered over three rate years to (a) the Company’s filed one-year request and (b) the likely 
outcome of a one-year litigated case.  This practice will be discussed in the standard of review

Return on Equity Too High and Earnings Sharing Mechanism Flawed 
According to DPS Staff, “[t]he Joint Proposal’s recommended allowed ROE of 9.25% is a fair 
compromise between the Company’s position in its original filing and Staff’s position in its 
direct pre-filed testimony.”5  The City of New York stated that “the return on equity (“ROE”)[ ] 
was negotiated as a single number and cannot be separated into component parts.6  A problem 
with these assertions is that application of the Commission’s three-decade-old Generic Finance 
Methodology (GFM) yields results that are not susceptible to interpretation or equivocation.  
DPS Staff did not include in its supporting filing any update to its pre-filed 8.75 percent.  Having 
failed to do so, it is completely appropriate to separate the JP’s ROE of 9.25 perent into the 
component parts of the GFM result and the stay out or risk premium.  
 
As AARP New York stated in our previous filing, in an era of a tough economy, existing 
affordability challenges, high levels of arrears and termination letters, and the JP’s recommended 
double-digit delivery charge increases imposed over a 19- or 20-month period, it is unreasonable 
and unjust to provide the Company with any bonus, especially one of 50 basis points.  As PULP 
stated:  “The proposed ROE is predicated on the same flawed multi-year settlement framework 
that has burdened Con Edison customers for the past four rate cases, costing them up to an 
estimated $1.8 billion in unnecessary, excessive delivery charges since 2010. [footnote 
omitted]”7  PULP also shares our objection to the JP’s earnings sharing mechanism (ESM).  
PULP asserts that “[s]ince 2010, this practice has resulted in ratepayers sharing just $31 million 
(8%) of the $385 million of Con Edison’s earnings exceeding its authorized ROE.”8  We agree 
with PULP that ESMs do not address the fundamental flaw of these rate plans, namely, that 
authorized ROEs are set for three years and are not subject to yearly adjustments to account for 
changes in macroeconomic and investment expectations affecting Con Edison’s cost of equity 
during the rate plan.  Accordingly, we join PULP’s request of the Commission regarding ROE 
updates for each year of Con Edison’s rate plan.9

Standard of Review is Flawed 
We explained in our previous filing that the Commission’s standard of review of joint proposals, 
and the way it is implemented by rote, is so flawed as to routinely result in approval of 
settlements that provide unjust and unreasonable rates and are contrary to the public interest. The 
filings of DPS Staff, Con Edison, and the City of New York provide ample support for our 
assertion. 
 
SANE provided criticism from a slightly different perspective: 
 
As we said in our Direct Testimony, utility rate cases in New York State are 
characterized by procedural injustice and fail to ensure the full and informed 
involvement of communities in the energy decisions that will affect them. The 
way the process is designed, there is no possibility a JP can be produced that is 
truly in the public interest.  As we expanded upon in our testimony, there are a 
number of interconnected procedural mechanisms that prevent affected 
communities from participating meaningfully in the rate case process, including 
insufficient public notice requirements, the power of utility companies to set the 
rate case agenda, steep information asymmetry between the parties, and 
communities’ lack of resources and technical expertise.12 
 
From this perspective, the lack of intervenor funding is a fatal flaw to the claim of settling 
parties, ALJs, and Commissioners that due procees is afforded to parties merely because they 
were invited to participate in all settlement meetings. 
 
The City of New York pointed out, likely inadvertently, the fundamental flaw in the 
Commission’s consideration of the merits of a joint proposal, stating:  “The Joint Proposal is 
intended to be, and should be read as, a comprehensive proposal that resolves numerous issues 
raised by the Company and/or parties in these proceedings. The Commission should view the 
provisions comprehensively and not consider any single provision in isolation.”13  DPS Staff 
concurred, asserting:  “When considering whether the Joint Proposal is in the public interest, the 
document should be considered as a whole, with each individual section providing support and 
balance to the others.”14  Con Edison remarked:  “Any opposition to one or more elements of the

As noted in AARP New York’s previous filing with examples of the Commission’s reasoning, 
this “overall, on balance” approach admittedly results in sound proposals that would benefit 
residential ratepayers not being adopted by the Commission for fear of upsetting the results of 
the “give and take” of negotiations.  For example: 
 
In our review of AARP’s objections, some of the party’s complaints argue that 
provisions could be improved, while other provisions are not clear or are not fair 
to some class of customers while benefitting others. However, as we consider 
the Joint Proposal and the numerous and diverse parties that support its terms, 
we do not find anything in AARP’s objections that rises to the level of 
rendering the Joint Proposal not in the public interest as a whole. In our 
view, the Joint Proposal has resulted from the bargaining of various interests as 
is made clear from the testimony filed in the underlying litigation. We are 
reticent to upset that balance where, as here, the Joint Proposal falls within the 
range of reasonable litigated outcomes and achieves a balance of multiple party 
interests.16  
 
Other than the 2019 NYSEG/RG&E proceedings, the Commission would be hard pressed to find 
an instance where it accepted a change to a joint proposal advocated by an opponent of the joint 
proposal. 
 
Implicit in the Commission’s standard of review is the belief that all parties have the same 
bargaining power.  This is obviously not the case in practice.  Even were all intervenors 
adequately funded, DPS Staff and Con Edison do not view residential consumer advocates as 
necessary supporters of a joint proposal. Their proposals, therefore, are given less consideration 
than say, the proposals of the City of New York. But, even this party noted its concern on its 
signature page that it worried its policy proposals would not be accepted if it did not sign the JP.  
Additionally, parties with one or just a handful of issues do not have the same ability to engage 
in the “give and take” since they have fewer isssues over which to bargain.  Most significantly, 
with few exceptions joint proposals rarely include provisions advocated by parties that oppose 
the overall settlement. 
 
It is not uncommon for DPS Staff or the utility to advise a “secondary tier” party that it would 
agree to include its position on the settlement document only if that party agrees to support the 
entire settlement, which may include rate increases or other elements with which the party would 
otherwise oppose. The PSC pointed out an example of this dynamic in its Order in a recent 
Central Hudson Electric & Gas proceeding, stating: 
 
The proposed residential customer and minimum charge amounts, which are a 
reduction from the current amounts, are the product of compromise between the

At a glance, this process of compromise may appear appropriate on first impression, and it may

having one’s position that would have a de minimus or no revenue impact included in a joint

"secondary tier" parties' support would be in exchange for those parties abandoning positions and 
arguments on delivery and customer charge issues that are central to those parties’ core mission.

revenue increases sought by the Company in its pre-filed testimony to the revenue increases

recommendations in the Joint Proposal reflect a 58% reduction in the electric revenue 
requirement and a 45% reduction in the gas revenue requirement from the amounts sought in 
the Initial Filing.”20  NYECC stated, after listing the Company’s original and updated requests 
and the JP’s revenue requirement:  “Accordingly, ratepayers in both rate plans will pay far less 
under the revenue requirements in the three-year rate plans in the JP than under the original or 
updated amounts previously sought by Con Edison.”21  It is not necessarily the correct

The pattern requires the utility to file overinflated revenue requests that is sometime

The electric and gas revenue levels established in the Joint Proposal, while

Instead, the legitimate and appropriate way to evaluate the reasonableness of a joint proposal’s

position.  The ALJs and Commission’s analysis should probe the reasonableness of each of the 
JP’s deviation from DPS Staff’s litigation position.  Just saying that a concession was made to 
achive a settlement is inadequate. 
 
Moreover, the belief of some Settling Parties that the rates resulting from litigation would be 
higher than the rates recommended in the JP is undermined by two factors.23  One, this belief 
assumes that the ALJs and the Commission would reject most of DPS Staff’s adjustments in its 
pre-filed testimony.  Second, this belief ignores the fact that the JP’s delivery and customer 
charge increases will go into effect in little more than 18 months. 
 
Accordingly, for the reasons expressed above and in our previous filing, as well as in PULP’s 
Statement in Opposition, AARP New York urges the ALJs to recommend rejection of the JP by 
the Commission.  The proposed massive double-digit delivery charge increases over a period of 
less than two years are per se unjust and unreasonable and not in the public interest. We also 
request that the ALJs’ recommend the Settling Parties modify the JP by adopting AARP New 
York’s recommendations, including: significantly reducing revenue increases (partially 
accomplished by eliminating the stay out/risk premium); eliminating proposed increases to fixed 
customer charges (for example, refraining from increasing the already too high electric customer 
charge of $17 to $20 per month); incorporating meaningful additional steps to increase EAP 
enrollment by 50%, with metrics and associated negative revenue adjustments; ensuring the 
Company will vigorously pursue it application to the U.S. Department of Energy for all available 
federal funds (as an alternative to spending ratepayer dollars), with milestones and associated 
negative revenue adjustments and requiring the Company to report how any federal funds 
received will impact the JP’s revendue requuirements; and removing EAMs that reward the 
Company for complying with state law.

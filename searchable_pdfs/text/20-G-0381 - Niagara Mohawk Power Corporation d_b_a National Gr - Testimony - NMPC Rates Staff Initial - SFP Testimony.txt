FAIR RATE OF RETURN DISCUSSION 
6 
Q. 
Please explain what is meant by a fair rate of 
7 
return. 
8 
A. 
A utility’s overall rate of return represents the 
9 
cost of capital used to finance the long-term 
10 
utility assets that are providing regulated 
11 
utility service to customers, which in the rate-
12 
setting context are referred to as “rate base.”  
13 
The estimation of a utility’s rate of return 
14 
requires an estimate of the company’s capital 
15 
structure and the cost rates of the various forms 
16 
of capital financing the utility’s rate base.  
17 
The ratemaking process should provide a utility 
18 
with a reasonable opportunity to earn a return on 
19 
utility investments equivalent to the return 
20 
available on investments of similar risk.  
21 
Ultimately, it is the utility management’s 
22 
responsibility to operate the company in a manner 
23 
that allows it to earn the allowed return for its 
24 


--- Page 11 ---
Cases 20-E-0380 & 20-G-0381 
SFP 
 
10 
 
investors. 
1 
Q. 
How is a fair rate of return calculated? 
2 
A. 
Generally, in New York State, the fair rate of 
3 
return for a utility company is calculated 
4 
through a weighted average of the individual cost 
5 
components of its expected capitalization during 
6 
the rate year.  Calculating the proper cost 
7 
components involves forecasting and reconciling a 
8 
company’s sources of capital with their 
9 
individual requirements.  The two primary sources 
10 
are long-term debt and common equity.  Preferred 
11 
stock is also used, although generally in much 
12 
smaller proportions than either long-term debt or 
13 
common equity.  Finally, customer deposits, while 
14 
a very small component, are almost always 
15 
reflected in the expected capitalization because 
16 
they are a relatively permanent and stable source 
17 
of capital employed by utilities. 
18 
Q. 
How are the cost rates determined for each of the 
19 
individual components? 
20 
A. 
The cost rates associated with the Company’s 
21 
long-term debt, preferred stock and customer 
22 
deposits are relatively simple to ascertain.  The 
23 
method for estimating the cost of long-term debt 
24 


--- Page 12 ---
Cases 20-E-0380 & 20-G-0381 
SFP 
 
11 
 
is generally established in the contractual terms 
1 
contained in the securities issued by the Company 
2 
to the investors of its debt securities.  In 
3 
return for lending money to the Company, debt 
4 
holders receive earnings in the form of 
5 
contractual payments of interest and principal.  
6 
However, since the intent is to forecast the 
7 
weighted average cost rate during the Rate Year, 
8 
the analyst then must forecast the amounts, 
9 
timing, and terms of new issuances contemplated 
10 
to occur during the rate proceeding and through 
11 
the end of the Rate Year.  Preferred stock cost 
12 
rates can be readily calculated by examining 
13 
their contractual terms; e.g., the preferred 
14 
dividends for the preferred stock.  Customer 
15 
deposits have a prescribed cost rate that is set 
16 
annually by the Commission.  The last cost 
17 
component is the Company’s common equity.  The 
18 
expected return requirements of a utility’s 
19 
common equity investors can only be gleaned 
20 
through a cost of equity analysis.  Generally, 
21 
methodologies such as the Discounted Cash Flow 
22 
(DCF) and the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM)

Q. 
Please briefly explain the concept of ring-
7 
fencing in this context? 
8 
A. 
Ring-fencing, with respect to a regulated public 
9 
utility subsidiary, occurs when a subsidiary 
10 
financially separates itself from its holding 
11 
company/parent company to protect the subsidiary 
12 
from financial instability or the bankruptcy of 
13 
the parent.  The intent of the ring-fencing 
14 
measures is to insulate assets in the subsidiary 
15 
from the risks of the holding company/parent and 
16 
from the other subsidiaries in the holding 
17 
company.  In theory, if a subsidiary is ring-
18 
fenced, and the holding company or another 
19 
subsidiary goes bankrupt, creditors would not be 
20 
able to attach their claims to the assets of the 
21 
ring-fenced subsidiary.  In addition, a fully 
22 
ring-fenced company should be insulated from its 
23 
parent’s influence regarding the financial 
24

Cases 20-E-0380 & 20-G-0381 
SFP 
 
17 
 
decisions of the subsidiary. 
1

2

3 
A. 
Ring-fencing may allow a subsidiary to be rated 
4 
higher than the parent.  If a utility subsidiary 
5 
achieves a higher credit rating than its parent, 
6 
there is a benefit of a lower cost of debt to the 
7 
subsidiary and ultimately to ratepayers.

CAPITAL ASSET PRICING MODEL 
11 
Q. 
Please describe the basic theory underlying the 
12 
CAPM. 
13 
A. 
The principle behind the CAPM theory is that 
14 
there is no premium, in terms of expected return, 
15 
for bearing risks that can be eliminated through 
16 
diversification.  According to the CAPM, rational 
17 
investors will hold a portfolio of stocks 
18 
generally 60 or more, such that the overall risk 
19 
of the portfolio, in terms of variability of its 
20 
returns, closely approximates that of the market 
21 
as a whole.  Thus, the only risk that matters in 
22 
the CAPM equation is said to be “systematic 
23 
risk,” which is common to all equity securities 
24 


--- Page 51 ---
Cases 20-E-0380 & 20-G-0381 
SFP 
 
50 
 
and cannot be diversified away. 
1 
Q. 
How can “systematic risk” be measured? 
2 
A. 
It is a measure of a stock’s volatility with the 
3 
stock market as a whole and is known as “beta.”  
4 
Systematic risk also is known as market risk, 
5 
which is the uncertainty inherent to the entire 
6 
market or market segment.  Changes in interest 
7 
rates, recessions, wars, and pandemics are all 
8 
examples of systematic risks. 
9 
Q. 
Do particular stocks have risks other than this 
10 
systematic risk? 
11 
A. 
Yes.  “Unsystematic” risk is risk that is 
12 
specific to a particular stock.  While it is 
13 
assumed that most stocks tend to go along with 
14 
the general market, at least to some extent, 
15 
factors that are specific to an individual stock 
16 
are said to affect its “unsystematic” risk. 
17 
Q. 
How does one measure an individual stock’s 
18 
unsystematic risk? 
19 
A. 
According to the CAPM, the appropriate way to 
20 
measure an individual stock’s risk is through a 
21 
correlation of its return relative to the market 
22 
as a whole, known as beta.  A stock with a beta 
23 
of 1.0 has a return that mirrors the return of 
24 


--- Page 52 ---
Cases 20-E-0380 & 20-G-0381 
SFP 
 
51 
 
the “market,” usually taken to mean the S&P 500, 
1 
as a whole.  Betas of less than 1.0, which are 
2 
typical for utility stocks given the moderating 
3 
influence of regulation and the accompanying 
4 
perception of reduced risk, indicate that the 
5 
stocks are less volatile than the market as a 
6 
whole.  The CAPM informs us that investors will 
7 
only be compensated for their actual risk, as 
8 
measured by beta.  Thus, in the case of 
9 
estimating the return requirements of utility 
10 
investors, the CAPM result will reflect the 
11 
degree to which utility stocks are less volatile 
12 
than the market as a whole. 
13 
Q. 
On what assumptions is the CAPM based? 
14 
A. 
The model is based on the assumptions that: (1) 
15 
the capital market is competitive and efficient; 
16 
(2) investors are risk-averse and demand higher 
17 
returns for higher risk; (3) market participants 
18 
can lend and borrow unlimited amounts under the 
19 
risk-free rate of interest; and (4) investors 
20 
hold diversified portfolios and operate in 
21 
capital markets with no transaction costs, taxes, 
22 
or restrictions on financial transactions.  Under 
23 
the CAPM model, the expected return of a security 
24

SFP 
 
52 
 
or portfolio is equal to the rate on a risk-free 
1

COMMERCIAL CHARGER PROGRAM 
16 
Q. 
What is ‘make-ready’ in the context of EV charging infrastructure development? 
17 
A. 
Make-ready is a term that refers to electrical infrastructure expansion or upgrade 
18 
investments that are necessary to deliver electricity to the EV charging equipment.  It 
19 
typically includes electrical infrastructure up to, but not including, the charging 
20 
equipment itself.  Examples of EV make-ready investments include electrical transformer 
21 
upgrades, trenching and boring for conduits, conductors, poles, and towers.   
22

Q. 
Why is make-ready financial support important for the deployment of DCFC 
1 
infrastructure? 
2 
A. 
Make-ready costs for installing and connecting DCFCs can be a significant financial 
3 
barrier to deployment.  Higher power DCFC technology (100 kW or higher) charges a 
4 
vehicle much faster than other types of EV charging infrastructure.  Doing so utilizes 
5 
significantly more electricity and requires more infrastructure upgrades to deliver the 
6 
electricity to the charger.  Department of Public Service (“DPS”) Staff noted in their 
7 
Whitepaper Regarding Electric Vehicle Supply Equipment and Infrastructure 
8 
Deployment, issued on January 13, 2020, (“Staff Whitepaper”) that make-ready costs for 
9 
a 150 kW station with four plugs may be approximately $200,000 in upstate New York 
10 
and can account for as much as two-thirds of the initial capital costs to build out a site.  
11 
With high upfront make-ready capital costs and the current low-level of electric vehicle 
12 
adoption, particularly in upstate New York, near-term revenues from low utilization of 
13 
chargers may not be sufficient for developers to invest in the DCFC necessary to address 
14 
range anxiety.  
15

Q. 
Why is there a need for the Company’s Commercial Program in light of the 
1 
statewide Make-Ready Program? 
2 
A. 
The Commission’s statewide Make-Ready Program is an important step toward reducing 
3 
the barriers to deployment of EV charging infrastructure.  But it is not the only barrier to 
4 
development.  The cost of the charger itself, especially for high-power DCFC machines, 
5 
can reach as high as $150,000 per charger, according to a whitepaper published by EVgo

7 
the purchase of the charger itself.  Therefore, the Company’s proposed EV charger rebate 
8 
will reduce the upfront cost of of EV charging infrastructure development and lead to the 
9 
deployment of more chargers.

14

Q. 
Does the Panel support the Company’s proposal to create dedicated single points of 
6 
contact for charging station applications?   
7 
A. 
Yes, the Panel supports the establishment of dedicated single points of contact.  Having 
8 
dedicated team members specializing in EV interconnection will help ensure that EV 
9 
charging developers get timely and EV-specific interconnection information.  EV 
10

13 
engineer handling the new service request.  Establishing single points of contact for EV 
14 
interconnection will lead to a better understanding of the unique characteristics of a new 
15

Q.

1

2 
A.

3

4

5

6

7

8

9

11

12 
with the highest electric demand also could impose constraints on the existing electric 
13 
distribution infrastructure.  Conversely, charging fleets during low-demand hours can 
14 
provide grid benefits by increasing the capacity utilization of distribution and generation 
15 
assets.  Off-peak charging also may benefit the transit agency by limiting impacts to 
16 
dispatch scheduling by removing buses from service at times of lower ridership.  The 
17 
costs and potential benefits arising from this incremental load make behavioral strategy 
18 
incentives, such as a smart charging option, effective tools to promote low-demand 
19 
period charging.

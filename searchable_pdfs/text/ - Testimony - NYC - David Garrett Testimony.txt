III.   REGULATORY STANDARDS 
Q. 
Discuss the standard by which regulated utilities are allowed to recover depreciation 
14 
expense. 
15 
A. 
In Lindheimer v. Illinois Bell Telephone Co., the U.S. Supreme Court stated that 
16 
“depreciation is the loss, not restored by current maintenance, which is due to all the factors 
17 
causing the ultimate retirement of the property.  These factors embrace wear and tear, 
18 


--- Page 14 ---
Cases 23-G-0225 and 23-G-0226 
DAVID GARRETT 
 
11 
 
decay, inadequacy, and obsolescence.”1  The Lindheimer Court also recognized that the 
1 
original cost of plant assets, rather than present value or some other measure, is the proper 
2 
basis for calculating depreciation expense.2  Referring to the straight-line method, the 
3 
Lindheimer Court stated that “[a]ccording to the principle of this accounting practice, the 
4 
loss is computed upon the actual cost of the property as entered upon the books, less the 
5 
expected salvage, and the amount charged each year is one year's pro rata share of the total 
6 
amount.”3  The original cost standard was reaffirmed by the Supreme Court in Federal 
7 
Power Commission v. Hope Natural Gas Co.  The Hope Court stated: “Moreover, this 
8 
Court recognized in [Lindheimer], supra, the propriety of basing annual depreciation on 
9 
cost.  By such a procedure the utility is made whole and the integrity of its investment 
10 
maintained.  No more is required.”4 
11 
The Lindheimer Court also held: 
12 
[T]he company has the burden of making a convincing showing that the 
13 
amounts it has charged to operating expenses for depreciation have not been 
14 
excessive. That burden is not sustained by proof that its general accounting 
15 
system has been correct. The calculations are mathematical, but the 
16 
predictions underlying them are essentially matters of opinion.5

Q. 
Discuss the definition and purpose of a depreciation system, as well as the 
4 
depreciation system you employed for this project.  
5 
A. 
The legal standards set forth above do not mandate a specific procedure for conducting 
6 
depreciation analysis.  These standards, however, direct that analysts use a system for 
7 
estimating depreciation rates that will result in the systematic and rational allocation of 
8 
capital recovery for the utility.  Over the years, analysts have developed depreciation 
9 
systems designed to analyze grouped property in accordance with this standard.  A 
10 
depreciation system may be defined by several primary parameters: 1) a method of 
11 
allocation; 2) a procedure for applying the method of allocation; 3) a technique of applying 
12 
the depreciation rate; and 4) a model for analyzing the characteristics of vintage property 
13 
groups.6  In this case, I used the straight-line method, the average life procedure, the whole 
14 
life technique, and the broad group model; this system would be denoted as an “SL-AL-
15 
WL-BG” system.  This depreciation system conforms to the legal standards set forth above.  
16 
I provide a more detailed discussion of depreciation system parameters, theories, and 
17 
equations in Exhibit ___(DJG-16).

V.   SERVICE LIFE ANALYSIS    
Q. 
Please describe the process you used to estimate the service lives of the Company’s 
3 
depreciable assets. 
4 
A. 
To develop depreciation rates for the Company’s mass property accounts, I obtained the 
5 
Company’s historical plant data to analyze the historical retirement patterns derived from 
6 
the data.  This is the same historical data used in the depreciation study.  I used a set of 
7 
empirically-based, standard survivor curves called “Iowa curves” to smooth and complete 
8 
the observed data in order to assess the average life of each account.  The depreciation 
9 
study also relied upon Iowa curves in developing proposed service lives.  More details 
10 
about the Iowa curves are included in my exhibits.  Exhibit ___(DJG-24).       
11 
Q. 
Please describe your approach in using Iowa curves to estimate service life. 
12 
A. 
The historical retirement data are used to develop an OLT for each account.  The data 
13 
points on the OLT can be plotted to form a curve (the “OLT curve”).  The OLT curve is 
14 
not a theoretical survivor curve, rather, it is a curve derived from the Company’s actual 
15 
historical data.  An OLT curve by itself, however, is rarely a smooth curve, and it is often 
16 
not a complete curve.  That is, it does not end at zero percent surviving.  In order to calculate 
17 
average life, a complete survivor curve is needed.  The Iowa curve-fitting process involves 
18 
selecting the best Iowa curve to fit the OLT curve.  This can be accomplished through a 
19 


--- Page 19 ---
Cases 23-G-0225 and 23-G-0226 
DAVID GARRETT 
 
16 
 
combination of visual and mathematical curve-fitting techniques, as well as professional 
1 
judgment.  The first step of my approach to curve-fitting involves visually inspecting the 
2 
OLT curve for any irregularities.  For example, if the tail end of the curve is erratic and 
3 
shows a sharp decline over a short period of time, it may indicate that this portion of the 
4 
data is less reliable, as further discussed below.  After inspecting the OLT curve, I use a 
5 
mathematical curve-fitting technique which essentially involves measuring the distance 
6 
between the OLT curve and the selected Iowa curve in order to get an objective, 
7 
mathematical assessment of how well the curve fits.  After selecting an Iowa curve, I 
8 
observe the OLT curve along with the Iowa curve on the same graph to determine how 
9 
well the curve fits.  I may repeat this process several times for any given account to ensure 
10 
that the most reasonable Iowa curve is selected.  More details about the process of actuarial 
11 
analysis are included in Exhibit ___(DJG-25).          
12 
Q. 
Do you always select the mathematically best-fitting curve? 
13 
A. 
Not necessarily.  Mathematical fitting is an important part of the curve-fitting process 
14 
because it promotes objective, unbiased results.  While mathematical curve fitting is 
15 
important, however, it may not always yield the optimum result; therefore, it should not 
16 
necessarily be adopted without further analysis.  In each of the adjusted accounts discussed 
17 
below, the Iowa curve I selected would result in a closer mathematical fit to the observed 
18 
data.  However, for most of the accounts at issue, I did not select the closest-fitting Iowa 
19 
curve from a mathematical standpoint.  In each case in which I selected an Iowa curve that 
20 
was not the closest fitting curve, I selected an Iowa curve that was shorter, rather than

DAVID GARRETT

approach highlights the balance between mathematical curve fitting and professional 
2 
judgment.

15 
on the size of the exposures.”7  In accordance with this standard, an analyst may decide to 
16 
“truncate” the tail end of the OLT curve at a certain percent of initial exposures, such as 
17 
one percent.  Using this approach puts a greater emphasis on the most valuable portions of

VI.   NET SALVAGE ANALYSIS    
Q. 
Please describe the concept of net salvage.     
3 
A. 
If an asset has any value left when it is retired from service, a utility might decide to sell 
4 
the asset.  The proceeds from this transaction are called “gross salvage.”  The 
5 
corresponding expense associated with the removal of the asset from service is called the 
6 
“cost of removal.”  The term “net salvage” equates to gross salvage less the cost of removal.  
7 
Often, the net salvage for utility assets is a negative number (or percentage) because the 
8 
cost of removing the assets from service exceeds any proceeds received from selling the 
9 
assets.  When a negative net salvage rate is applied to an account to calculate the 
10 
depreciation rate, it results in increasing the total depreciable base to be recovered over a 
11 
particular period of time and increases the depreciation rate.  Therefore, a greater negative 
12 
net salvage rate equates to a higher depreciation rate and expense, all else held constant.  
13

1

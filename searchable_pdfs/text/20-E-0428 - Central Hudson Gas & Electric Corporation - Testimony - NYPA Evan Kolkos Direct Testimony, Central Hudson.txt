The Company’s Climate and Energy Leadership Panel, or Panel, states that the proposed 
18 
CDG Interconnection Program is intended to “encourage the installation of renewable 
19 
generation and remove the barriers to the development of DER.” Specifically, the 
20 
Program is intended to address the “first mover” interconnection problem that has been

2 
Q. 
What is the first mover problem?  
3 
A.  
The first mover problem describes a scenario in New York’s DER market where 
4 
developers are hesitant to pay the full cost for system-level upgrades that are required for 
5 
their project to interconnect to the system. System-level upgrades may benefit subsequent 
6 
interconnecting projects that, under current rules, would be required to share a portion of 
7 
certain substation-level upgrade costs. The “first mover” has to pay the full upfront cost 
8 
for those upgrades and does not know if it will be reimbursed for any part of it or, if so, 
9 
when it would receive those payments. As a result, the “first mover” may not move 
10 
forward with a DER project because the higher upfront costs can make the project 
11 
uneconomic.     
12 
Q. 
Are there other financial barriers to DER development?   
13 
A.  
Yes. Under the current rules for interconnection in New York State, the Standardized 
14 
Interconnection Requirements (SIR), customers must pay for necessary utility 
15 
distribution system upgrades to interconnect a project before the utility begins those 
16 
upgrades. These interconnection costs often represent a substantial portion of a project’s 
17 
budget, with substation upgrades and reconductoring upgrades alone often accounting for 
18 
over half of project costs. High interconnection costs threaten project viability in early 
19 
stages of development and have contributed to the decision not to pursue many projects 
20 
in New York. They are especially threatening to DER project economics given the 
21 
business model under which these projects operate, where the majority of costs are borne 
22

early in project development and revenues are not generated until the DER is fully 
1 
interconnected and able to inject electricity into the grid. 
2 
Q. 
What existing mechanisms, if any, are in place to address the first mover problem? 
3 
A. 
In January 2017, the Commission amended the SIR to adopt a cost-sharing mechanism 
4 
whereby interconnecting projects that bear the full cost for certain substation upgrades 
5 
(First Moving Projects) may be reimbursed by projects on the same substation with 
6 
subsequent interconnection queue positions (Sharing Projects) who also will benefit from 
7 
the upgrades. However, this mechanism has not been successful in supporting DER 
8 
deployment in New York because it provides no guarantee that such reimbursement will 
9 
occur. 
10 
Q. 
Are there other proposals pending to improve the cost-sharing mechanism? 
11 
A.  
Yes. On October 29, 2020, Central Hudson and other members of the IPWG filed with 
12 
the Commission a petition proposing to amend the SIR to incorporate a cost sharing 
13 
proposal that allocates the costs of distribution system upgrades more equitably and 
14 
increases project cost certainty to developers (Cost-Sharing 2.0 Proposal). The exact 
15 
mechanics of participation in Cost-Sharing 2.0 would depend on whether the upgrade 
16 
was initiated by the utility or the market, as well as the specific equipment to be 
17 
upgraded. Generally, projects participating in the Cost-Sharing 2.0 Proposal would pay 
18 
for their pro rata share of hosting capacity increase associated with the upgrade 
19 
(Qualifying Upgrade Charge), rather than the full cost of the upgrade. The Qualifying 
20 
Upgrade Charge would be determined as part of the Coordinated Electric System 
21

Q.  
Please summarize the Company’s proposed CDG Interconnection Program. 
6 
A.  
The Panel proposed the CDG Interconnection Program as a mechanism to address 
7 
barriers to DER interconnection posed by the first mover problem. The CDG 
8 
Interconnection Program would allow DER interconnection costs borne by qualified

that “projects choosing to participate in the CDG Interconnection Program would pay the

17 
interconnection costs.”

To send developers a price signal to discourage development in high-cost areas, 
19 
the CDG Interconnection Program would limit funding for each interconnection upgrade 
20 
to $250,000 per MW, which the Company calculated to represent the approximate 75th 
21 
percentile of interconnection costs for CDG systems in its service territory. As proposed,

III.  SUPPORT FOR THE CDG INTERCONNECTION PROGRAM 
12 
Q. 
Would the proposed CDG Interconnection Program help address barriers to DER 
13 
development in New York?  
14 
A. 
Yes. The proposed CDG Interconnection Program will address two specific barriers to 
15 
DER development: (i) high interconnection costs that must be paid upfront, before a 
16 
project starts generating revenues; and, (ii) uncertainty over whether any portion of the 
17 
cost may be recovered in the future from a Sharing Project.   
18 
Q. 
How would the proposed CDG Interconnection Program help to mitigate the effect 
19 
of high upfront interconnection costs on DER development? 
20 
A. 
The CDG Interconnection Program addresses this market barrier by providing developers 
21 
with an option to amortize the interconnection costs associated with system upgrades

3 
the Program would allow developers to better align project costs with revenue streams,

Q. 
Please describe your recommendation pertaining to the relationship between the 
5 
proposed CDG Interconnection and Cost-Sharing 2.0 Programs. 
6 
A. 
For any project that opts into both the proposed CDG Interconnection and Cost-Sharing 
7 
2.0 Programs, the average interconnection costs used to determine the Repayment Charge 
8 
should be based on the Qualifying Upgrade Charge presented in the CESIR, not the full 
9 
cost of the upgrade. Because the Cost Sharing 2.0 Program effectively splits the 
10 
interconnection costs for certain upgrades between Triggering and Sharing Projects, this 
11 
arrangement will ensure that interconnection costs used to determine the Repayment 
12

13 
Additionally, it will ensure that interconnection costs are not “double counted” when 
14 
determining the Repayment Charge amount. Because the Qualifying Upgrade Charge 
15 
would be developed as part of the existing CESIR process under the Cost Sharing 2.0 
16 
Program, this arrangement should be no more difficult for the Company to administer 
17 
than one where the Repayment Charge is based on overall interconnection costs. Thus, 
18 
the Company should be directed, for any project that opts into both the proposed CDG 
19 
Interconnection and Cost-Sharing 2.0 Programs, to use an average interconnection cost 
20 
that is based on the Qualifying Upgrade Charge contained in the CESIR.

Q. 
Do you have a concern regarding the proposed CDG Interconnection Program’s 
1 
payment structure? 
2 
A. 
Yes. According to the Company’s response to NYPA-17, the Repayment Charge would 
3 
consist of a monthly charge based on the size of the project and the average 
4 
interconnection costs at the time the developer enters the program. This program design 
5 
effectively allows projects with above-average interconnection costs to benefit from 
6 
pooling with projects that have below-average interconnection costs. In other words, the 
7 
proposal to socialize interconnection costs should help to lower the interconnection cost 
8 
for projects with above-average system upgrade costs. As noted by the Company in its 
9 
response to NYPA-17, however, “projects choosing to participate in the CDG 
10 
Interconnection Program would pay the average interconnection costs, even if their 
11 
particular project costs are below the average interconnection costs.” 
12 
This arrangement is likely to drive up the overall cost of participation in the 
13 
Program because projects with significantly lower-than-average interconnection costs 
14 
may opt out of the program instead of choosing to pay a substantial premium on their 
15 
interconnection costs to participate. The first chart in Exhibit__(EK-3) illustrates how 
16 
projects in the Company’s interconnection queue from 2016 to 2019 that would be 
17 
eligible to have their entire interconnection costs financed through the proposed CDG 
18 
Interconnection Program would over- or under-pay to participate in the CDG 
19 
Interconnection Program, based on NYPA’s understanding of the Company’s proposed 
20 
repayment structure.

Q. 
What do you recommend to address this issue? 
1 
A. 
The Company should identify a participation model whereby the Repayment Charge is 
2 
aligned more equitably with actual interconnection costs. It then should modify the 
3 
proposed CDG Interconnection Program to address this problem. One option the 
4 
Company could pursue is to establish three “tiers” for upgrades participating in the CDG 
5 
Interconnection Program that would be differentiated by interconnection cost (Tiered 
6 
Repayment Model). The average interconnection cost used to determine a Repayment 
7 
Charge could then be based on the average interconnection cost of projects in each tier, 
8 
rather than all projects participating in the Program. This would ensure that Repayment 
9 
Charges align more closely with expected project interconnection costs and reduce the 
10 
extent to which below-average cost projects pay a premium to participate in the Program.  
11 
Alternatively, the Company could establish a cap on the premium that a project 
12 
would pay to participate in the Program (Capped Repayment Model). The cap could be 
13 
structured as a percentage of overall project costs. Participating projects would pay either 
14 
the cap or the average interconnection cost, whichever is lower. This would ensure that 
15 
lower cost projects can participate without paying an excessive premium to do so, and the 
16 
Program still would provide an avenue for higher-cost projects to benefit from paying the 
17 
socialized interconnection cost.

II. 
LIFE SPAN METHOD 
1 
Q. 
What is the life span method? 
2 
A. 
The life span method is a method of determining average service lives and remaining lives 
3 
for large facilities, such as gas storage plants or electric generating plants, for which all 
4 
assets are expected to be retired concurrently at the date of the retirement of the entire 
5 
facility.  In this method, the survivor characteristics of such facilities are described using 
6 
interim survivor curves and estimated probable retirement dates.  The interim survivor 
7 
curves describe the rate of retirement related to the replacement of elements of the facility, 
8 
such as the retirements of piping, pumps, and other assets that occur during the life of a 
9 
facility such as a power plant.  The probable retirement date provides the rate of final 
10 
retirement for each year of installation for the facility by truncating the interim survivor 
11 
curve for each installation year at its attained age at the date of probable retirement.  The 
12 
use of interim survivor curves truncated at the date of probable retirement provides a 
13 
consistent method for estimating the lives of the multiple years of installation for a 
14 
particular facility inasmuch as a single concurrent retirement for all years of installation 
15 
will occur when it is retired. 
16 
 
17 
Q. 
Why is the life span method the preferred method for these types of facilities? 
18 
A. 
The life span method accounts for the fact that upon the final retirement of a facility, all 
19 
assets will be retired -- whether brand new or dating to the original installation of the 
20 
facility.  Using a mass property approach (i.e., not using an estimated retirement date) will 
21

Q. 
Are depreciation estimates based on purely analytical or mathematical 
1 
computations? 
2 
A. 
No.  Even in depreciation studies where there are not considerations similar to the 
3 
CLCPA, depreciation is a forecast of the future that requires judgment and cannot be 
4 
based on a purely quantitative basis.  For example, NARUC makes clear that there must 
5 
be a subjective component to estimating service lives.  Chapter XIII of Public Utility 
6 
Depreciation Practices, entitled “Actuarial Life Analysis,” discusses and emphasizes the 
7 
subjective nature of the process of estimating service lives.  NARUC starts this chapter 
8 
by explaining that the analysis of historical data is only one part of the process of 
9 
estimating service lives: 
10 
Actuarial analysis objectively measures how the company has retired its 
11 
investment.  The analyst must then judge whether this historical view 
12 
depicts the future life of the property in service.  The analyst takes into 
13 
consideration various factors, such as changes in technology, services 
14 
provided, or capital budgets.  
15 
 
16 
Thus, NARUC clarifies that the process of estimating service lives is more than merely a 
17 
simple mathematical measurement of the past.  In describing the determination of a 
18 
survivor curve estimate (referred to as the “projection life” in this passage), NARUC 
19 
states: 
20 
The projection life is a projection, or forecast, of the future of the property.  
21 
Historical indications may be useful in estimating a projection life curve.  
22 
Certainly the observations based on the property’s history are a starting 
23 
point.  Trends in life or retirement dispersion can often be expected to 
24 
continue.  Likewise, unless there is some reason to expect otherwise, 
25 
stability in life or retirement dispersion can be expected to continue, at least 
26 
in the near term. 
27 
 
28 


--- Page 17 ---
Testimony of Ned W. Allis 
Page 15 of 24 
Depreciation analysts should avoid becoming ensnared in the mechanics of 
1 
the historical life study and relying solely on mathematical solutions.  The 
2 
reason for making an historical life analysis is to develop a sufficient 
3 
understanding of history in order to evaluate whether it is a reasonable 
4 
predictor of the future.  The importance of being aware of circumstances 
5 
having direct bearing on the reason for making an historical life analysis 
6 
cannot be understated.  These circumstances, when factored into the 
7 
analysis, determine the application and limitations of an historical life 
8 
analysis.   
9 
10 
NARUC advises against focusing only on the statistical analysis of historical data, stating 
11 
that “relying solely on mathematical solutions” should be avoided.  Further, we can be 
12 
quite certain that the future will be different from the past and, therefore, we should expect 
13 
that service life estimates should differ from the Companies’ historical experience.

Are your estimates consistent with accepted methods for estimating net salvage? 
Yes.  The industry standard method for estimating net salvage, which is accepted by most 
regulatory commissions (including the Commission) and supported by depreciation 
textbooks, is to estimate net salvage based on informed judgment that incorporates 
statistical analysis of historical net salvage data in which historical net salvage (cost of 
removal less gross salvage) is expressed as a percentage or ratio of historical retirements. 
This method, which I will refer to as the “traditional method,” is the method used in 
the Companies depreciation study. 
Have Staff and NYC followed the same approach as you related to proposing net 
salvage estimates? 
In part, yes.  Staff Witness Darmetko and NYC Witness Garrett have, for the most part 
employed a similar approach.  Staff states (at 18-20) that their “review of the net salvage 
statistics employed a method very similar to that used by the Company in determining its 
net salvage factors.”  Based on my review of Staff’s testimony and workpapers, I agree 
that for the most part Staff used a similar approach, although Staff appears more reluctant

II. OVERVIEW OF TESTIMONY 
6 
Q. 
Please provide an overview of the Panel’s testimony. 
7 
A. 
Electricity is an essential service that directly impacts our customers’ quality of life and 
8 
the electric system continues to play an increasingly vital role in public policy (such as 
9 
policies designed to fight climate change) and the advancement of technology.  The 
10 
operation and maintenance of our system is critical to providing customers safe and 
11 
reliable electric service.  However, the operational practices that served the system well 
12 
in the past are becoming less and less applicable in light of: 
13 
1) NYSEG’s pervasive exposure to trees, the single largest source of outages at the only 
14 
major electric utility in New York that does not have at least a five-year full-cycle 
15 
distribution vegetation management program; 
16 
2) A rapidly increasing system wide exposure to equipment failures, driven by large 
17 
populations of assets progressing deeper into advanced service age; and 
18 
3) An upsurge in the demand and complexity of system operation and control 
19 
requirements, particularly with respect to compliance, security, and new technologies.   
20 
Moreover, NYSEG’s outdated System Average Interruption Frequency Index 
21 
(“SAIFI”) target is increasingly unachievable without changes to expenditure levels, line 
22 
clearance programs, and target calculations.  These challenges continue to evolve and, if

IV. CHALLENGES IMPACTING ELECTRIC SYSTEM OPERATIONS  
1 
AND RELIABILITY 
2 
Q. 
Are the operations of the electric system changing? 
3 
A. 
Yes.  The electric system has always been a principal enabler to advancements in 
4 
technology, industrialization, and economic development.  People, companies, and 
5 
communities are becoming more dependent upon reliable, consistent energy – especially 
6 
as we move to a more electrified world.  The quality and availability of electric service,  
7 
particularly for industrialized businesses as well as for people who are more reliant on 
8 
electronic devices (like smartphones) must meet a far wider variety of expectations.  The 
9 
electric system is the backbone to advancements in smart grid technologies, distributed 
10 
energy resources (“DER”), communication systems, and a society that increasingly 
11 
emphasizes online connectivity.  Concurrent with this evolution, vast segments of the 
12 
electric system are undergoing replacement and modernization.  The Companies are also 
13 
experiencing acute reliability challenges.  All of this contributes to changes in the day-to-
14 
day operations of the electric system.  As the complexity and materiality of these changes 
15 
continue to increase, they present a variety of operational challenges. 
16 
Q. 
Please describe these operational challenges. 
17 
A. 
The challenges fall into the following four categories: 
18 
1) Pervasive tree contacts at NYSEG; 
19 
2) Exposure to aging infrastructure; 
20 
3) Executing a large and dynamic workplan; and 
21 
4) Operational integrity and responsiveness.

complexity of the impact on electric system operations.

A. Pervasive Tree Contacts at NYSEG 
1 
Q. 
Are tree contacts a principal risk to NYSEG’s electric system? 
2 
A. 
Yes.  Trees have always been a principal risk to the NYSEG system.  NYSEG is the only 
3 
major electric utility in New York that is not on at least a five-year full cycle distribution 
4 
vegetation management program.  In the 2019 Rate Case, the Commission authorized 
5 
NYSEG to implement a new Reclamation Program and a new Danger Tree Program. 
6 
While both programs have proven to be highly effective, NYSEG is still far from having 
7 
its distribution vegetation management program on an adequate trim cycle.  The 
8 
Vegetation Management Panel discusses the proposal for expanding NYSEG’s 
9 
distribution vegetation management program, which is critical to managing NYSEG’s 
10 
increasing exposure to tree contacts.  We focus in our testimony on the reliability aspects 
11 
of NYSEG’s pervasive exposure to tree contacts.

Q. 
How does equipment performance change over the course of its design life? 
1 
A. 
As the assets progress through their design life and become more aged, their physical and 
2 
electrical integrity deteriorates and they become increasingly prone to failure.  In addition 
3 
to a higher likelihood of failure, the mode and timing of their failure become more 
4 
unpredictable.  Therefore, as assets age, their ability to perform reliably begins to 
5 
deteriorate.  This deterioration (i.e., exposure to failure) is typical to any type of 
6 
infrastructure – gas pipelines, bridges, highways, water systems, heavy equipment, etc.   
7 
Q. 
Does this exposure to failure occur in phases? 
8 
A. 
Yes.  During the early stages of an asset’s design life, the primary exposure to failure is 
9 
related to material defects or design/installation problems.  This is sometimes referred to 
10 
as “infant mortality” and the period of exposure is generally short.  The middle stage is 
11 
typically the longest and most stable period.  Assets in this stage have relatively 
12 
predictable performance.  The primary exposure to failure during this phase is “wear and 
13 
tear,” and periodic maintenance helps manage this exposure.  The late phase is 
14 
characterized by an increasing likelihood of failure, generally attributable to the assets 
15 
starting to wear out.  As assets progress deeper into this phase, asset failure can become 
16 
harder to predict, with a potential to occur suddenly and sometimes catastrophically. 
17 
Q. 
Is age the only indicator of increasing exposure to failure? 
18 
A. 
No.  While age (i.e., time in service) correlates to an increasing likelihood of failure, it 
19 
alone is not a holistic indicator of exposure to failure.  The condition of the asset, and the 
20 
way this condition changes over time, is far more insightful.  This is sometimes referred 
21 
to as “service aging.”  While service aging can result in an asset exceeding its expected 
22 
design life, failure remains an increasing and inevitable certainty.

Q. 
What type of performance indicator is SAIFI? 
12 
A. 
SAIFI is considered a “lagging indicator.”  A lagging indicator is rearward focused and 
13 
measures performance that has already occurred (i.e., it changes in correlation with 
14 
changes in actual performance).  Lagging indicators are useful in determining whether a 
15 
desired performance has been achieved.  In the case of SAIFI, the indicator looks at the 
16 
customer interruptions that have occurred over the course of a year, and it can be 
17

compared to both performance targets and prior period results.  While lagging indicators 
1 
help confirm trends and changes in trends, they are typically insufficient, on their own, 
2 
for indicating why the trend or changes in trends are happening.  This is where leading 
3 
indicators become important. 
4 
Q. 
What are leading indicators? 
5 
A. 
A leading indicator looks forward at the factors that are likely to influence future 
6 
performance.  Leading indicators provide context for the actions and conditions that are 
7 
pertinent to achieving a targeted level of performance.  For example, we have discussed 
8 
pervasive tree contacts and their effect on performance (as measured through the lagging 
9 
indicator SAIFI).  Some of the pertinent leading indicators would include: the extent of 
10 
tree exposure on a system; the degree of change in this exposure; how much of the 
11 
system is subject to periodic trimming; the scope and execution of periodic trimming; and 
12 
the ability to mitigate the effects of tree contacts (via e.g., reclosers, tree wire, and 
13 
sectionalizing).  Leading indicators provide context for factors that are likely to impact 
14 
performance (favorably or unfavorably).  Lagging indicators measure the factors that 
15 
have affected actual performance. 
16 
Q.

17

19

20 
target levels (and/or the methods for achieving the targets) would need to change.  
21 
Leading indicators can inform the need and extent of such change.  The previous sections 
22 
on tree contacts and aging infrastructure are examples of a need for change.

V. 
Price Responsiveness and Rate Design 
15 
Q.  
How can electric tariffs be designed in order to ensure loads are managed in a 
16 
cost-effective manner? 
17 
A. 
Electricity rates send price signals to the electric customer regarding how the 
18 
customer could behave, so as to minimize their electric bill. Optimally, these price 
19 
signals reflect the cost that the consumption places on the system and show how 
20 
shifting away from high-priced times would also help reduce total costs (including 
21 
generation and distribution system costs).  
22 


--- Page 22 ---
 
 
20-E-0380  
 
 
Direct Testimony of Elisheba Spiller 
20-G-0381 
                                                       
20 
 
The structure of the prices, that is, the tariff, and not just the prices alone, will also 
1 
affect the charging decisions. For example, tariffs that do not have time variation 
2 
send a signal to the customer that shifting consumption across hours is unimportant; 
3 
the customer facing a non-time-variant price will not attempt to shift consumption 
4 
because they receive no benefit from doing so. Tariffs that have some time variation 
5 
thus signal when consumption should be reduced (during high-priced times) or 
6 
when would be an appropriate time to consume (during low-priced times).  
7 
This holds true whether the price signal is on demand (kW) or consumption (kWh). 
8 
Indeed, tariffs can have time-variant demand-based charges, and/or time-variant 
9 
volumetric charges; however, depending on which type of charge is levied, the 
10 
response will also vary because the signal sent to the customer is different.  
11 
A time-variant volumetric rate sends the customer a signal to reduce consumption 
12 
(or kWh) during high priced times. A time-variant demand-based rate, on the other 
13 
hand, sends the customer a signal to reduce maximum demands (kW) during high- 
14 
priced times. Though similar, the resulting effect and impacts on the distribution 
15 
system can be quite different, especially when very large batteries (or fleets of 
16 
batteries) are being charged. Essentially, under a time-variant demand-based rate 
17 
the fleet owner will attempt to mitigate demand by charging over longer time 
18 
periods at a lower rate of charge (managed charging software can help enable this 
19 
reduction in maximum demands). However, under a time-variant volumetric rate, 
20 
the fleet owner could simply shift the charging into the off-peak period without 
21 
attempting to reduce the maximum demand caused by the vehicles charging.  
22

20-G-0381

Overall, both of these types of time-variant pricing mechanisms provide the 
1 
customer with an incentive to reduce charging during peak times; however, a time-
2 
variant demand charge would also be more likely to reduce maximum demands 
3 
during off-peak times. This will be especially important for very large fleets of EVs, 
4 
whose maximum demands could become a significant concern for the distribution 
5 
system, even if they occur during off-peak hours. As New York moves towards 
6 
greater adoption of large EVs, this concern will attain greater importance. However, 
7 
in the near term, when individual fleet customers are unlikely to pose very high 
8 
demand during off-peak periods, incentivizing fleet customers to manage their off-
9 
peak demand will be less important.

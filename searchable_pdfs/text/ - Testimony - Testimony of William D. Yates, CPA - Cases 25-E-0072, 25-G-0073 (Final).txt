Definitions 
13 
Q.  
What is “ACS Microdata”? 
14 
A. 
The American Community Survey (“ACS”) microdata consist of individual records with 
15 
information about the characteristics of each person and housing unit in the survey. The 
16 
ACS Public Use Microdata Sample (“PUMS”) includes a subsample of the ACS microdata, 
17 
excluding personalized information. The PUMS represents about two-thirds of the 
18 
responses collected in the ACS in a specific 1-year or 5-year period.1 
19 
Q.  
What are “Public Use Microdata Areas”? 
20 
A. 
Public Use Microdata Areas (“PUMAs”) are non-overlapping statistical geographic areas, 
21 
each comprised of a collection of U.S. census tracts, that partition each state or equivalent 
22 
entity into geographic areas containing no fewer than 100,000 people each.2 
23

Findings 
4 
Q.  
What are the major findings of your testimony in these proceedings? 
5 
A. 
My testimony examines affordability and customer service matters in several areas 
6 
including but not limited to evidence that: 
7 
1. Under the Company’s one-year proposal, the average monthly electric delivery bill 
8 
increase for residential customers would be about 19%. For residential gas, the increase 
9 
would be approximately 25%. Over three years, cumulative electric and gas delivery 
10 
bill impacts would approach 42% and 45%, respectively. Con Edison also proposes to 
11 
raise monthly fixed charges for both electric and gas service. 
12 
2. Under the Company’s proposals, and assuming Con Edison’s Rate Year budget for 
13 
EAP does not exceed the Commission’s 2% Cap, delivery bills for Tier 1 electric EAP 
14 
participants with average monthly usage up to 300 kWh would decrease; while 
15 
participants with monthly usage of 375 kWh would see their delivery bills increase by 
16 
about $2.00. For gas, average EAP heating delivery bills increases would likely be 
17 
more than offset for all tiers. In contrast, average usage participants in all non-heating 
18 
gas tiers would experience 17% increases in their delivery bills. 
19 
3. The average number of EAP program participants in arrears rose 9% from 2019 through 
20 
March 2025, while the amount these participants owed rose collectively by 133% (from 
21 
$109 million to $253 million), and per customer by 114% ($1,796 vs. $839). The 
22 
percentage of EAP participants in arrears remained relatively stable, in the range of 
23 
30% - 31%. 
24 
4. In contrast, the number of non-EAP accounts past due increased 89% from 2019 
25 
through March 2025, while the amount these customers owed rose collectively by 
26 
214% (from $205 million to $643 million), and per customer by 66% ($2,060 vs. 
27 
$1,243). The percentage of Non-EAP customers in arrears nearly doubled, from about 
28 
6% to almost 12%.  
29

5. The number of Con Edison residential customers experiencing service termination 
1 
since the resumption of collections activity had been limited, until March when the 
2 
Company terminated service to almost 15,000 residential customers — the highest

monthly number in over twenty years — only to followed by almost 29,000 residential 
4 
terminations in April, for a two-month total of almost 44,000 shut-offs. Monthly 
5 
terminations could rise further for those customers not resolving their arrears by 
6 
entering into HEFPA Deferred Payment Agreements (“DPAs”) and/or receiving 
7 
sufficient bill payment assistance through DSS, entry into EAP, or by other means. 
8 
However, and importantly, it is encouraging to note that the Company increased the 
9 
number of outstanding DPAs from March 2024 to April 2025 by 44%, or 62,000, to 
10 
204,500; and that more than half of the residential customers experiencing service 
11 
termination in March and April were able to get re-connected by entering into DPAs. 
12 
It is also highly significant that, net, 15,500 more customers had their service re-
13 
connected in March and April than had their service terminated (59,500 re-connections 
14 
versus 44,000 shut-offs). That said, the termination and reconnection dynamics of this 
15 
new phase of collections activity are at an early stage, and I remain very concerned that 
16 
many vulnerable customers who cannot afford their utility bills may ultimately be 
17 
included amongst those whose service is terminated; and/or who are unable to satisfy 
18 
the terms of the DPAs they have entered into to resolve their arrears.

6. At the federal level, consideration is being given to reducing or eliminating 
20 
expenditures for many assistance programs, most notably HEAP, Supplemental 
21 
Nutrition Assistance Program (“SNAP”), and Medicaid. Many low-income utility 
22 
customers depend on HEAP and Emergency HEAP for financial assistance when they 
23 
fall behind. This comes just as demand for HEAP among New Yorkers has put the 
24 
State’s already limited resources under strain. Curtailment or elimination of these 
25 
programs could have a concomitant negative impact on EAP enrollment. It is possible 
26 
that recently enacted statewide matching legislation, together with efforts to establish 
27 
an “Enhanced EAP” program, as currently being considered in Cases 14-M-0565 and 
28 
23-M-0298, could mitigate some of the negative consequences of any federal actions 
29 
that reduce low-income assistance.

Problems with Landlord Accounts and Multiple Dwellings 
1 
Q. 
Please explain what the rules are around multiple dwellings where the Con Edison 
2 
account is in the landlord, owner, or property manager’s name and service may be 
3 
disconnected for nonpayment, which will affect the tenants residing at the premise.  
4 
A.  
In New York State, Section 235-a of the New York Real Property Law and Public Service 
5 
Law Sections 33 and 116 when the utility service belongs to an account holder who is the 
6 
landlord, owner or property manager’s name (hereinafter referred as “landlord accounts”) 
7 
and that service benefits tenants. Such service could be to common areas, such as lights in 
8 
the hallways or the electricity needed to safely operate the elevator. It also could be direct 
9 
electric or gas to the tenants’ units, such as cooking gas or heating to the apartment. When 
10 
the landlord falls behind on their payments, the utility can proceed with the termination of 
11 
service, but they must provide at least fifteen days’ notice to the tenants prior to conducting 
12 
the disconnection. The tenants can stop the termination of service in two ways, depending 
13 
on the number of total units. When there are less than 3 units, the tenants can pay the utility 
14 
directly and then offset the payment from their rent. When there are 3 or more units, the 
15 
tenants may form a tenant’s association, collecting payment from their fellow tenants, and 
16 
then pay the Company directly.

the account is in the landlord’s name. In PULP’s experience, the tenants are essentially

22 
am unaware of any instances in which tenants of a multiple dwelling with more than three

monthly payments moving forward.

1. 
Introduction 
 
On October 8, 2021, Administrative Law Judge (“ALJs”) Costello and ALJ Moreno 
issued a Ruling on Schedule and Process, setting October 22, 2021 as the date for parties to 
submit statements in support or opposition to the Joint Proposal filed by Niagara Mohawk Power 
Corporation d/b/a National Grid’s (“National Grid”) on September 27, 2021. Mission:data 
Coalition (“Mission:data”) hereby submits its statement in opposition pursuant to said October 8, 
2021 ruling. 
 
 
 
2. 
The Joint Proposal is Silent on the Most Significant Developments in Advanced 
Metering to Occur in Over a Decade, Raising Anti-Competitive Concerns and Risking 
Ratepayer Benefits 
As a general matter, Mission:data supports advanced metering infrastructure (“AMI”) 
deployment because it is a prerequisite for high penetrations of distributed energy resources 
(“DERs”) and for achieving the goals of the Commission’s Reforming the Energy Vision 
(“REV”) initiative. In particular, Mission:data supports several aspects of National Grid’s AMI

proposals to date such as the fact that National Grid has committed to provide Green Button 
Connect by March 31, 2021.1 That said, however, Mission:data must oppose the Joint Proposal 
for the following reasons. Despite ensuring millions in cost recovery and profits for National 
Grid relating to AMI investments, the Joint Proposal does not address the most important aspects 
of advanced meters that provide value to consumers: namely, the ability for customers to take 
maximum advantage of their data-sharing capabilities so that consumers can access energy-
saving products and services. National Grid will be among the first utilities in the nation to 
deploy advanced meters with “distributed intelligence” capabilities, which is defined as 
generalized computing power on the meter itself. Load disaggregation and Wifi capabilities are 
“apps” that can be loaded onto the meter. In testimony, Mission:data highlighted the 
unscrutinized novelty of these technologies; the fact that National Grid has provided only scant 
details about the meters’ new technical capabilities, a total of only six (6) sentences; and that the 
Commission is severely under-informed about the technology’s potential for imminent anti-
competitive harm to DERs in New York.2 The Joint Proposal addresses none of these critical 
concerns and merely continues a “business as usual” scenario in which National Grid is assured 
of cost recovery and profits. For these reasons, Mission:data urges the Commission to reject the 
Joint Proposal.  
Mission:data’s concerns regarding anti-competitive harms are real and imminent. In 
testimony, Mission:data presented several ways in which National Grid could discriminate 
against DERs by restricting access to information, crippling features, and self-preferencing 
National Grid’s products and services at the expense of DER providers. It could be very difficult 
or impossible for a competitive market to develop in New York around load disaggregation 
services or real-time, Wifi-based energy analysis if National Grid offers its own services and 
denies access to DER competitors. For example, National Grid states cryptically that it would 
only allow “compatible” Wifi devices to receive real-time data from advanced meters; the clear 
implication of this vague use of the word “compatible” means that National Grid can “pick

of anti-competitive behavior is not speculative: Xcel Energy has promised Wifi-based advanced 
meters to its customers in Colorado but only allows customers to view real-time usage data

ecosystems” and “enabling innovation” and “future proofing” when it comes to National Grid’s 
AMI plans, but the reality is that these promises will fall flat if National Grid is permitted to 
recover costs and earn profits without regulatory scrutiny of these novel technological features.  
At the root of these concerns is that the Commission has not clearly defined the 
boundaries of regulated monopoly service as it relates to distributed intelligence and Wifi 
capabilities. Approving the Joint Proposal would be to grant National Grid a de facto expansion 
of its monopoly into novel areas, such as load analysis and disaggregation on the meter, and 
device control from the meter. The Commission has never deemed these sub-markets to be 
“natural monopolies,” nor has the Commission ever granted National Grid a license to be the 
exclusive service provider in these markets. Approval of the Joint Proposal will inevitably lead 
to a kind of technological somnambulism in which the Commission is only able to exercise 
oversight in order to ensure fair competition after it is too late to take meaningful action.  
Finally, Mission:data opposes the Joint Proposal because achieving open, non-
discriminatory access to the benefits of advanced meters will inevitably cost ratepayers a lot 
more in the future than if it is addressed head-on now in the present docket. Once advanced 
meter vendors have a utility such as National Grid under contract, the vendor gains substantial 
negotiating leverage in subsequent changes because the cost of ripping out the vendor’s products 
is prohibitive. The result is that “change orders” from vendors become overpriced due to the lack 
of competitive discipline. In testimony, Mission:data cited California as a cautionary tale because 
one utility in the Golden State insisted on a $41.90 charge per meter simply in order to allow 
residential customers to participate in a competitive demand response market.4 The result – 
convenient for the utility and serving its strategic interests – was an un-level playing field in

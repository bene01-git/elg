POINT II:  
The Proposed EAMs Are Contrary to the Public 
Interest and Should Be Rejected 
 
 
The Joint Proposal advances four proposed electric EAMs: (1) Solar DER Utilization 
(“Solar EAM”); (2) Storage DER Utilization (“Storage EAM”); (3) Demand Response EAM 
(“DR EAM”); and (4) Electric Vehicle CO2 Reduction (“EV EAM”).  (JP at 51-52, App. X; Tr. 
189-190.)  The EAMs are positive-only electric incentives with a potential total cost of 
approximately $56 million.  (Id., App. X at 2-4, 13.)  For the reasons set forth below, the 
proposed EAMs are unnecessary, contrary to the public interest, and should be rejected. 
 
EAMs first were introduced in a 2016 order issued in the Reforming the Energy Vision, 
or “REV,” proceeding.23  Importantly, much has changed since that decision was issued.  Indeed, 
EAMs were “thought of as a bridge” and that “through the opportunity to earn from platform 
service revenues that produce sustained value to end-use customers and shareholders, the need to 
establish specific EAMs to accomplish the same consumer benefit will diminish.”24  The EAMs 
proposed here, however, differ substantially from those envisioned by the Commission,25 and 
any “platform service revenues” being earned for customer benefit appear negligible at best.  
Perhaps the most significant change impacting any perceived “need” for EAMs is the enactment 
of the Climate Leadership and Community Protection Act (“CLCPA”).  The CLCPA contains 
specific mandates with respect to, inter alia, solar generation and storage capacity and, as a result 
of that legislation and related activities (e.g., issuance of the Final Scoping Plan), there is no 
dispute that promotion of demand response and electric vehicles (“EVs”) also are consistent with

The Solar EAM, for instance, incentivizes the Companies based on the amount of solar 
capacity that is installed and interconnected in their service territories.  (JP, App. X at 9-10.)  As 
Staff acknowledged, however, customers currently fund solar incentives and deployment in New 
York “is driven by those programs.”  (Tr. 191-192.)  The cost of building solar projects also plays 
a large role in terms of how much is developed.  (Tr. 192.)  Thus, although the amount of solar 
capacity that is developed primarily is a function of incentives and development costs, the Solar 
EAM would reward the Companies, at customer expense, for interconnecting solar projects to 
the grid.  Significantly, however, as Staff concedes, (i) the interconnection of solar projects is 
consistent with State and Commission policy, (ii) the Companies already are obligated to 
interconnect such projects, and (iii) the Commission has interconnection standards and 
requirements with which the Companies are required to comply.  (Tr. 193.)  In fact, to the extent 
there are a growing number of solar projects for the Companies to interconnect, the Joint 
Proposal provides funding for increased employees (Tr. 193-194), and the Companies’ 
interconnection costs are borne by project developers (Tr. 197).

Quite simply, there is no compelling need for the EAMs proposed herein.  The objectives 
of the proposed EAMs already are being supported heavily by customers, and NYSEG and 
RG&E are seeking three consecutive years of electric Levelized Net Base Delivery Increases of 
17.1 percent and 11.0 percent, respectively, raising the issue of how even-greater collections 
from customers possibly could be considered in the public interest.28  Indeed, the specific 
activities that the Companies would be expected to undertake to “earn” such EAMs are activities 
that they already are required to do and are mandated by State and/or Commission policies. 
CONCLUSION 
 
For the reasons set forth herein and in Multiple Intervenors’ prior submissions, the 
proposed electric and gas revenue requirements, and the resulting delivery rate impacts on 
customers, are exorbitant, contrary to the public interest, and require material moderation in 
these proceedings.  The Joint Proposal advances numerous priorities other than, or in preference 
to, customer affordability, and the Commission needs to rebalance these priorities to accord 
much greater emphasis on maintaining affordability for all types of customers.  For numerous 
reasons, the proposed EAMs should be rejected as unnecessary and without merit.

Depreciation
The Company proposes that it be permitted to accelerate cost recovery (depreciation)
for those assets which are likely to have their useful economic lives reduced below his-
torical expectations as a result of the imperatives established by the Climate Leadership
and Community Protection Act (CLCPA).1 Speciﬁcally, they propose that all assets which
would normally be assigned an Average Service Life (ASL) extending beyond 2050 should
be assigned ASLs that ensure full cost-recovery before 2050.
The year 2050 is that year by which the CLCPA requires that aggregate New York State
carbon emission equivalents be reduced to at least 85% of their levels in 1990.2 Given
that natural gas combustion is a primary source of such emissions, and given that nat-
ural gas combustion has increased signiﬁcantly since 1990, achievement of the CLCPA’s
mandatory emission limits is likely to require more than an 85% reduction in emissions
from natural gas combustion in New York State and within the territory served by the
Company. As the Company testiﬁed, the CLCPA "will reduce gas use, revenue and ac-
cess to external capital."3 No evidence to the contrary was presented by any party to this
case.
As the volume of sales is reduced, the costs associated with the Company’s assets, which
were designed and funded to serve a higher volume of sales, will not be reduced. Be-
cause costs are recovered from ratepayers based on the volume of their use, the in-
evitable result of reduced sales is that rates will need to be increased to reﬂect a higher
allocation of ﬁxed costs to each unit of gas sold. If nothing is done to reduce the quantity
of undepreciated assets, future rate increases will be dramatic and unsustainable.
The Company wisely argues that "It is imperative that depreciable lives are reconciled
with the expectations of the CLCPA in this case, rather than delaying such action."4 Such
a reconciliation would be consistent with generally accepted principles of utility rate
design since ensuring that depreciable lives reﬂect reasonable expectations of the eco-
nomic life of assets ensures that ratepayers pay for their fair share of those costs which
were incurred for their beneﬁt. If depreciable lives are longer than the reasonably ex-
pected economic lives of assets, then future rate payers will be required to bear an unfair
portion of the cost of assets which served earlier ratepayers paying lower rates. Addition-
ally, if ratepayers abandon gas service, as many are expected to do, then the remaining
ratepayers will be required to pay for that portion of the Company’s assets which had
been installed for the use by those departed ratepayers. Given that a large portion of the
Company’s assets is services,5, regulators, and meters which serve only one customer,

Any delay in reconciling the depreciable lives of the Company’s assets with the expec-
tations created by the CLCPA will result in an increase in the inequitable burden borne
by future ratepayers for assets which beneﬁted earlier ratepayers. Such a delay would
not be consistent with the Commission’s requirement to ensure that rates are just, rea-
sonable, and non-discriminatory. Ensuring that customers must pay the costs of service
which beneﬁtted others is neither just nor reasonable and discriminates against future
rate payers for the beneﬁt of today’s ratepayers.
While reduction of the ASLs used to compute cost-recovery would result in signiﬁcant
rate increases in the current rate case, those rate increases will be signiﬁcantly less than
those required in future rate cases if no reconciliation is provided. The more rapidly the
assets are depreciated, the more closely their depreciation rates match their actual ASLs,
the lesser will be the need to raise rates in the future. Thus, reducing ASLs and increasing
the rate of cost-recovery in the current rate case ensures not only a more equitable allo-
cation of costs, it also reduces the magnitude of need for future rate increases. The more
years over which these costs can be spread, the less painful will be the burden of paying
those costs. As the Company’s CFO stated: "the quicker that we incorporate this legisla-
tion [the CLCPA] into our rates, the less cost per year it would be to our customers."6
It is reasonable to expect that the CLCPA’s impact on asset lives will be felt not only by
the Company and its ratepayers, but also by all of the Company’s suppliers. As gas sales
in New York decline, those who produce and transport gas into New York, and to the
Company, will ﬁnd that their own ﬁxed asset costs must be recovered from a declining
sales volume. The expected result should be increases in the per-unit allocation of ﬁxed
asset costs by the Company’s suppliers which will drive up gas commodity costs at the
same time that rates are being increased due to the CLCPA’s impact on the Company’s
own cost-recovery needs.
As the Company points out "nominal gas commodity prices are [now] low, so consumer
impacts will be mitigated"7 if the process of reconciling asset lives with CLCPA expecta-
tions is begun now, and without further delay. Waiting to adjust ASLs will only increase
the risk that costs will be multiplied as a result.
Yet another source of future rate increase risk is the potential for increases in the cost of
capital which directly impacts customers rates. Banks and investors currently provide
capital to the Company at rates which are based on an assumption that the Company
will remain a going-concern for the foreseeable future and that it will maintain, if not
grow, its sales volumes over time. However, it is becoming increasingly recognized that

In its testimony, the Staff Infrastructure and Operations Panel (SIOP) dismisses the Com-
pany’s request to accelerate depreciation while failing to address any of the long-term
issues that delay might cause and which have been outlined above. SIOP also failed to
offer any alternative approaches that might mitigate the issues that will be caused by de-
lay they propose. They clariﬁed that "We did not consider any alternatives. We did not
look into any alternatives."16 SIOP’s opposition to the proposal appears to be entirely
based on a concern for the immediate, near-term impacts of accelerated depreciation.
They said: "we believe it is premature to make any major change to the average ser-
vice lives over which gas assets are depreciated, other than LPP, due to the potential for
large bill impacts to customers and the uncertainty regarding how CLCPA will be imple-
mented."17
As Charles Lenns responded in his rebuttal testimony “The adjustment proposed by
Staff effectively ‘kicks the can down the road.’ The Company believes that waiting two
or more years to resolve the issue of shortened depreciation lives resulting from the ex-
pansive climate change initiatives being implemented in New York is unwarranted.”18
Given the potentially severe impacts of delay in beginning to address the issue of ac-
celerated depreciation and given the absence of substantive alternatives in the record,
the Commission should grant the Company’s request to begin now the process of ad-
dressing the impacts on its business of the CLCP and related policies. While this will
undoubtedly result in near-term rate increases, beginning now will reduce the magni-
tude of future rate increases, lower long-term costs to ratepayers, and strengthen the
Company’s ability to provide safe and reliable service.

service territories during the three-year rate period, discontinuing their
promotion of natural gas services, incentivizing the expanded use of heat
pumps and pursuing nonpipe alternatives. They also agree to conduct a

These provisions, particularly "discontinuing their promotion of natural gas services"
were discussed during the Evidentiary Hearing. The Company stated that such a re-
quirement would not have a signiﬁcant impact on their business because most of their
new customers are the result of organic demand, not promotion.19 Given that the ﬁrst
step in ﬁlling a hole is to stop digging it deeper, it makes sense to end now promotion
of services which require the installation of assets whose costs will simply add to the
already signiﬁcant quantity of undepreciated assets burdening existing ratepayers.

III.   DEPRECIATION STANDARDS AND SYSTEMS 
Q. 
Discuss the standard by which regulated utilities are allowed 
12 
to recover depreciation expense. 
13 
A. 
In Lindheimer v. Illinois Bell Telephone Co., the U.S. Supreme 
14 
Court stated that “depreciation is the loss, not restored by 
15 
current maintenance, which is due to all the factors causing 
16 
the ultimate retirement of the property. These factors 
17 
embrace wear and tear, decay, inadequacy, and obsolescence.”  
18 
Lindheimer v. Illinois Bell Tel. Co., 292 U.S. 151, 167 
19 


--- Page 9 ---
Cases 25-E-0072 and 25-G-0073 
Direct Testimony of David Garrett 
 
7 
 
(1934).  The Lindheimer Court also recognized that the 
1 
original cost of plant assets, rather than present value or 
2 
some other measure, is the proper basis for calculating 
3 
depreciation expense.  Referring to the straight-line method, 
4 
the Lindheimer Court stated that “[a]ccording to the 
5 
principle of this accounting practice, the loss is computed 
6 
upon the actual cost of the property as entered upon the 
7 
books, less the expected salvage, and the amount charged each 
8 
year is one year's pro rata share of the total amount.”  Id. 
9 
at 168. 
10 
The Lindheimer Court also held: 
11 
[T]he company has the burden of making a convincing 
12 
showing that the amounts it has charged to 
13 
operating expenses for depreciation have not been 
14 
excessive. That burden is not sustained by proof 
15 
that its general accounting system has been 
16 
correct. The calculations are mathematical, but the 
17 
predictions underlying them are essentially matters 
18 
of opinion.    
19 
Lindheimer, supra, at 169 (emphasis added).  Thus, the 
20 
Commission must ultimately determine if the Company has met

its burden of proof by making a convincing showing that its 
22 
proposed depreciation rates are not excessive.

Q. 
Please describe your approach in using Iowa curves to estimate 
10 
service life. 
11 
A. 
The historical retirement data are used to develop an observed 
12 
life table (OLT) for each account. The data points on the OLT 
13 
can be plotted to form a curve (the “OLT curve”). The OLT 
14 
curve is not a theoretical survivor curve, rather, it is a 
15 
curve derived from the Company’s actual historical data. An 
16 
OLT curve by itself, however, is rarely a smooth curve, and 
17 
it is often not a complete curve. That is, it does not end at 
18 
zero percent surviving. In order to calculate average life, 
19 
a complete survivor curve is needed.  The Iowa curve-fitting 
20 
process involves selecting the best Iowa curve to fit the OLT 
21 


--- Page 16 ---
Cases 25-E-0072 and 25-G-0073 
Direct Testimony of David Garrett 
 
14 
 
curve. This can be accomplished through a combination of 
1 
visual and mathematical curve-fitting techniques, as well as 
2 
professional judgment. The first step of my approach to curve-
3 
fitting involves visually inspecting the OLT curve for any 
4 
irregularities. For example, if the tail end of the curve is 
5 
erratic and shows a sharp decline over a short period of time, 
6 
it may indicate that this portion of the data is less 
7 
reliable, as further discussed below. After inspecting the 
8 
OLT curve, I use a mathematical curve-fitting technique which 
9 
essentially involves measuring the distance between the OLT 
10 
curve and the selected Iowa curve in order to get an 
11 
objective, mathematical assessment of how well the curve 
12 
fits. After selecting an Iowa curve, I observe the OLT curve 
13 
along with the Iowa curve on the same graph to determine how 
14 
well the curve fits. I may repeat this process several times 
15 
for any given account to ensure that the most reasonable Iowa 
16 
curve is selected. More details about the process of actuarial 
17 
analysis are included in Exhibit__(DJG-16).

Q. 
Should every portion of the OLT curve be given equal weight?   
9 
A.

17 
standard, an analyst may decide to truncate the tail end of 
18 
the OLT curve at a certain percent of initial exposures, such 
19 
as one percent. Using this approach puts a greater emphasis 
20 
on the most valuable portions of the curve. For my analysis

Q. 
How have the current public health and economic conditions affected RG&E’s 
7 
deliveries? 
8 
A. 
The COVID-19 Pandemic (“COVID”) significantly changed electric deliveries since the 
9 
second quarter of 2020 and continuing through 2021.  Compared to normal expectations, 
10 
commercial and industrial deliveries declined while residential deliveries increased above 
11 
normal levels.  Remote working and schooling from home contributed to the increased 
12 
residential use.  In addition, COVID restrictions have adversely impacted commercial 
13 
and industrial activity.  Between 2019 and 2021, residential electric deliveries increased 
14 
6.9% (185,290 MWh) and commercial and industrial electric deliveries declined 6.2% 
15 
(287,749 MWh).  As 2022 commences, restrictions are easing, leading to a modified 
16 
return to usual activity and a post-COVID “new normal” is anticipated in the Electric 
17 
Deliveries forecast.  For example, with this “new normal,” it is anticipated that the recent 
18 
increases in residential electric deliveries will decline and the commercial and industrial 
19 
deliveries will grow.  To date, the Companies have not experienced any significant 
20 
impact on gas deliveries as a result of the COVD Pandemic.

7 
A. Electric Deliveries Forecast 
8 
 
Please summarize the historical Test Year electric billed deliveries and electric customers 
9 
by AD ID as well as the Panel’s forecast for the Rate Year.

A. 
Exhibit __ (RGEDRP-2) shows NYSEG’s electric billed deliveries (MWh) and electric

12 
Q. 
How did the Panel forecast monthly electric billed deliveries? 
13 
A. 
We used an econometric modeling methodology to estimate the relationship between 
14 
certain explanatory, or independent, variables and the dependent variable, which, in this 
15 
case, is the monthly billed electric deliveries by customer class.  We examined various 
16 
functional forms of the models, including linear, log-linear, semi-log linear, and log-log.  
17 
The log-log form of the models produced the best model results.   
18 
Q. 
What is econometric modeling? 
19 
A. 
Econometric modeling applies statistical techniques, such as linear regression, to estimate 
20 
the relationship between certain explanatory, or independent variables, and the dependent 
21 
variable, which, in this case, is monthly deliveries data.  An econometric model, also 
22 
known as a linear regression model, is an estimate of a best-fit line between one 
23

dependent variable and one or more explanatory variables.  The term “best-fit” refers to 
1 
the line with the lowest sum of squared errors. 
2 
Q. 
Has RG&E employed such a methodology for electric deliveries forecasting in previous 
3 
rate cases? 
4 
A. 
Yes.  RG&E most recently used econometric modeling for electric deliveries forecasting

in the 2019 Rate Case.

Q. 
Did the Panel make any out of model adjustments to the residential, commercial, 
1 
industrial, and municipal deliveries forecasts? 
2 
A. 
Yes.  An out of model adjustment was made to the econometric forecasts to account for 
3 
factors that have an effect on projected sales which cannot be adequately accounted for 
4 
within the econometric model.  Out of model adjustments were made to account for 
5 
RG&E’s Energy Efficiency Plans in the residential, commercial, industrial, and 
6 
municipal deliveries forecasts.  Because the expected decrease to load due to incremental 
7 
Energy Efficiency program impacts is not reflected in the historical data used in our 
8 
econometric models, it was necessary to make out of model adjustments to the forecasted 
9 
residential, commercial, industrial, and municipal load. 
10 
Q. 
How did the Panel calculate the Energy Efficiency out of model adjustments? 
11 
A. 
We obtained estimates from the NYSEG/RG&E Energy Efficiency group.  This 
12 
information is based on the Energy Efficiency spending authorized by the Commission in 
13 
its Order Authorizing Utility Energy Efficiency and Building Electrification Portfolios 
14 
through 2025 issued on January 16, 2020, in Case 18-M-0084.  The Energy Efficiency 
15 
group also provided allocations of the targets by residential, commercial, industrial, and 
16 
municipal revenue classes.  A summary of the annual out of model adjustments by 
17 
revenue class for May 2023 to April 2024 can be found in Exhibit __ (RGEDRP-3).  The 
18 
annual targets were then allocated monthly according to each revenue class’s monthly 
19 
load shape.

Q. 
Did the Panel make any other out of model adjustments to the residential, commercial, 
1 
industrial, and municipal deliveries forecasts? 
2 
A. 
Yes.  Out of model adjustments were made to account for the impact of new installations 
3 
of customer-owned PV systems in the residential, commercial, and municipal deliveries 
4 
forecasts.  These installations operate “behind the meter” and reduce deliveries to these 
5 
customers.  The impact of new PV installations is not captured in our econometric 
6 
models, and thus, it is necessary to make out of model adjustments. 
7 
Q. 
Please explain how the Panel calculated the out of model adjustment for customer-owned 
8 
PV systems. 
9 
A. 
First, the Panel developed a monthly megawatt capacity forecast of new installations 
10 
beginning in January 2022.  The forecast included the following types of PV installations: 
11 
NM, Community Distributed Generation (“CDG”) and Remote Net Meter (“RNM”).  For 
12 
each of these categories, we considered current pending interconnection applications and 
13 
the recent historical rate of interconnections.  We also used forecasts by the New York       
14 
Independent System Operator’s (“NYISO”) 2021 Load and Capacity Data Report (Table 
15 
I-9d, p. 41) for years beyond the horizon of our current interconnection applications and 
16 
established a “glide path” to meet RG&E’s share.  Exhibit __ (RGEDRP-3) includes a 
17 
chart and summary of actual and forecasted PV interconnections for 2012 to 2030.  After 
18 
establishing the monthly capacity forecast, we converted that forecast to monthly 
19 
megawatt hour output with software obtained from the National Renewable Energy 
20 
Laboratory (“NREL”), using Rochester, New York as a proxy location.  The software is 
21 
called System Advisor Model and it may be used for any purpose.  NYSEG used version 
22 
2013.1.15.

Q. 
How does each type of PV installation affect billed deliveries? 
1 
A. 
For NM PV installations, the output will reduce load on the customer meter and thus 
2 
reduce billed kilowatt hour (“kWh”) amounts to the customer.  As a result, an out of 
3 
model adjustment is required.  For CDG and RNM PV installations, customers receive 
4 
financial adjustments.  Therefore, RG&E did not include these types of installations in 
5 
the out of model adjustments. 
6 
Q. 
How did the Panel allocate the out of model adjustments to the residential, commercial, 
7 
industrial, and municipal revenue classes? 
8 
A. 
The Interconnection Queue database identifies NM PV installations by residential and 
9 
non-residential.  We allocated residential NM PV installations to the residential class

C. Model Validation 
4 
Q. 
How did the Panel validate the models used to develop the forecasts of monthly electric 
5 
deliveries and customers? 
6 
A. 
The Panel analyzed “Goodness of Fit” tests to determine what percentage of the variation 
7 
in the dependent variable can be explained by the explanatory variables that we selected.  
8 
In other words, these tests check a model’s summary statistics that may explain how well 
9 
the model fits or explains a dataset.  The first test statistic is called the Coefficient of 
10 
Determination, better known as the R-squared.  An R-squared value of 1 means that the 
11 
dependent variable is, on average, completely explained by the explanatory variables, 
12 
while a value of 0 means that no explanatory relationship between the dependent variable 
13 
and the independent variables can be estimated from the sample data.  The second test 
14 
statistic is called the Mean Absolute Percent Error (“MAPE”).  The MAPE is the ratio of 
15 
the absolute value difference between the monthly forecast value derived from the model 
16 
and the actual monthly value averaged over the entire dataset of actual values.  The 
17 
smaller the MAPE, the better.  A model that results in a high R-squared value and a small 
18 
MAPE is said to have strong summary statistics. 
19 
One of the most common tests for checking if a model performs well is the Ex 
20 
Post forecast test.  For this test, a portion of the sample is withheld and the model is 
21 
estimated on the remaining sample.  An estimate or forecast of the withheld data is then 
22 
generated by the model which is compared to the withheld dataset.  Summary statistics 
23

Case 22-E-_____; Case 22-G-____; Case 22-E-____; Case 22-G-____ 
 
DIRECT TESTIMONY OF RG&E DELIVERIES AND REVENUES PANEL 
 
25 
 
are analyzed to see how well the model actually forecasts the withheld sample.  The 
1 
primary statistic analyzed for an Ex Post test is the MAPE.  We have performed Ex Post 
2 
forecast tests on all customer and billed unit models presented in this testimony for two 
3 
and four-year periods to demonstrate how well the models forecast two and four years 
4 
out.  Exhibit __ (RGEDRP-4) summarizes the statistics for all the models.

Q. 
Please describe the natural gas weather normalization methodology. 
8 
A. 
We used billing month weather for the residential, commercial, municipal, and industrial 
9 
classes.  The first step is to take the actual deliveries for each year and subtract the base 
10 
load deliveries.  The term “base load” is defined as the average of the non-weather-
11

12

13

14 
yields the actual weather response per HDD for the billing month.  The third step is to 
15 
multiply the quotient by the normal billing month HDDs.  The term “normal degree 
16 
days” in this context is defined as the 30-year rolling average (1992-2021) of degree days 
17 
for any given calendar day as obtained from the National Oceanic and Atmospheric 
18 
Administration.  The last step is to add the base load that was originally removed.

4

Q. 
What is the first step? 
6 
A. 
The first step of the gas delivery revenue forecast is to incorporate the forecasted 
7 
deliveries and customers by SC and by customer class into the Gas Revenue Model.  The 
8 
monthly forecasted delivery volumes are allocated to the gas rate blocks established in 
9 
RG&E’s gas tariff based on distributions derived from a block analysis covering three 
10 
calendar years (January 2019 to December 2021).  The individual month used for the 
11 
block analysis was based on the month in the three-year period where the actual billing 
12 
month  HDD was closest to normal HDD.  The forecasted deliveries by block are then 
13 
multiplied by the current tariff rates, effective as of May 1, 2022, for the respective SCs, 
14 
and the customer counts are multiplied by the fixed monthly customer charge for the 
15 
respective SCs, to derive forecasted base delivery revenues.  
16 
Q. 
What is the second step? 
17 
A. 
The forecasted deliveries, where applicable, are multiplied by the forecasted MFC 
18 
Surcharge, RAM Surcharge and the Research and Development (“R&D”) Surcharge. 
19 
Q. 
What is the third step? 
20 
A. 
The third step is to apply the revenue tax rates to the delivery revenues using the actual 
21 
revenue tax rates for service type (i.e., sales vs. transportation), and customer type 
22 
(i.e., residential vs. non-residential).

The program seeks to underground approximately 24 miles of overhead distribution

12 
Q. 
ARE OVERHEAD-TO-UNDERGROUND CONVERSIONS COMMONLY 
13 
USED TO IMPROVE SYSTEM RESILIENCE?  
14 
A. 
Overhead-to-underground conversion has been extensively analyzed and has 
15 
generally been determined to be a very expensive way to “harden” primary 
16 
distribution systems.  This is especially true when undergrounding involves three-
17 
phase sections, with typical cost estimates for this type of work ranging upwards of 
18 
$5 million per mile. 
19 
 
 
Some utilities have recently pursued targeted undergrounding on single-
20 
phase lateral taps (i.e., “spurs”), where undergrounding costs are much lower when 
21 
compared to three-phase main trunks.  This is especially true when directional 
22 


--- Page 9 ---
Case 22-E-0064 
 
RICHARD E. BROWN 
8 
boring can be used.  The typical goal of these “lateral undergrounding” programs 
1 
is to underground lateral taps in heavily forested areas so that there are fewer 
2 
damage locations to address after a major weather event.  The cost for lateral 
3 
undergrounding typically ranges from $300,000 to $1 million per circuit mile. 
4 
Q. 
WHAT ARE YOUR OBSERVATIONS REGARDING THE COMPANY’S 
5 
PROPOSED SELECTIVE UNDERGROUNDING PROGRAM? 
6 
A. 
This program estimates undergrounding costs of $10 million per mile, with a total 
7 
program costs from 2022 through 2026 of approximately $345 million.  This $10 
8 
million per mile for spur undergrounding is very high when compared to the typical 
9 
cost of lateral tap undergrounding.  The proposed cost is very high even when 
10 
compared to the typical cost of main trunk undergrounding, which has significantly 
11 
higher resilience benefits.  Given the high cost to perform this type of work in New 
12 
York City and Westchester, it is not disputed that the Company’s program costs per 
13 
mile to complete the work will be this high.  However, this does not negate the fact 
14 
that comparatively speaking, $10 million per mile for undergrounding is an 
15 
unprecedented cost for hardening a distribution circuit spur. 
16 
Q. 
DOES THE HIGH COST OF THE SELECTIVE UNDERGROUNDING 
17 
PROGRAM RAISE CONCERNS REGARDING BENEFITS VERSUS 
18 
COST? 
19 
A. 
Yes.  The benefits of hardening are related to less damage that will occur during 
20 
future major weather events.  Less damage during weather events results in lower 
21 
restoration costs for the utility and faster restoration times for customers.  Faster 
22

restoration time results in continued health and safety of customers, less economic 
1 
impact for the economy, as well as other potential monetary benefits such as 
2 
reduced food and medicine spoilage and reduced costs for temporary lodging. 
3 
 
 
By way of example, consider a potentially advantageous area to 
4 
underground that consists of lateral spurs in heavily treed areas in a region that 
5 
experiences frequent major events that cause significant vegetation damage to 
6 
overhead utility systems.  In these areas, the benefits of spur undergrounding are 
7 
unlikely to exceed the costs of spur undergrounding when program costs exceed $2 
8 
million per mile.  The program the Company is proposing costs five times this 
9 
amount.

Q. 
ARE 
THERE 
ALTERNATIVE 
CAPITAL 
INVESTMENTS 
FOR 
11 
RESILIENCE THAT THE COMPANY SHOULD CONSIDER?  
12 
A. 
Yes.  Con Edison should consider replacing the Selective Undergrounding program 
13 
with a Selective Hardening program.  This would be a broader program that 
14 
analyzes each area to be hardened and considers a comprehensive approach that 
15 
uses a suite of technologies including, but not limited to, overhead hardening, 
16 
enhanced vegetation management, underground conversion, and other known 
17 
system hardening approaches.  Underground conversion can still be used when less 
18 
expensive hardening options are not practicable.  This “hardening toolkit” approach 
19 
will achieve the intended outcome of improved system reliability and resiliency in 
20 
a much more cost-effective way.  In other words, a “hardening toolkit” approach 
21 
would achieve similar customer benefits with far less of a rate impact to customers.

Q. 
DO YOU HAVE ANY OTHER COMMENTS WITH REGARDS TO THE

COMPANY’S SELECTIVE UNDERGROUNDING PROGRAM? 
2 
A. 
Yes.  A program intended to harden spurs through either undergrounding or other 
3

4

5

6

10 
order-of-magnitude information about the relative scale of costs and benefits.  A 
11 
plan such as this is important since hardening programs are expensive and, unless 
12 
good selection and prioritization is used, can result in significantly less resilience 
13 
benefits.

A. 
Tariff classes should be based on groups of customers with similar cost-of-service 
10 
characteristics.  This is precisely why tariffs for temporary service are distinct from 
11 
those for permanent service. 
12

13

14

15

16 
of installation will typically be collected up front.  Similarly, the customer must 
17 
typically pay directly for service removal.

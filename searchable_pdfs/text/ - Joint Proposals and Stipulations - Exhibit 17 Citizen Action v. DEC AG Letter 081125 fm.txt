Dear Justice Schreibman:
This office represents respondent-defendant New York State Department of 
Environmental Conservation (DEC) in the above-referenced proceeding. At the 
conclusion of the July 25, 2025 oral argument in this matter, the Court noted that it 
would consider further correspondence related to new developments. This letter 
addresses two categories of new developments: (1) the publication of the 2025 Draft 
New York State Energy Plan by the New York State Energy Planning Board on
July 23, 2025 and (2) additional actions by the federal government that impede New 
York’s efforts to achieve the Climate Leadership and Community Protection Act’s 
(the Climate Act) goals in a timely manner. As explained below, analysis in the 
draft plan and increasingly significant federal impediments both further support 
DEC’s argument that a 40% reduction in greenhouse gases by 2030 based upon the 
Climate Act’s scope and accounting methodology, including with respect to increases 
of out-of-state emissions and the potential for carbon leakage, is currently 
infeasible. As a result, discretion warrants denying petitioners’ request for 
mandamus relief as it would order DEC to damage the public interest by imposing
unanticipated and undue costs on New Yorkers. 
As background on the draft state energy plan, New York’s Energy Law 
establishes the Energy Planning Board and charges it with periodically developing
and adopting a state energy plan.0F1 A state energy plan must satisfy a number of 
energy-planning goals, forecasting energy supplies, demand and policy 
considerations for at least a ten-year period.1F2 Among its many tasks, the Board 
assesses energy costs for consumers and, more specifically, low-income consumers.2F3

The Board is comprised of fourteen members, including two voting members 
respectively selected by each house of the New York State Legislature.3F4 Before 
issuing a final state energy plan, the Board prepares a draft energy plan for public 
consideration and input.4F5 At its July 23, 2025 meeting, the Board unanimously 
voted in favor of publishing the 2025 Draft Energy Plan for public consideration and 
comment. The draft plan provides a fifteen-year forecast—to 2040.  
 
Both legislative appointees voted in favor of issuing the draft plan for 
comment. Before casting their vote in support, one of the legislative members stated 
that “much ha[d] changed” since they had voted in 2019 for the Climate Act.5F6 The 
headwinds they specifically referenced included wars in Ukraine and the Middle 
East, the Covid pandemic, the “supply-chain breakdown,” inflation, and 
“uncertainties and challenges from the federal government.”6F7 On the latter topic, 
the elected official elaborated that the “current administration’s attacks on offshore 
wind projects and recent cuts to federal tax credits to renewable energy and electric 
vehicles are simply unprecedented.”7F8 They concluded that the draft plan is meant to 
provide a “roadmap” to achieve “a reliable, affordable, and clean energy system by 
2040.”8F9 Of note, the draft plan was developed prior to many of the federal 
government’s recent changes in law and policy that dramatically impact the energy 
sector and obstruct renewable energy projects and programs.9F10 
 
 
The draft plan itself shows that a 40% greenhouse gas reduction from 1990 
levels by 2030 is infeasible under the Climate Act’s accounting methodology and 
unaffordable for consumers. The draft plan specifically looks at five scenarios, none 
of which achieves the 2030 goal. As well as no action and current policy scenarios, 
an additional action scenario projects further adoption of greenhouse gas reducing 
policies.10F11 The most aggressive projections, the two net zero scenarios, reflect what 
would be needed for full achievement of 2050 emission reduction targets.11F12 As the 
chart below shows, the most aggressive projection—which would require imposing 
extraordinary and damaging costs upon New Yorkers—has New York achieving a 
40% reduction in greenhouse gas emissions in 2033.12F13

improvements.16F17 This does not, however, offset the insurmountable upfront costs 
that New Yorkers would face if DEC were forced to try to achieve the Legislature’s 
aspirational emissions reductions by the 2030 deadline rather than proceeding at 
an ambitious but sustainable pace.  
 
Consider the issues of electricity generation and load. As the draft plan 
makes clear, “[i]n all pathways scenarios, new large loads interconnecting to the 
system drive growing electricity demand, across both annual loads and peaks.”17F18 
Loads are expected to grow because of “electrification of transportation and 
buildings,” with the net-zero scenarios showing large projected increases in annual 
and peak loads.18F19 Increased electrical usage in those areas thus will mean more 
need for electrical generation that is reliable: “Meeting this growing electricity load, 
while maintaining system reliability, will require investments in expansion of the 
electricity system in all pathways.”19F20 Further, as the draft plan states: “There is 
uncertainty as to whether the continuous growth in clean energy supply resources 
will meet growing demand given the federal policy context, broader economic 
headwinds, and the need for new clean firm technologies to become commercially 
available.”20F21 
 
Projections about heat pump adoption serve as one practical example of the 
substantial gap between the pace of change needed to achieve Climate Act targets 
and an ambitious but achievable path represented by the additional action scenario. 
The most aggressive net zero scenarios—which still do not meet the 2030 emissions-
reduction goal—identify that nearly 50% of all residential heating equipment sales 
by 2030 would need to be electric heat pumps.21F22 This projection is approximately 
five times the level of deployment the no action scenario projects for the same 
year.22F23 And by 2035, the net zero scenarios assume 100% of sales of residential 
heating equipment will be heat pumps. That is still an insufficient trajectory for the 
Climate Act’s 2030 goal. What is more, as the analysis notes, “[t]he cost of energy 
upgrades as well as energy and housing costs in general are acute challenges for 
[low-and-moderate income] households, affordable housing property owners, and 
building owners in disadvantaged communities who lack financial and capital 
resources to make these investments.”23F24

the state on track to have all medium and heavy-duty vehicle sales be fully zero

In sum, under even the most aggressive scenario the State Energy Planning 
Board considered—one that by 2040 would lead to an added $42 billion in annual 
energy costs—New York would not meet the Climate Act’s 2030 goal. While the 
draft plan shows that ambitious progress under the Climate Act is achievable, the 
2030 goal itself is not practically feasible due to costs consumers simply cannot 
bear.26F27 
 
 
The challenge of timely progress that is laid out in the draft plan is made all 
the more difficult by ongoing federal action that undermines New York’s emissions-
reducing efforts. Since DEC noted this issue in its papers and since I discussed it at 
oral argument, the federal government has taken additional action to impede 
greenhouse gas reductions by all states acting within their own authorities to 
determine energy resources, including deploying renewable energy to reduce 
greenhouse gas emissions.  
 
   
As expected and as I previewed during oral argument, U.S. EPA announced 
on July 29 that it will revoke its “endangerment finding”—the 2009 conclusion that 
carbon dioxide and other greenhouse gases are a form of air pollution that EPA can 
regulate under the Clean Air Act because they contribute to climate change and 
pose a danger to human health and welfare.27F28 That finding has been the 
underpinning for a whole host of federal and federally-authorized state climate 
actions, including regulations limiting greenhouse gas emissions from motor 
vehicles, which in New York are the second largest sector of emissions, after the 
building sector. Indeed, at the same time it announced it will revoke the 
endangerment finding, EPA also announced that it intends to rescind all 
greenhouse gas emissions limits from motor vehicles, compounding the harm from 
Congress’s May 2025 rescission of the federal preemption waivers under Section 177 
of the Clean Air Act. That provision of the Clean Air Act is the legal authority for 
New York’s regulation of greenhouse gas emissions of motor vehicles under the 
Advanced Clean Cars and Advanced Clean Trucks rules, which underpin the 
previously anticipated greenhouse gas emission reductions from requiring a

Additionally, just last Thursday, U.S. EPA terminated all grants in a 
national $7 billion Solar for All program to deploy solar energy, with New York 
receiving $250 million. New York currently ranks as the leading state for these 
community solar installations, and the loss of this program, as well as other actions, 
is a significant setback to continued growth of the industry in the state. And New 
York’s clean energy industries will face increasingly negative impacts from the 
implementation of H.R. 1, commonly referred to as the “One Big Beautiful Bill Act,” 
signed into law on July 4, 2025, which terminated consumer tax credits for 
renewable energy, energy efficiency improvements, and electric vehicles. In light of 
these major recent developments and irrespective of whether they ultimately are 
legally sustained or vacated, DEC and other state agencies will necessarily rework 
their statewide greenhouse gas analyses to better evaluate the scope and nature of 
reductions they will need to impose to reach the Climate Act’s goals in an 
economically sustainable manner.  
 
 
As I discussed at oral argument, the quintessential scenario in which a court 
should exercise its discretion and decline to issue a mandamus order is when 
mandamus could cause “damage to the public interest.”30F31 Petitioners have not 
shown a plausible scenario where the 2030 greenhouse gas reduction goal can be 
achieved without inflicting unanticipated and undue harm on New York consumers, 
and the concrete analysis in the 2025 Draft Energy Plan dispels any uncertainty on 
the topic: New Yorkers will face alarming financial consequences if speed is given 
preference over sustainability. Because the public interest weighs heavily against 
any order of mandamus, I reiterate DEC’s request that the petition be denied and 
the proceeding dismissed.

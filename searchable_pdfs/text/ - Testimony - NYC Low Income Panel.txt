Q. 
HOW DID YOU DEFINE THE TERM “ENERGY COST BURDEN” FOR 
3 
PURPOSES OF YOUR ANALYSIS?   
4 
A. 
For our analysis, we defined energy cost burden as the sum of (1) reported out-of-
5 
pocket monthly natural gas expenses multiplied by 12 plus (2) reported out-of-
6 
pocket monthly electricity expenses multiplied by 12 plus (3) reported annual 
7 
other energy expenses, such as fuel oil or kerosene purchases, minus an estimated 
8 
HEAP benefit, divided by annual pre-tax income.  Tax credits and non-cash 
9 
subsidies are not included in the income calculation.  A family is determined to be 
10 
energy cost burdened if its energy cost burden is greater than six percent.  Six 
11 
percent is both the State target and a target level used by energy affordability 
12 
experts, including, for example, Fisher, Sheehan & Colton Public Finance and 
13 
General Economics, who report a Home Energy Affordability Gap and whose 
14 
work was relied upon by Department Staff and the Commission in establishing the 
15 
State’s target.

which the ACS data was reported, thus corresponding to economic conditions and

costs in the year the survey was conducted.

17 
individuals may reside in the same apartment, home, or other housing unit.

Q. 
WHY WAS THE ANALYSIS PERFORMED AT THE FAMILY LEVEL? 
3 
A. 
Poverty levels are defined based on family size and income, not household 
4 
income. Therefore, to properly evaluate energy costs against the low income 
5 
thresholds noted in Exhibit__(LIP-2), the family units created for the New York 
6 
City poverty measure are used.  We note that this differs from the Commission’s 
7 
usage of households to determine energy cost burden.  In our analysis, more than 
8 
one family unit in a household would mean that we identify in the census data 
9 
more than one “resource sharing unit” in that household.  For example, a group of 
10 
family members plus an unrelated couple who are identified as boarders would be 
11 
considered two families under our analysis, and utility costs would apportioned 
12 
out to each family according to size.  In other words, when there are multiple 
13 
families in a household, our analysis would show that each is paying less in costs 
14 
than the combined household and those costs are held relative to each family’s 
15 
income to determine their cost burden.  Our understanding is that the vast majority 
16 
of households in the New York City are single-family households, and so we 
17 
expect the implication of performing our analysis at the family level (as opposed 
18 
to the household level) to be minor.

A. 
The energy deliveries1 forecast was developed in several steps.  The first 
1 
step was to “reconstitute,” or add-back or subtract, as applicable, the 
2 
impacts of energy efficiency (“EE”), solar-photovoltaics (“PV”), and 
3 
electric vehicles (“EV”) to the historical monthly energy dataset.  This set 
4 
of programs and technologies is termed Distributed Energy Resources 
5 
(“DERs”) in this testimony.2  The reconstituted data is also termed “gross” 
6 
in this testimony to reflect the fact that it represents data prior to the 
7 
impacts of DERs.  The second step is to develop an econometric forecast 
8 
of gross energy deliveries based on Upstate New York economic 
9 
conditions, normal weather, and days billed using this reconstituted 
10 
dataset.  The third step is to create the “net” forecast by adjusting the gross 
11 
forecast by the projections for future DERs.  Impacts for EE and PV 
12 
(reflecting decreased electric load on the system) are subtracted from the 
13 
gross forecast, while impacts of EV (reflecting increased electric load on 
14 
the system) are added to the gross forecast to create the net forecast.  
15 
These forecasts were first developed in terms of revenues classes – 
16 
residential, commercial, and industrial.  They were then allocated to the

C.  Weather Variables 
1 
 
2 
Q. 
Please summarize the weather and other explanatory variables used 
3 
in the forecast models. 
4 
A. 
Annual weather is summarized in Exhibit__(JFG-2) for the years 2010 to 
5 
2026.  The weather variables are cooling degree days (“CDD”) and 
6 
heating degree days (“HDD”).  Each day type (i.e. CDD and HDD) is 
7 
derived using a base temperature of 65°F.  Daily degree days are the 
8 
difference between the average daily temperature and the base 
9 
temperature.  Monthly degree days are the sum of the daily values for each 
10 
month.  Seasonal interactive variables with these degree day variables 
11 
were also used, if statistically significant, in the models to provide 
12 
additional seasonal specificity between cooling and heating months.  The 
13 
cdd_season variable includes months from May to October.  The 
14 
hdd_season variable includes months from November to May.3  
15 
 
16

17 
A. 
Historic CDD and HDD were calculated from the daily temperature 
18 
readings of the National Weather Service’s Albany, Utica, Syracuse, 
19 
Buffalo, Watertown, and Messina weather stations.  These weather

13 
of days billed. 
14 
A. 
The number of days billed refers to the number of days between meter 
15

16 
billed was calculated directly from the Company’s meter reading schedule.  
17 
The meter reading schedule is dependent upon the number of non-holiday 
18 
weekdays, which varies from month to month and year to year.  All else 
19 
constant, a given percentage increase/decrease in the number of days 
20 
billed can be expected to increase/decrease energy delivery volumes by 
21

the same percentage amount.  On a monthly basis, the billing day impacts 
1

2

3

4

Q.

14 
deliveries. 
15 
A. 
The Company developed econometric models that related actual monthly 
16 
energy deliveries to economic and demographic variables, as well as 
17 
weather, days billed, and seasonal indicator variables.  These forecasts are 
18 
developed by revenue classes – residential, commercial, and industrial.  
19 
For the residential and commercial classes these forecasts are stated in 
20

terms of energy use per customer.  For the industrial class the forecast is 
1 
stated directly as energy deliveries.   
2 
 
3 
 
The customer count forecasts are similarly based on econometric models 
4 
that relate customer counts to economic and/or demographic, calendar, 
5 
and time trend variables.  Customer counts are defined as active electric 
6 
bill accounts.  These are also developed for the three revenue classes - 
7

8

To determine the energy delivery forecasts for the residential and

12

13 
directly.  The Company’s preferred approach is to develop the energy use 
14

15 
to derive the final energy deliveries forecasts as in the residential and 
16 
commercial models.  The industrial class, however, because of its non-
17

18

Q. 
How were the econometric models developed?   
1 
A. 
The models were designed to ensure that the variables used to estimate the 
2 
energy deliveries and customer counts were relevant (causal and 
3 
correlated) to the revenue class, were statistically valid, and produced 
4 
meaningful results.   The Company assessed overall explanatory strength 
5 
of the model by examining the R-squared statistics, and confirmed the 
6 
significance of the model variables by strong t-statistics and the 
7 
reasonableness of results by reviewing future projections versus historic 
8 
values.   R-squared values closest to 1.0 are strongest.  T-statistics of 0.05 
9 
or lower are considered significant.  Other typical statistical tests, 
10 
including normality of residuals, multicollinearity, autocorrelation, and 
11 
influential data points were also reviewed and confirmed the validity of 
12 
the models.   
13 
 
 
14 
 
The Company used historic reconstituted actual (vs. weather-normal) 
15 
monthly data to develop the equations using ordinary least squares 
16 
regression.   The forecast was accomplished by inputting the future values 
17 
of the explanatory variables into these equations to determine the 
18 
projected deliveries and customer counts.  Specifics for each of the models 
19 
are more fully discussed later in this testimony.  The results of the models 
20 
are attached in the work papers included as Exhibit __ (JFG-29).

Yes.  The overall forecasting methodology of using an econometric based

regression model, normal weather based on degree days, and consideration

6 
 
7 
Q.   Have any changes been made to the prior forecast methodology 
8 
presented in the 2017 Electric Rate Case? 
9 
 A.  
Yes, a number of changes have been made. These include: 
10 
• The econometric models are now specified on a gross basis, or as if the 
11 
DERs were not in-place during the historical period.  Then, post-model 
12 
adjustments are made to the forecast to derive the “net” impact of the 
13 
DERs forecast.  In the 2017 Electric Rate Case, the econometric 
14 
models were specified directly on the historical “net” values, meaning 
15 
that the impacts of DERs were embedded in the data.  Because of the 
16 
significant growth of DERs over time, the historic link between 
17 
economic growth and energy growth was masked.  Reconstituting, or 
18 
removing the discernable changes in load that are attributed to DERs 
19 
prior to running the models unmasks the natural and intuitive 
20 
correlation between energy growth and economic growth.  The 
21 


--- Page 20 ---
Testimony of Joseph F. Gredder 
 
 
Page 17 of 53 
 
impacts of the specific DERs are then presented after the statistical 
1 
modeling process, both separately and in aggregate. 
2 
• The residential and commercial models are now specified as “energy 
3 
use per customer” models.  This is a preferred approach because it 
4 
maintains a relationship between energy use per customer and 
5 
customer counts when determining total energy deliveries.  In the 2017 
6 
Electric Rate Case, these models were specified directly as energy 
7 
deliveries models.  The switch to the reconstituted method allows for 
8 
statistically valid and suitable models using this new approach for the 
9 
residential and commercial classes.  In the 2017 Electric Rate Case, in 
10 
part because of the downward impacts of the DERs (which, taken 
11 
together, reduce electric load on the system), the use per customer 
12 
models were not statistically valid.   The industrial model continues to 
13 
use a total energy deliveries model because an energy use per

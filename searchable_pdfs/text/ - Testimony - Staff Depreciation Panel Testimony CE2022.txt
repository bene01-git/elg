Q. 
Can you please explain the terms “net salvage” 
4 
and “depreciation” as used in ratemaking? 
5 
A. 
Net salvage is any cash received for the asset, 
6 
less the cost to remove it.  Depreciation is a 
7 
method of recovering capital costs, less net 
8 
salvage, related to plant in service over the 
9 
expected life of the plant.   
10 
Q. 
Please define “average service life.” 
11 
A. 
ASL is the arithmetic average of the lives of 
12 
the units of property, represented by the area 
13 
under the survivor curve from age zero to the 
14 
maximum life, divided by 100 percent. 
15 
Q. 
What is a “survivor curve?” 
16 
A. 
A survivor curve is a graphical indication of 
17 
the percentage of original plant, by age, that 
18 
is still in service.  Known curves, such as Iowa 
19 
or h-curves, are fit to the observed curve to 
20 
help determine an appropriate ASL.  These known 
21 
curves include a mode, which describes the 
22 
relative height of the curve.  Low mode curves 
23 
have wide retirement patterns, i.e., the assets 
24 


--- Page 11 ---
Cases 22-E-0064 and 22-G-0065 
 
Staff Depreciation Panel 
 
 
10 
 
retire over a wide range of lives and less 
1 
retirements occur near the average service life.  
2 
Curves with higher modes have much narrower 
3 
retirement patterns, with more retirements 
4 
occurring near the ASL.  The area under the 
5 
survivor curve is the ASL or whole life.  The 
6 
observed curves are generated from a mortality 
7 
study that examines the actual retirement 
8 
patterns over multiple years. 
9 
Q. 
Please define “depreciation rate.” 
10 
A. 
A depreciation rate is a percentage rate that is 
11 
applied to the value of gross plant in service, 
12 
by account, to determine the annual depreciation 
13 
expense. 
14 
Q. 
Please define “depreciation expense.” 
15 
A. 
Depreciation expense in any given year is the 
16 
product of gross plant in service multiplied by 
17 
the applicable depreciation rate.  Depreciation 
18 
expense allows for the recovery of an 
19 
investment, less net salvage, over the expected 
20 
life of the investment. 
21 
Q. 
Please define “net salvage factor.” 
22 
A. 
Net salvage factor refers to the component of 
23 
the depreciation rate that allows recovery of

the estimated net salvage.  It is expressed as a 
1 
percentage based on the original cost of plant.  
2 
For example, let us assume that a unit of 
3 
property has a total installed cost of $1,000, 
4 
$0 estimated future salvage, and estimated costs

net salvage factor.

Please define “book reserve.” 
10 
A. 
The book reserve is the continued accumulation

of the annual depreciation expense accruals, 
12

13 
any salvage received.  The book reserve is 
14

the gross plant, or original cost of the plant,

to calculate the net plant, or the remaining 
17 
plant balance that is not yet depreciated.

Q. 
Please explain the process of comparing the book 
19 
reserve to the theoretical reserve. 
20 
A. 
When a depreciation study is performed, the 
21 
current book reserve is compared to the proposed 
22 
theoretical reserve.  The proposed theoretical 
23 
reserve incorporates any proposed changes to the 
24 


--- Page 27 ---
Cases 22-E-0064 and 22-G-0065 
 
Staff Depreciation Panel 
 
 
26 
 
survivor curves, ASLs, and net salvage factors.  
1 
Typically, if the difference between the book 
2 
reserve and the theoretical reserve is within a 
3 
margin of 10 percent, above or below, no 
4 
adjustment is made to amortize the over or 
5 
under-accruals.  However, if the difference is 
6 
greater than 10 percent, an adjustment can be 
7 
made to amortize some or all of the difference 
8 
between the theoretical and book reserves.  If 
9 
the book reserve is larger than the theoretical 
10 
reserve, some or all of the difference can be 
11 
used for other ratemaking treatments, rather 
12 
than a reduction to depreciation expense.  The 
13 
use of a 10 percent margin and the rate 
14 
treatment are discretionary.  An adjustment is 
15 
often made because the difference is too large 
16 
to be corrected on its own, going forward.

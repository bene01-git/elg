Transformation of Electric Utility Business Models 
6 
Q. 
Please describe how the electric utility business model in New York State has 
7 
changed over the last few years. 
8 
A.  
The Commission instituted the REV Proceeding in 2014 to “align electric utility 
9 
practices…with technological advances in information management and power 
10 
generation and distribution.”12  Since this proceeding commenced, the Commission has 
11 
directed New York’s electric utilities to make many fundamental changes in their 
12 
business and operational models both inside and outside of the REV proceeding.  The 
13 
Commission has recognized the value of DER to the grid and has required electric 
14 
utilities to be the DSP provider.  DER, which includes energy efficiency, demand 
15 
response programs, energy storage, and solar photovoltaic technology enables customers 
16 
to use less electricity, reduce their bills, and in many cases provide value to the grid and 
17 
the environment.  In that vein, the Commission has emphasized the need for utilities to 
18 
consider and implement non-wires solutions when such approaches make sense 
19 
                                                 
12

14 
 
economically.  As noted above the Commission also recently increased its focus on non-
1 
pipes alternatives for gas utilities.  Given this, DER penetration is likely to continue in 
2 
the future.  
3 
Q. 
What are the implications of a proliferation of DER? 
4 
A.  
The proliferation of DER in the future creates uncertainty for electric utilities in several 
5 
ways.  First and foremost, customers with DER will be less reliant on the grid to obtain 
6 
power and therefore their use of the grid will decline.  This is significant because the 
7 
credit rating agencies rely heavily on the monopoly ownership of the grid when 
8 
determining that electric utilities have low business risks.  For example, Moody’s states 
9 
that the strength of a utility’s monopoly is a credit concern and that “The strength of the 
10 
monopoly refers to the legal, regulatory and practical obstacles for customers in the 
11 
utility’s territory to obtain service from another provider. Examples of a weakening of 
12 
the monopoly would include the ability of a city or large user to leave the utility system 
13 
to set up their own system, the extent to which self-generation is permitted (e.g. 
14 
cogeneration) and/or encouraged (e.g., net metering, DSM generation).”13  Equally 
15 
telling is Standard & Poor’s statement that the high barriers of entry to the transmission 
16 
and distribution business enable utilities to have a low business risk profile.14  Because 
17 
DER can be viewed as a substitute for the use of the grid, it is a disruptive force capable 
18

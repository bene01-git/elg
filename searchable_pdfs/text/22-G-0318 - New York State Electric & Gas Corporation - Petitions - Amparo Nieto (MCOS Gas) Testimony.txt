III. OVERVIEW OF MARGINAL COST STUDY 
1 
Q.  
Please describe the elements of marginal cost of gas delivery in your study. 
2 
A. 
The MCOS study evaluates all components of the marginal distribution costs of service, 
3 
taking into account the specific characteristics of the Companies’ gas distribution systems 
4 
and their customers.  The following elements of marginal distribution costs were 
5 
examined: 
6 
a) 
Marginal costs of transmission, high-pressure regulator stations, and upper 
7 
medium-pressure mains.  These plant components are sized based on near-
8 
term design-day demands and expanded as load grows;   
9 
b) 
Marginal costs of local facilities, including medium- and low-pressure 
10 
regulator stations and lower medium- and low-pressure mains and their 
11 
associated operations and maintenance (“O&M”) expense.  These costs vary 
12 
with customers’ long-term expected peak-day demands (“design demand”); 
13 
and   
14 
c) 
Marginal customer-related costs—meter, regulator, relief valve and service 
15 
lateral and their associated O&M, and customer-related expenses.    
16 
Q. 
What approach did you use for estimating marginal transportation mains? 
17 
A. 
I reviewed RG&E and NYSEG’s plans for gas transmission projects and there are no 
18 
such projects in its near-term plans.  Consequently, I treated the marginal cost of this 
19 
component as zero in the near term. 
20

Q.  
What approach did you use for estimating marginal high-pressure regulator station and 
1 
upper-medium pressure mains? 
2 
A.  
High-pressure regulator stations connect the high-pressure transmission system to the 
3 
upper medium-pressure system, as illustrated above.  They are sized based on expected 
4 
downstream design-day demand in the near-term, to maintain system reliability.  The 
5 
Companies do not expect any capacity expansion needs at the regulator stations, and 
6 
therefore this component of the study was zero in the current study update.  The 
7 
Companies do identify expansion needs for the upper-medium pressure mains in the next 
8 
planning period.  To estimate growth-related investment in upper-medium pressure mains 
9 
I divided, for each of the Companies, the planned five-year investment in upper medium-
10 
pressure mains by their estimated capacity.  Finally, since I intended to estimate a region-
11 
wide marginal cost, the estimated marginal investment needed to be adjusted to reflect 
12 
the near-term excess capacity situation.  Expected high-pressure regulator station and 
13 
upper-medium pressure mains capacity in the overall service area is larger than the 
14 
minimum required to handle expected load growth in the near term.  The Companies 
15 
expect that natural gas growth will trigger investment only in a small fraction of their 
16 
system, particularly in the case of NYSEG.  I estimated the share of the system’s design 
17 
day demand in upper-medium pressure that is driving new investment in each Company, 
18 
and applied these percentages to the marginal upper-medium pressure main investment to 
19 
compute a system-wide marginal cost for each Company.  These percentages were only 
20 
2% for NYSEG and about 15% for RG&E.  The next step was to apply the economic 
21 
carrying charges, marginal operation and maintenance expenses, and marginal loading 
22 
factors to the marginal investment to convert these costs into annual costs .  The

Q. 
How did you estimate marginal local gas delivery costs? 
3 
A. 
The Companies provided the replacement cost of typical medium- and low-pressure 
4 
regulator stations (including farm tap regulators) in 2022 dollars, as well as the current 
5 
number of regulator stations and farm tap regulators.  I computed the weighted average of 
6 
the replacement cost of medium and low-pressure stations and multiplied them by the 
7 
number of regulators on the system.  To estimate the marginal cost of all existing lower-
8 
medium and low-pressure mains the replacement cost (stated in 2022 dollars) was 
9 
divided by the aggregate medium and low-pressure design demand at the customer 
10 
premises as of year 2021.  The next step was to apply the economic carrying charges to 
11 
obtain an annualized cost, and marginal operation and maintenance expenses, and and 
12 
general plant loading factors to convert marginal investment into annual costs. 
13 
Q. 
Please describe your computation of gas customer-related costs. 
14 
A. 
The Companies provided the current installed cost of gas meters, house regulators and 
15 
relief valves, by customer class.  To these costs, I added the installed cost of the 
16 
communicating module that the Companies plan to begin deploying late this year for the 
17 
majority of the customers, to enable advanced metering infrastructure (“AMI”) 
18 
functionality.  The Companies also provided typical costs of service laterals by class.  
19 
These costs were annualized using the economic carrying charge for service laterals  
20 
Marginal service lateral O&M expenses were added, taking into account recent O&M 
21 
service expenses per customer, and the average number of services per customer.  The 
22 
marginal customer cost also includes an estimate of per-customer customer accounts

IV. USE OF THE MCOS STUDY FOR RATE DESIGN 
5 
Q.  
Please summarize your view on the key guiding principles regarding use of marginal cost 
6 
information for rate design. 
7 
A. 
Efficient pricing would use a rate design that mirrors the structure of the Companies’ 
8 
marginal cost of service, and sets charges for each rate component at marginal cost as a 
9 
starting point, before reconciliation to meet the class revenue requirement.  Efficient rate 
10 
designs for gas delivery service customers would consist of a monthly local facilities 
11 
charge per MCF of design demand, which use meter capacity as a proxy, as this study has 
12 
relied on, and a monthly customer charge.  For service classifications with similar design 
13 
demands or connected loads, the per-kW facilities change could be combined with the 
14 
customer charge. The facilities costs do not change month by month with variations in 
15 
customer usage and therefore they are best recovered in a facilities charge or a per-
16 
customer charge.  Tables 1 and 2 below show what efficient, marginal cost-based charges 
17 
before reconciliation with revenue target for each Company would look like, using the 
18 
current rate structure.

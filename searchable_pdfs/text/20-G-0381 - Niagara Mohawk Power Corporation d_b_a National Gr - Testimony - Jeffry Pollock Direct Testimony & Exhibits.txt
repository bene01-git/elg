Electric ECOS-CU Study 
1 
 
With two material exceptions, NiMo’s Electric ECOS-CU Study generally comports 
2 
with accepted industry practice.  Further, the Electric ECOS-CU Study properly 
3 
recognizes the different types of costs incurred by the Company, as well as the 
4 
different ways electricity is delivered to, and used by, the various types of 
5 
customers.   
6 
 
The first exception is a proposed adjustment to reflect the peak load carrying 
7 
capacity (“PLCC”) of the minimum size distribution system to allocate the demand-
8 
related portion of distribution network costs.  In theory, the PLCC adjustment 
9 
removes the portion of each class’s non-coincident peak (“NCP”) demand served 
10 
from the minimum size distribution system.   
11 
o A PLCC adjustment is unprecedented in New York.   
12 
o Further, NiMo selectively applied the PLCC adjustment only to the SC-1, 
13 
SC-1C, SC-2ND, and Lighting classes.  If the PLCC adjustment is deemed 
14 
appropriate, it should impact all classes taking distribution service.   
15 
o NiMo’s PLCC adjustment is not supported by engineering analysis.  The 
16 
minimum system cannot serve loads above a certain discrete size.  Xcel 
17 
Energy (“Xcel”) in Minnesota has determined that its minimum system, 
18 
which is based on the same size conductor as NiMo’s minimum size 
19 
system, can supply 1.5 kW of load per customer.   
20 
o If the Commission accepts a PLCC adjustment, class NCP demands 
21 
should be reduced by 1.5 kW per customer, and the adjustment should 
22 
apply to all distribution-level classes.   
23 
 
The second exception is with NiMo’s allocation of electric distribution load 
24 
dispatching expense on a volumetric, per kilowatt-hour (“kWh”) basis.   
25 
o Electric distribution load dispatching expense reflects NiMo’s management 
26 
of its distribution system to maintain reliability.  Further, approximately 88%

28

primary demand-related distribution plant.

 
Gas distribution load dispatching provides control over the flow of gas to maintain

Class Revenue Allocation 
7 
 
Electric and gas rates should move closer to cost, limited only by principles of 
8 
gradualism.  Cost-based rates are equitable, encourage conservation and 
9 
efficiency, and also stabilize the utility’s revenues and earnings.  Further, cost-
10 
based rates are consistent with long-standing New York State Public Service 
11 
Commission (“Commission”) policy.   
12 
 
The electric and gas delivery increases should be allocated to service classes in a 
13 
manner that moves each class to cost, while also recognizing gradualism 
14 
principles to the extent necessary to avoid an overly abrupt rate increase.  Except 
15 
in extreme circumstances (i.e., a class that is earning an excessively high or 
16 
substantially negative rate of return), no class should receive an increase higher 
17 
than 1.25 times the system-average delivery rate increase or below 0.75 times the 
18 
system average delivery rate increase, and no class should receive a decrease at 
19 
the requested revenue requirement.   
20 
 
The gradualism constraint should be 1.75 times for classes earning substantially 
21 
negative rates of return.   
22 
 
The use of a 1.25 times gradualism constraint is consistent with past Commission 
23 
practice.   
24 
 
Should the Commission approve a lower delivery revenue increase for NiMo, the 
25 
recommended delivery revenues generally should be reduced proportionally for 
26 
each class.  Additionally, to the extent the Commission authorizes zero or greatly-
27 
reduced electric and/or gas delivery revenue increases, it may be possible to relax 
28 
my recommended gradualism constraints.   
29

30 
 
A proper, cost-based rate design should closely track the customer-related costs 
31 
derived in the Electric ECOS-CU Study.  Thus, Customer charges should recover 
32 
customer-related costs as closely as practicable.   
33 
 
NiMo is proposing only nominal increases in the SC-3 and SC-3A Customer 
34 
charges.  The proposed Customer charges would continue to be substantially 
35 
below the allocated customer-related costs.  Accordingly, the SC-3 and SC-3A 
36 
Customer charges should be increased by at least 50% higher than the 
37 
corresponding Demand charges.

Revenue Decoupling Mechanisms 
1 
 
NiMo is proposing to retain the current structure of the electric and gas revenue 
2 
decoupling mechanisms (“RDM”), and it is proposing to expand the gas RDM to 
3 
include SC-5, SC-8, SC-12, and SC-13.   
4 
 
The loss or gain of customers alone could trigger a rate adjustment, especially for 
5 
lower populated groups that serve very large customers.   
6 
 
RDMs are designed to cushion the impact of energy efficiency programs on a 
7 
utility’s earnings, although it also reduces a utility’s risks associated with variations 
8 
in weather and business cycles.  An RDM should not be designed to recognize 
9 
gains or losses in the number of customers.   
10 
 
The electric and gas RDM reconciliations should be modified so that no adjustment 
11 
is triggered if the revenue variation is solely the result of the gain or loss of 
12 
customers.   
13

2. ELECTRIC ECOS STUDY 
Q 
WHAT IS A CLASS COST-OF-SERVICE STUDY? 
1 
A 
A class cost-of-service study is an analysis used to determine each class’s 
2 
responsibility for the utility’s costs.  Thus, it determines whether the revenues a class 
3 
generates cover the class’s cost-of-service.  A class cost-of-service study separates 
4 
the utility's total costs into portions incurred on behalf of the various customer groups.  
5 
Most of a utility's costs are incurred to jointly serve many customers.  For purposes of 
6 
revenue allocation and rate design, customers are grouped into homogeneous classes 
7 
according to their usage patterns and service characteristics.  The procedures used in 
8 
a cost-of-service study are described in more detail in Exhibit ___ (JP-3).   
9 
Q 
HAS NIMO CONDUCTED AN EMBEDDED COST-OF-SERVICE STUDY FOR 
10 
ELECTRICITY SERVICE IN THIS PROCEEDING?  
11 
A 
Yes.  NiMo presented an ECOS Study in its original filing.2  In addition, it provided an 
12 
ECOS Study that incorporated changes reflected in the CU filing (Electric ECOS-CU 
13 
Study) relative to the originally filed ECOS Study.3  Just prior to the filing date of this 
14 
testimony, NiMo presented a revised Electric ECOS-CU Study in which AMI costs 
15 
were allocated in accordance with its proposed cost allocation framework.4  I discuss

AMI cost allocation later in my direct testimony.

4. CLASS REVENUE ALLOCATION 
Q 
WHAT IS CLASS REVENUE ALLOCATION? 
1 
A 
Class revenue allocation is the process of determining how any delivery revenue 
2 
change approved by the Commission should be spread to each service class served 
3 
by the utility.   
4 
Q 
HOW SHOULD ANY CHANGE IN DELIVERY REVENUES APPROVED IN THIS 
5 
PROCEEDING BE SPREAD AMONG THE VARIOUS SERVICE CLASSES SERVED 
6 
BY NIMO? 
7 
A 
Delivery revenues should reflect the actual cost of providing service as closely as 
8 
practicable.  As a general rule, rates should be set at cost.  However, regulators 
9 
sometimes limit the immediate movement to cost of service based on other 
10 
considerations, such as gradualism concerns.  Gradualism is a concept that the 
11 
Commission historically has used to prevent a class from receiving an overly-large 
12 
rate increase.  Under this practice, the movement to cost of service is made gradually, 
13 
rather than all at once.   
14 
Q 
WHY SHOULD THE RESULTS OF A COST-OF-SERVICE STUDY BE THE 
15 
PRIMARY FACTOR IN DETERMINING HOW ANY BASE REVENUE CHANGE

Q 
WHY ARE COST-BASED RATES EQUITABLE? 
1 
A 
Rates that primarily reflect cost-of-service considerations are equitable because each 
2 
customer pays what it actually costs the utility to serve that customer – no more and 
3 
no less.  If rates are not based on cost, then some customers must pay part of the cost 
4 
of providing service to other customers, which is inequitable. 
5 
Q 
HOW DO COST-BASED RATES PROMOTE ENGINEERING EFFICIENCY? 
6 
A 
With respect to engineering efficiency, when rates are designed so that customer, 
7 
demand and/or energy charges are properly reflected in the rate structure, customers 
8 
are provided with the proper incentive to minimize their costs which will, in turn, 
9 
minimize the costs to the utility. 
10 
Q 
HOW CAN COST-BASED RATES PROVIDE STABILITY? 
11 
A 
When rates are tied closely to cost, the utility's earnings are stabilized because 
12 
changes in customer use patterns result in parallel changes in revenues and 
13 
expenses.  If rates are not based on cost, then an increase in usage by subsidized 
14 
classes or a decrease in usage by classes providing subsidies would adversely affect 
15 
the utility’s earnings.  That is because, as subsidized classes grow, a utility’s revenue 
16 
shortfall also will grow over time.  Conversely, as an overcharged customer class 
17 
grows, its customers become increasingly important to utility earnings, while those 
18 
customers are simultaneously given greater incentives to reduce their use of the 
19 
utility’s system.

PROCEDURE FOR CONDUCTING A CLASS COST-OF-SERVICE STUDY 
Q 
WHAT PROCEDURES ARE USED IN A CLASS COST-OF-SERVICE STUDY? 
1 
A 
The basic procedure for conducting a class cost-of-service study is fairly simple.  First, 
2 
we identify the different types of costs (functionalization), determine their primary 
3 
causative factors (classification), and then apportion each item of cost among the 
4 
various service classes (allocation).  Adding up the individual pieces gives the total 
5 
cost for each class.  
6 
 
 
Identifying the utility’s different levels of operation is a process referred to as 
7 
functionalization.  The utility’s electric investments and expenses are separated into 
8 
production, transmission, distribution, and other functions.  Gas investments and 
9 
expenses are separated into production, storage, transmission, distribution, and other 
10 
functions.  To a large extent, this is done in accordance with the Uniform System of 
11 
Accounts developed by the FERC.   
12 
 
 
Once costs have been functionalized, the next step is to identify the primary 
13 
causative factor (or factors).  This step is referred to as classification.  Costs are 
14 
classified as demand-related, energy (or commodity)-related or customer-related.  
15 
Demand (or capacity) related costs vary with peak demand, which is measured in 
16 
kilowatts or peak day send out.  This includes production, transmission, and some 
17 
distribution investment and related fixed O&M expenses.  As explained later, peak 
18 
demand determines the amount of capacity needed for reliable service.  Energy-
19 
related costs vary with the production of energy or natural gas throughput, which is 
20 
measured in kilowatt-hours or dekatherms.  They include fuel and variable O&M 
21 
expense.  Customer-related costs vary directly with the number of customers and 
22 
include expenses such as a portion of the electric distribution network and gas 
23 
distribution mains, meters, service drops, billing, and customer service.   
24

Each functionalized and classified cost must then be allocated to the various 
1 
customer classes.  This is accomplished by developing allocation factors that reflect 
2 
the percentage of the total cost that should be paid by each class.  The allocation 
3 
factors should reflect cost-causation; that is, the degree to which each class caused 
4 
the utility to incur the cost.   
5 
 
 
Further, each customer class should be comprised of customers having similar 
6 
characteristics.  The relevant characteristics include the type of end-use customer 
7 
(e.g., residential, lighting, standby), average size, load factor, coincidence factor and 
8 
delivery voltage.  Allocating costs to homogeneous customer classes will ensure that 
9 
the rates derived from a class cost-of-service study are just and reasonable and reflect 
10 
the actual cost to serve.   
11 
Q 
WHAT KEY PRINCIPLES ARE RECOGNIZED IN A CLASS COST-OF-SERVICE 
12 
STUDY FOR ELECTRIC DELIVERY SERVICE?  
13 
A 
A properly conducted CCOSS recognizes two key cost-causation principles.  First, 
14 
customers are served at different delivery voltages.  This affects the amount of 
15 
investment the utility must make to deliver electricity to the meter.  Second, since cost 
16 
causation is also related to how electricity is used, both the timing and rate of energy 
17 
consumption (i.e., demand) are critical.  Because electricity cannot be stored for any 
18 
significant time period, a utility must size the required delivery facilities to meet the 
19 
maximum projected demand.  Customers that use electricity during the critical peak 
20 
hours cause the utility to invest in delivery facilities.   
21

Q 
ARE THE KEY PRINCIPLES RECOGNIZED IN CONDUCTING A CLASS COST-OF-
1 
SERVICE STUDY FOR AN ELECTRIC DELIVERY COMPANY SIMILAR FOR A 
2 
LOCAL GAS DISTRIBUTION COMPANY? 
3 
A 
Yes.  A properly conducted CCOSS for a gas local distribution company (LDC) 
4 
recognizes two key cost-causation principles.  First, not all gas customers purchase 
5 
gas supplied by the LDC.  Some customers purchase and transport their own gas to 
6 
the city gate.  Thus, the LDC does not incur purchased gas and other related costs to 
7 
serve a transportation customer.  Second, not all customers take the same delivery 
8 
service.  Larger transportation customers may take delivery service directly from either 
9 
the transmission system or high-pressure distribution mains.  Third, the use of storage 
10 
services will depend on the tolerances between actual and nominated gas deliveries.  
11 
The smaller the tolerances, the lower the amount of storage services.  Fourth, since 
12 
cost causation is also related to how natural gas is used, both the timing and rate of 
13 
gas consumption (i.e., demand) are critical.  Consistent with the obligation to serve 
14 
and to ensure reliability, the LDC must purchase sufficient gas supply to meet the 
15 
maximum needs of its sales customers.  The LDC must also construct the required 
16

n. DEMAND AND CUSTOMER CLASSIFICATIONS
OF
DISTRIBUTION
PLANT ACCOUNTS
When the utility installs distribution plant to provide service to a customer and
to meet the individual customer's peak demand requirements, the utility must classify
distribution plant data separately into demand- and customer-related costs.
Classifying distribution plant as a demand cost assigns investment of that plant to
a customer or group of customers based upon its contribution to some total peak load.
The reason is that costs are incurred to serve area load, rather than a specific number of
customers.
Distribution substations costs (which include Accounts 360 -Land and Land
Rights, 361 - Structures and Improvements, and 362 -Station Equipment), are normally
classified as demand-related.
This classification is adopted because substations are nor-
mally built to serve a particular load and their size is not affected by the number of cus-
tomers to be served.
Distribution plant Accounts 364 through 370 involve demand and customer costs.
The customer component of distribution facilities is that portion of costs which varies
with the number of customers. Thus, the number of poles, conductors, transformers, serv-
ices, and meters are directly related to the number of customers on the utility's system.
As shown in Table 6-1, each primary plant account can be separately classified into a de-
mand and customer component.

tomer components of distribution facilities. They are, the minimum-size-of-facilities

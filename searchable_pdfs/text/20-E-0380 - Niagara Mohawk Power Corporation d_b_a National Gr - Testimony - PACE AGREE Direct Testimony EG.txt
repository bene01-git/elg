22

23

24 
gas is sometimes called “fossil gas” or more often simply “natural gas.”5

Q. 
 Where does RNG typically come from? 
12 
A. 
Methane RNG can be sourced from biomass, like manure or material at wastewater 
13 
treatment plants. This methane RNG is sometimes called “biomethane.” Once 
14 
biomethane is available, RNG is produced from the biomethane via various purification, 
15 
pressurization, and other processes designed to make the RNG compatible with fossil gas 
16 
infrastructure. RNG can also be produced using power-to-gas (“P2G”) technology. This 
17 
technology allows for the manufacture of synthetic methane from carbon dioxide and 
18 
water.15 P2G also allows for the manufacture of hydrogen from water using electricity. 
19 
 
20 
Q.  
Can you elaborate on how biomass is transformed into biomethane? 
21 
A. 
In some circumstances (e.g., sealed landfills), biomass breaks down under anaerobic (no 
22 
oxygen) conditions without intervention and transforms into methane. But often, biomass 
23 
is intentionally transformed into biomethane using anaerobic digesters or other deliberate 
24 
interventions such as gasification.16

Q.  
Does the manner in which biomethane is produced have implications for climate 
1 
change? 
2 
A. 
Intentionally intervening to increase methane production means converting carbon that 
3 
otherwise would have become carbon dioxide into a greenhouse gas with a higher GWP. 
4 
In other words, a powerful greenhouse gas that would not otherwise have existed is 
5 
intentionally produced. If this intentionally produced methane is released into the 
6 
atmosphere, then it will have significant climate impacts.  
7 
 
8 
Q. 
Can you explain the power-to-gas technology that allows for the production of 
9 
synthetic methane and hydrogen in some more detail? 
10 
A.  
Power-to-gas (“P2G”) is a process that can be used to produce either hydrogen or 
11 
methane. In this process, electricity is used to split water into hydrogen and oxygen. The 
12 
hydrogen can either be pressurized and used directly as hydrogen RNG or used as a 
13 
feedstock for synthetic methane, which entails combining this hydrogen and some source 
14 
of CO2 to make methane. No gases manufactured via P2G pathways would have existed 
15 
otherwise, so in both cases, P2G entails manufacturing powerful GHGs that have 
16 
significant climate impacts if released.

A. MAJOR OBSTACLES TO SCALING UP THE COMPANY’S RNG 
1 
PROGRAMS. 
2 
 
3 
Q.  
Is there enough RNG available to fully decarbonize the Company’s gas network?  
4 
A. 
Likely no. Both true waste methane and methane that can be generated from wastes are 
5 
limited,34 as is the realistic potential to generate RNG from P2G. 
6 
 
7 
Q.  
Why can’t the Company produce enough RNG through P2G to fully decarbonize 
8 
the gas network?  
9 
A.  
Synthetic methane and hydrogen produced through P2G are theoretically scalable but the 
10 
amount produced depends on large amounts of available electricity at reasonable cost. It 
11 
will be especially difficult to decarbonize the network by relying on renewable electricity 
12 
to produce synthetic methane and hydrogen.35 For example, the Company acknowledges 
13 
that supplying sufficient green hydrogen to meet the demand of its customers on a peak 
14 
winter day would require electricity inputs similar in scale to the entire installed summer 
15 
capacity of upstate New York, or 13.3 – 17.2 GW depending on assumed electrolysis 
16 
efficiency.36 It’s hard to see where all that excess electricity would come from. Keep in 
17 
mind that renewable electricity will be in high demand in the coming decades as 
18 
emerging alternative potential users for excess electricity, including direct air carbon 
19 
capture facilities, flexible industries, battery users, and others compete for supply. And, 
20 
as things stand in New York, an enormous amount of renewable electricity will need to 
21 
be used in the upcoming decades to ensure that the state meets the CLCPA’s zero-carbon 
22 
electricity mandate by 2040. It is unlikely that there will be much to spare for the

6 
GHG emissions assessment and similar analyses. For example, an analysis might 
7

8 
end-of-life (“cradle-to-grave”) or from resource production through release to a user

12 
emissions would include leakage from customer appliances in addition to leakage from 
13 
production, processing, transportation, and storage events.

18 
emissions. Consider the Company’s claim that combustion of RNG does not result in new 
19 
GHG emissions into the atmosphere.66 The problem with this claim is that combustion 
20 
emissions are not the same as life cycle emissions. Life cycle emissions typically include 
21 
emissions associated with production, processing, transportation, use, and end-of-life life 
22 
cycle stages. Gases like methane and hydrogen can leak into the atmosphere at any of 
23 
these life cycle stages. To provide an accurate and informative emissions assessment, the 
24 
company must consider system boundaries and do so in a clear and consistent manner.

IV. 
RECOMMENDATIONS 
12 
 
13 
Q. 
Should the Company proceed with its four RNG proposals? 
14 
A. 
I would strongly recommend against proceeding with any of the four proposals. The 
15 
Company has failed to supply compelling evidence or analysis that the proposed 
16 
expenditures and investments will allow the service area to take meaningful steps towards 
17 
meeting the CLCPA’s aggressive mandate of an 85% emissions reduction by 2050. The 
18

19

20

21 
evaluated before investing in long-lived infrastructure or contracts. That has not 
22 
happened here. Finally, whatever short-term GHG reductions might be secured by the 
23 
Company’s investments in RNG procurement and interconnection will be negated by the 
24 
Company’s failure to secure the environmental attributes of the fuel.

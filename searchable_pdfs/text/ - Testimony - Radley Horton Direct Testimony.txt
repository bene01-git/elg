A.  
THE SCIENTIFIC FOUNDATION OF CLIMATE PROJECTIONS 
1 
Q. 
WHAT ARE GLOBAL CLIMATE MODELS? 
2 
A. 
Global climate models are mathematical representations of the physical processes 
3 
that govern the Earth’s climate.  They are comprised of hundreds to thousands of 
4 
pages of computer code that are compiled by supercomputers for a variety of 
5 
applications.  Climate models are the best tools available for projecting the future 
6 
state of the climate, and they have been embraced by a number of scientific 
7 
bodies including the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (“IPCC”).  (The 
8 
IPCC is the leading international scientific body charged with assessing climate 
9 
change science, impacts, and potential solutions.)  Computer models are standard 
10 
research tools that are used in a variety of contexts to understand and predict the 
11 
dynamics of complex systems, including regional or global climate.  Model 
12 
projections of future climate have improved over time as scientific understanding 
13 
and computing power have advanced.   
14 
Q. 
CAN YOU TEST THE RELIABILITY OF MODEL OUTPUTS? 
15 
A. 
Yes.  Climate models have been validated, or back-tested, using climates of the 
16 
past.  Models are back-tested by inputting prior conditions such as historic 
17 
greenhouse gas concentrations, changes in incoming solar radiation, or the impact 
18 
of volcanic eruptions.  Model outputs are then evaluated to determine whether the 
19 
predicted results match the observed conditions.  It has been demonstrated in this 
20 
manner that climate models are able to reproduce the general climate responses 
21 


--- Page 7 ---
Cases: 16-G-0058 
New York City  
 
16-G-0059 
Radley Horton, Ph.D. 
 
6 
 
 
that actually occurred.  These responses go far beyond surface air temperature, 
1 
and include everything from temperature gradients in the upper atmosphere to the 
2 
seasonal progression of monsoons.  By looking at all of these outputs, we can 
3 
develop a variety of theories.  The modeling results point to a dominant influence 
4 
of greenhouse gas increases on the climate, and indicate that the climate 
5 
projections produced by the models are useful. 
6 
Q. 
ARE CLIMATE MODELS OUR ONLY TOOLS FOR PROJECTING 
7 
FUTURE CLIMATE?   
8 
A. 
No.  Some physical processes – such as melting at the edge of an ice sheet and 
9 
other events that occur at fine spatial scales – exceed the capability of even the 
10 
most powerful climate models.  In those instances, we rely primarily on observed 
11 
data and expert knowledge as a basis for climate projections.  In general it is the 
12 
combination of climate model results, observed data, and expert knowledge based 
13 
on physical understanding that yield climate risk-based approaches to decision-
14 
making. 
15 
Q. 
WHAT DO YOU MEAN BY CLIMATE RISK-BASED APPROACHES 
16 
FOR DECISION-MAKING? 
17 
A. 
Risk-based approaches present a range of possible outcomes to inform decision-
18 
making.  They are based on the principle that although the future is uncertain, 
19 
there is enough information available to inform decision-making, especially 
20 
relative to the alternate approach of assuming that what was experienced in the 
21

past will continue without change.  Several scientific and professional bodies 
1 
including the National Research Council (“NRC”) have embraced risk-based 
2 
approaches of the type described below that were developed for New York City 
3 
and Long Island.  The future climate cannot be known with precision for a variety 
4 
of reasons including uncertainty about exactly how much greenhouse gases 
5 
society will emit and exactly how sensitive the climate system will be to those 
6 
emissions.  However, climate science has advanced to the point where we can say 
7 
with high confidence that decisions involving important and expensive long-lived 
8 
assets should reflect a range of possible future climate outcomes and not rely 
9 
solely on the static historical climate data.

12

17

18

WILL CLIMATE CHANGE IMPACT THE FUTURE FREQUENCY OF 1-
10 
IN-100 YEAR FLOODS? 
11 
A. 
The NPCC projects that the current likelihood of a hundred-year coastal flood will 
12 
increase.  By the 2020s, the probability of water heights reaching the level 
13 
corresponding to the current 1-in-100 year flood is projected to increase from the 
14 
current probability of 1.0% per year, to 1.1% to 1.7% per year.  By the 2050s, this

17

18

19 
consideration of how storms themselves may change.

18 
Islip event produced more than 13 inches of rain over a 24 hour period, including 
19 
more than one inch in 9 minutes. This event produced life threatening flash 
20 
flooding, the shutdown of major highways, and damage to critical infrastructure.

1 
Q. 
What is leak prone pipe?  
2 
A. 
Generally, LPP is unprotected steel pipe, cast 
3 
iron pipe, wrought iron, and some vintages of 
4 
plastic pipe that may become brittle.  
5 
Q. 
What does unprotected mean in this context?  
6 
A. 
For purposes of the LPP removal pipeline safety 
7 
measure, unprotected means that the pipe lacks 
8 
adequate cathodic protection rendering it 
9 
susceptible to corrosion.  Unprotected steel 
10 
pipe is also commonly referred to as bare steel 
11 
pipe. 
12 
Q. 
What is Cathodic protection? 
13 
A. 
Cathodic protection is a method by which steel 
14 
pipelines are protected from corrosion by making 
15 
the surface a cathode, thereby inhibiting the 
16 
formation of iron oxides which can result in 
17 
reduced wall thickness of the pipe.  Unprotected 
18 
pipe often has no surface coating or inadequate 
19 
coating, rendering efforts to cathodically 
20 
protect the pipe ineffective and uneconomical.   
21 
Q. 
How does the LPP removal program contribute to 
22 
the safety of the Companies’ gas systems?  
23 
A. 
Leaks on underground piping can create safety 
24 


--- Page 25 ---
Cases 23-G-0225 & 23-G-0226  
    SPSP  
 
24 
 
 
risks to the public and can potentially lead to 
1 
gas-related incidents, such as fires or more 
2 
serious catastrophic events that could impact 
3 
health and safety.  LPP generally leaks at a 
4 
higher rate than coated and cathodically 
5 
protected steel or plastic pipes so LPP poses a 
6 
higher risk of failure or gas-related incidents.  
7 
Since other methods to resolve the leak are 
8 
infeasible, ineffective or uneconomical the 
9 
removal of LPP aptly reduces these safety risks.  
10 
Q. 
Explain the importance of removing unprotected 
11 
or bare steel and cast-iron pipe.  
12 
A. 
Unprotected or bare steel pipes are more prone 
13 
to leakage because they are more susceptible to 
14 
corrosion.  Removal of these pipes reduces risks 
15 
associated with leakage.  As for cast iron pipe, 
16 
it is brittle, susceptible to graphitization 
17 
which is a form of corrosion, and has low beam 
18 
strength meaning that the material can fail if 
19 
it is subjected to heightened loads or a loss of 
20 
ground support, which makes the material

particularly susceptible to stresses resulting

from underground disturbances.  This includes

ground settlement, freeze-thaw cycles, soil 
24

erosion, or nearby excavation activities.  Its

physical characteristics make it more prone to

catastrophic failure than cathodically protected 
3 
steel and plastic pipe.  In the event of a major 
4 
leak or failure, these circumstances may lead to 
5 
increased migration of below ground gas and pose 
6 
a greater risk of fires or explosions.  Removal 
7 
of this type of pipe reduces the potential for 
8 
leaks and incidents resulting from its failure.  
9

10 
This is important since most bare steel or cast-
11 
iron pipe was installed many decades ago, it

where there are many enclosed structures and

II. 
Depreciation Concepts 
17 
Q. 
What is utility depreciation? 
18 
A. 
The National Association of Regulated Utility Commissioners (“NARUC”), in Public 
19 
Utility Depreciation Practices (commonly referred to as the NARUC Depreciation 
20 
Manual) defines depreciation this way: 
21 
As applied to the depreciable plant of utilities, the term depreciation means 
22 
the loss in service value not restored by current maintenance, incurred in 
23 
connection with the consumption or prospective retirement of utility plant 
24 
in the course of service from causes that are known to be in current 
25

5 
 
operation, against which the company is not protected by insurance, and the 
1 
effect of which can be forecast with reasonable accuracy. Among the causes 
2 
to be considered are wear and tear, decay, action of the elements, 
3 
inadequacy, obsolescence, changes in the art, changes in demand, and the 
4 
requirement of public authorities.5 
5 
 
6 
It goes on to define depreciation accounting as “the process of charging the book cost of 
7 
depreciable property, adjusted for net salvage, to operations over its useful life.”6 
8 
From these definitions, we glean two primary parameters that control depreciation 
9 
rates and expenses for public utilities.  First, if we are distributing the book cost of property 
10 
over its useful life, then we must estimate the useful life of the plant.  Second, the book 
11 
cost of plant should be adjusted for net salvage.   
12 
A simplistic calculation of depreciation expense based on just the service life looks 
13 
like this: 
14 
Plant Value ($) / Service Life (years) = Depreciation Expense ($/year) 
15 
Q. 
How are do we estimate the useful life of plant in service? 
16 
A. 
The Retirement Rate method is an actuarial technique used to study plant lives, much like 
17 
the actuarial techniques used in the insurance industry to study human lives.  It requires a 
18 
record of the dates of placement (birth) and retirement (death) for each asset unit studied. 
19 
Retirement data that contains this date of placement and retirement is referred to as “aged 
20 
data” because it tells the analyst the age of the plant at the time it was retired.  The 
21 
Retirement Rate method is the most sophisticated of the statistical life analysis methods 
22 
because it relies on the most refined level of data.

Q.

8

9 
A. 
Most companies, although not Niagara Mohawk, utilize the remaining life methodology 
10

11

12

13

14

15

16

17

18

Gross Salvage – Cost of Removal = Net Salvage 
1 
Gross salvage is the theoretical value of property after retirement which the utility can 
2 
theoretically recoup through the sale of parts and scrap metal.  Cost of removal is the 
3 
expense the utility incurs for the process of retiring plant in service.     
4 
 
One thing that is important to understand about net salvage is that in recent history 
5 
for most companies and for most accounts, cost of removal exceeds gross salvage.  For 
6 
most accounts, net salvage will be a negative number. 
7 
Q. 
Is net salvage included in depreciation rates as it is incurred or is it collected in 
8 
depreciation expense prospectively? 
9 
A. 
The Depreciation Manual indicates that net salvage is intended to be included on an accrual 
10 
accounting basis.  The cost of net salvage is intended to be collected over time as part of 
11 
the service value of the plant that will be removed.  Thus, depreciation expense includes 
12 
net salvage that the company has not yet incurred, which we refer to as “future net salvage.”  
13 
Because net salvage is typically collected prospectively, the amount of future net salvage 
14 
that needs to be collected must be estimated as part of a depreciation study. 
15 
Q. 
How is future net salvage estimated? 
16 
A. 
Typically, the amount of future net salvage (“FNS”) to be collected along with plant in 
17 
service is estimated by determining a net salvage percentage.  A simple formula would 
18 
look like this. 
19 
Plant in Service * Net Salvage % = Future Net Salvage 
20 
 
Thus, the purpose of net salvage analysis is to arrive at an appropriate net salvage 
21 
percentage for each account.  The way that this is typically done is to compare historical 
22 
retirements to historical cost of removal in a ratio.  The ratio of cost of removal over

calculate FNS.

Once net salvage is calculated, it is incorporated into the calculation of what we call 
3 
“future accruals.”  A simple calculation of future accruals looks like this: 
4 
Plant in Service – FNS – Depreciation Reserve = Future Accruals 
5 
Looking at the formula above, we can see that because net salvage—and thus, FNS—is 
6 
negative in most cases, then FNS serves to increase future accruals. 
7 
Q. 
Can you summarize these depreciation concepts? 
8 
A. 
Yes.  To summarize, let us now look at a depreciation expense calculation that 
9 
incorporates all of the concepts discussed thus far.  This is a complete, but simple, 
10 
calculation of depreciation expense using the remaining life methodology. 
11 
(Plant – FNS – Reserve) / Remaining Life = Depreciation Expense ($/year) 
12 
 
This calculation and the foregoing discussion demonstrate that the two primary drivers of 
13 
depreciation expense are the selected service life and net salvage percentages. 
14

17

Q.

A. 
The NARUC Depreciation Manual lists a number of factors that can cause utility plant to 
4

5

6

7 
b. Decay or deterioration 
8

9 
2. Functional Factors 
10 
a. Inadequacy 
11 
b. Obsolescence 
12 
c. Changes in the art and technology 
13 
d. Changes in demand 
14 
e. Requirements of public authorities 
15 
f. Management discretion 
16 
3. Contingent Factors 
17 
a. Casualties or disasters 
18 
b. Extraordinary obsolescence 
19

20 
functional factors, such as a combination of inadequacy and obsolescence in conjunction 
21

22

24 
given careful consideration when determining average service lives.  The Depreciation 
25 
Manual acknowledges this with the identification of “requirements of public authorities” 
26 
as a factor that can result in plant retirement.27

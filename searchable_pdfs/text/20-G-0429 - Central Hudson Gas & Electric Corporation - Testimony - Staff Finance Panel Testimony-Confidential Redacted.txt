FAIR RATE OF RETURN DISCUSSION 
8 
Q. 
Earlier, the Panel mentioned that the fair rate 
9 
of return that it recommends will be used to 
10 
establish the Company’s revenue requirement.  
11 
Please explain what the Panel means by revenue 
12 
requirement. 
13 
A. 
In the context of Commission rate proceedings, 
14 
the revenue requirement is the dollar amount 
15 
required by a company to provide service during 
16 
a rate year.  It is the amount that will allow 
17 
the company to recover all of its reasonably 
18 
expected operating costs, including taxes and 
19 
depreciation.  In addition, the revenue 
20 
requirement includes a fair return that will 
21 
allow the utility the opportunity to recover the 
22 
cost of funds supplied to it by investors.  The 
23 
funds provided by these investors are necessary 
24 


--- Page 11 ---
Cases 20-E-0428 & 20-G-0429   
SFP 
 
 
10 
 
for the company to finance its long-term utility 
1 
assets and working capital requirements, which 
2 
in the rate-setting context are referred to as 
3 
its “rate base.”  
4 
Q. 
Generally speaking, what is a fair rate of 
5 
return for a regulated utility? 
6 
A. 
A fair rate of return for a regulated utility is 
7 
one that enables the company to provide safe and 
8 
adequate service to its customers, while at the 
9 
same time assuring the utility continuing 
10 
support in the capital markets for both its 
11 
long-term debt and common equity securities at 
12 
terms that are reasonable given the company’s 
13 
risk.   
14 
Q. 
How is a fair rate of return calculated? 
15 
A.   The rate of return for a utility company is 
16 
calculated through a weighted average of the 
17 
individual cost components of its expected 
18 
capitalization during the rate year. 
19 
Typically, there are three sources of capital.  
20 
The two primary sources are long-term debt and 
21 
common equity.  Customer deposits, while a very 
22 
small component, are almost always reflected in 
23 
a company’s capitalization because it is a 
24 


--- Page 12 ---
Cases 20-E-0428 & 20-G-0429   
SFP 
 
 
11 
 
relatively permanent and stable source of 
1 
capital employed by utilities.  It should be 
2 
noted that preferred stock is sometimes a source 
3 
of capital as well, although generally in much 
4 
smaller proportions than either long-term debt 
5 
or common equity.   
6 
 
 
Since the Commission uses a fully 
7 
forecasted rate year, it is also important that 
8 
the rate year capitalization reflect the 
9 
utility’s projected capital requirements and 
10 
that it is consistent with the goal of achieving 
11 
the optimal cost of capital. 
12 
Q. 
What do you mean by the optimal cost of capital? 
13 
A. 
Debt financing is significantly cheaper than 
14 
equity because it ranks higher than equity in 
15 
the event of a bankruptcy.  However, as the 
16 
proportion of a company’s rate base that is 
17 
financed with long-term debt increases, so does 
18 
its financial risk.  All else the same, an 
19 
increase in leverage will decrease a company’s 
20 
financial flexibility and increase both the cost 
21 
of new borrowing along with the cost of equity.  
22 
Therefore, a utility’s management should strive 
23 
to employ as much inexpensive debt as it 
24 


--- Page 13 ---
Cases 20-E-0428 & 20-G-0429   
SFP 
 
 
12 
 
reasonably can without undermining the company’s 
1 
financial flexibility along with its continued 
2 
access to reasonable costing capital. 
3 
Q. 
How are the cost rates of the components of the 
4 
capital structure typically calculated? 
5 
A. 
The cost rates associated with a utility’s long-
6 
term debt and customer deposits are relatively 
7 
straightforward to ascertain.  The weighted 
8 
average cost rate of a utility’s existing long-
9 
term debt can be readily calculated by examining 
10 
their contractual terms; e.g., the interest 
11 
payment on the long-term debt and the 
12 
amortization of issuance costs.  The costs of 
13 
any new long-term debt instruments, however, 
14 
require estimation.  The cost rate for customer 
15 
deposits is a matter of applying the cost rate 
16 
that is currently prescribed by the Commission. 
17 
The cost of common equity, however, is 
18 
neither contractual nor prescribed by the 
19 
Commission.  Its calculation is further 
20 
complicated by the fact that all variables 
21 
affecting a company’s cost of capital cannot be 
22 
directly observed, and instead some must be 
23 
estimated.

 Q. Is the cost of common equity typically more  
1 
 
expensive than the cost of debt for a utility? 
2 
A. 
Yes.  Even though both lenders and equity 
3 
investors supply the utility with the funds it 
4

to ratepayers that adds to the expense of the

equity return.

and liabilities between the parent and

be biased or potentially inaccurate.  Similarly, 
11 
beta determinations used in the CAPM methodology

are based on historical observations that, due 
13 
to circumstances such as corporate 
14 
restructurings or major industry 
15 
transformations, may not be representative of 
16 
the level of earnings volatility expected in the 
17 
future.  By employing a sufficiently large proxy 
18 
group of similarly situated companies in our 
19 
analyses, we can minimize the undesirable 
20 
effects of bias, both upward and downward, or 
21 
inaccurate estimates for any one company.

CAPITAL ASSET PRICING MODEL 
15 
Q. 
Please describe the basic theory underlying the 
16 
CAPM. 
17 
A. 
The principle behind the CAPM theory is that 
18 
there is no premium, in terms of expected 
19 
return, for bearing risks that can be eliminated 
20 
through diversification.  According to the CAPM, 
21 
rational investors will hold a portfolio of 
22 
stocks, generally 60 or more, such that the 
23 
overall risk of the portfolio, in terms of 
24 


--- Page 88 ---
Cases 20-E-0428 & 20-G-0429   
SFP 
 
 
87 
 
variability of its returns, closely approximates 
1 
that of the market as a whole.  Thus, the only 
2 
risk that matters in the CAPM equation is said 
3 
to be “systematic” risk, or risk that cannot be 
4 
diversified away. 
5 
 
 
“Systematic” risk is a risk common to all 
6 
equity securities that cannot be eliminated 
7 
through diversification.  It is measured by a 
8 
variable denominated as “beta.”  Systematic risk 
9 
is also known as market risk, which is the 
10 
uncertainty inherent to the entire market or 
11 
market segment.  Changes in interest rates, 
12 
recessions, and wars are all examples of 
13 
“systematic” risks. 
14 
 
 
“Unsystematic” risk, on the other hand, is 
15 
risk that is specific to a particular stock.  
16 
While it is assumed that most stocks tend to go 
17 
along with the general market, at least to some 
18 
extent, factors that are specific to an 
19 
individual stock are said to affect its 
20 
“unsystematic” risk. 
21 
 
 
According to the CAPM, the appropriate way 
22 
to measure an individual stock’s risk is through 
23 
a correlation of its return relative to the 
24 


--- Page 89 ---
Cases 20-E-0428 & 20-G-0429   
SFP 
 
 
88 
 
market as a whole, known as beta.  A stock with 
1 
a beta of 1.0 has a return that mirrors the 
2 
return of the “market,” usually taken to mean 
3 
the S&P 500, as a whole.  Betas of less than 
4 
1.0, which are typical for utility stocks given 
5 
the moderating influence of regulation and the 
6 
accompanying perception of reduced risk, 
7 
indicate that the stocks are less volatile than 
8 
the market as a whole.  Therefore, the CAPM 
9 
informs us that investors will only be 
10 
compensated for their actual risk, as measured 
11 
by beta.  Thus, in the case of estimating the 
12 
return requirements of utility investors, the 
13 
CAPM result will reflect the degree to which 
14 
utility stocks are less volatile than the market 
15 
as a whole. 
16

A. 
The model is based on the assumptions that: (1) 
18 
the capital market is competitive and efficient; 
19 
(2) investors are risk-averse and demand higher 
20 
returns for higher risk; (3) market participants 
21 
can lend and borrow unlimited amounts under the 
22 
risk-free rate of interest; and, (4) investors 
23 
hold diversified portfolios and operate in 
24

SFP

capital markets with no transaction costs, 
1 
taxes, or restrictions on financial 
2 
transactions.  Under the CAPM model, the 
3 
expected return of a security or portfolio is 
4 
equal to the rate on a risk-free security plus a 
5 
risk premium multiplied by the asset’s 
6 
systematic risk.

I. 
WE ACT’s Argument In Opposition To Section IV.7.8. Of The Joint Proposal Relies 
On Baseless Assumptions And Mischaracterizations Of Both Law And Fact 
In recommending that the Commission reject Section IV.7.8. of the Joint Proposal, WE 
ACT seeks to derail the development of biomethane in the Companies’ service territories; a result 
that is inconsistent with applicable New York State policy and Commission precedent.  As the 
Commission found in its 2023 Con Edison Rate Order,2 the New York Climate Action Counsel’s 
Final Scoping Plan recognizes that renewable natural gas (“RNG”) (biomethane) may be 
considered to decarbonize the gas system as it transitions.3  Based on this finding, the Commission 
further found that an RNG project proposed by Con Edison would allow it to investigate the use 
of RNG in its system as a means of reducing Greenhouse Gas (“GHG”) emissions in the State, and 
therefore the project was beneficial.4 
For the same reasons that the Commission approved Con Edison’s RNG proposal, the 
Commission should also adopt Section IV.7.8. of the Joint Proposal, which is carefully crafted so 
that the Companies will only be permitted to defer for future recovery the costs of interconnecting 
their distribution systems with biomethane facilities to the extent that those facilities are 
commercially viable and able to obtain necessary permits from the environmental regulators that 
have the legal responsibility to determine whether those facilities can be operated consistent with 
the requirements of the Climate Leadership and Community Protection Act (“CLCPA”)5 and other 
applicable laws and regulations.6

In addition, contrary to WE ACT’s claims, the Commission does not need to perform a 
detailed analysis of the upstream operations of the potential biomethane production facilities in 
order to authorize the Companies to defer the costs of interconnection facilities that will do nothing 
more than permit the Companies to ensure that the biomethane that they may purchase or receive 
in the future can be injected safely into their distribution systems.7  In this case, the Commission 
need only decide that authorizing the deferral of costs associated with the interconnection facilities 
themselves is consistent with the CLCPA and other applicable laws.  WE ACT’s claim that the 
Commission must make a full evaluation of the merits of the biomethane production facilities 
themselves is nothing more than an untenable request for the Commission to unreasonably delay 
the development of biomethane facilities through the exercise of authority that is more 
appropriately exercised by the environmental regulators charged with permitting the biomethane 
facilities.   
In addition, leaving aside the fact that adoption of WE ACT’s legal position would impose 
an unreasonable duty on the Commission to address environmental issues that other agencies are 
specifically charged with considering, the record of this proceeding supports a finding that the 
operation of the biomethane facilities that are planned by third party developers would be 
beneficial and consistent with the public interest.  As the Companies’ witness panel testified, the 
biomethane facilities that the Companies are planning to interconnect with will: 
 reduce the carbon footprint of certain wastewater treatment plants and food scrap 
and paper processing plants by purifying most of the biogas as opposed to flaring

While WE ACT bases its request for rejection of Section IV.7.8 on the fact that the Companies

II. 
The Fact That A Staff Witness Characterized Gas As A Political Issue Provides No 
Basis For The Commission To Reconsider Its Policy On The Recovery Of 
Informational Advertising Expenses 
WE ACT’s claim13 that the Commission should disallow expenses associated with the 
production of materials such as those that address the Companies’ Clean Energy Vision because a 
Staff witness asserted that “sometimes gas is a political issue”14 is without merit.  The fact that 
some individuals may view gas as a political issue neither eliminates the Companies’ need to 
advise their customers of how they plan to provide service in the future, nor calls into question the 
Commission’s longstanding policy of permitting recovery of the costs of informational advertising.  
The Companies should be permitted to continue to recover the costs of providing informational 
advertising consistent with applicable Commission precedent.15

III. 
There Is No Factual Or Legal Basis For The Commission To Impose Financial 
Penalties On The Companies For Failing To Achieve NPA Benchmarks 
At the May 20 evidentiary hearing, the Companies’ witness panel explained the provisions 
of the Joint Proposal that are intended to enhance the prospects for the Companies to achieve 
success in promoting NPAs.16  Not satisfied with these provisions, WE ACT asserts that the 
Commission should require the Companies “to meet certain NPA benchmarks or risk a financial 
penalty.”17  This proposal should be rejected.  There is no record evidence that supports the 
establishment of NPA benchmarks, addresses the issue of why a penalty structure would be lawful 
or reasonable, or sets forth what that penalty structure should be.18  More importantly, as Staff 
accurately observes in its post-hearing brief, NPAs cannot be completed absent customers and/or 
third party market buy-in and participation.19  As the Companies cannot ultimately control whether 
NPAs can be implemented, it would clearly be unreasonable to penalize them for a failure to 
complete NPAs.20

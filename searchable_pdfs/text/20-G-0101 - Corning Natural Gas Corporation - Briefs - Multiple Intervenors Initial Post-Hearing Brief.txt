I. 
INTRODUCTION  
 
 
Multiple Intervenors, an unincorporated association of approximately 60 large industrial, 
commercial, and institutional energy consumers with manufacturing and other facilities located 
throughout New York State, including the Corning Natural Gas Corporation (“CNG” or the 
“Company”) service territory, hereby submits its Initial Post-Hearing Brief in Case 20-G-0101, 
Proceeding on Motion of the Commission as to the Rates, Charges, Rules and Regulations of 
Corning Natural Gas Corporation for Gas Service. 
II. 
PROCEDURAL HISTORY 
 
 
On February 27, 2020, CNG filed a cover letter, testimony, exhibits, and proposed tariff 
leaves with the New York State Public Service Commission (“Commission”) seeking to increase 
annual gas delivery revenues and proposing a three-year rate plan.  The Company sought an 
approximately $6.3 million revenue requirement increase for the twelve months ending January 
31, 2022, and smaller increases in later years of the proposed rate plan.   
 
On June 26, 2020, the following parties filed pre-filed direct testimony and, in some cases, 
exhibits with the Commission in response to CNG’s rate filing: (a) New York State Department 
of Public Service Staff (“Staff”); (b) Bob Wyman; and (c) Multiple Intervenors.   
 
On July 24, 2020, the following parties filed pre-filed rebuttal testimony and, in some 
cases, exhibits: (a) the Company; (b) Staff; and (c) Bob Wyman. 
 
On August 4, 2020, the Company filed a notice of impending settlement discussions.  
Settlement discussions occurred thereafter, but no agreement was reached.  Accordingly, on March 
23, 2021, Administrative Law Judges Gregg Sayre and Sean Mullany conducted an evidentiary 
hearing.

III. 
ARGUMENT 
 
A. 
Certain Aspects of CNG’s Embedded Cost Of Service Study Are Inconsistent 
With Cost-Causation And Require Modification 
 
An Embedded Cost of Service Study (“ECOSS”) is a traditional study wherein revenue 
requirements are allocated among customer service classes on the basis of cost causation.  (Tr. 
879.)  An ECOSS illustrates the costs a utility incurs to serve each customer service class.  (Id.)  
Once those costs have been identified, they are classified as energy-based, demand-based or 
customer-based, and then allocated to service classes on the basis of how or why those costs are 
incurred by the utility.  (Tr. 880.)  Rates that are based on consistently applied cost causation 
principles are fair and reasonable because they further the policies of stability and economic 
efficiency, while ensuring that customers are not required to fund utility costs for which they bear 
no responsibility.  (Id.)  While other factors such as simplicity, gradualism, economic development 
and ease of administration may also be taken into consideration when determining the final 
allocation of the revenue requirement among classes, the fundamental starting point and guideline 
should be the cost of serving each service class produced by the ECOSS.  (Id.)

For the purposes of this proceeding, CNG conducted an ECOSS based on the 12-month 
historic period ended September 30, 2019.  (See Ex. 11, Schedule PMN-6.)  Specifically with 
respect to the classification and allocation of distribution mains, CNG’s expert witness, Mr. Paul 
Normand, testified that CNG conducted a minimum size system study that: (1) classified 
distribution mains 2 inches or smaller in diameter as 100% customer-related and allocated the costs 
thereof to residential and small commercial customers, and (2) classified distribution mains greater 
than 2 inches as 65.25% customer-related and 34.75% demand-related.  (See Ex. 11, Schedule 
PMN-10, p. 4.)  For distribution mains greater than 2 inches, the demand-related portion of costs 
is allocated based on class Design Day Demand factors.   
Significantly, for the customer-related component, CNG proposes to utilize a hybrid 
“adjusted customer allocation factor” to allocate such costs to the various customer service classes.  
Under this so-called adjusted customer allocation factor, CNG proposes to (1) divide the total 
consumption of larger customers not allocated any of the costs of mains 2 inches or less by the 
total number of such customers to derive a “usage-per-customer” ratio; and (2) divide the total 
throughput of the residential and small commercial classes with the usage-per-customer ratio to 
arrive at an “equivalent customer count” for those classes.   
Multiple Intervenors does not oppose CNG’s use of a minimum size system study for 
purposes of classifying distribution mains, nor does it oppose Mr. Normand’s findings that: (1) the 
costs of mains 2 inches or smaller should be assigned to residential and small commercial 
customers; and (2) the demand-related portion of distribution mains greater than 2 inches should 
be allocated based on class Design Day Demand factors.  Multiple Intervenors witness Baudino 
testified that the minimum size system study performed by CNG is, for the most part, not 
inconsistent with the methods presented in the National Association of Regulatory Utility

Commissioners Gas Distribution Rate Design Manual dated June, 1989.  (Tr. 883.)  Mr. Baudino 
also testified that CNG’s ECOSS “may be used as a rough guide to allocating class revenue….”  
(Tr. 887.) 
Notwithstanding the foregoing, the record in this proceeding demonstrates that use of an 
adjusted customer allocation factor to allocate customer-related costs of distribution mains greater 
than 2 inches is improper.  Preliminarily, introduction of an adjusted customer allocation factor is 
inconsistent with cost causation principles. (Tr. 883-884.)   Customer-related costs generally are 
“associated with the number of customers” and therefore should be “allocated based upon the 
number of customers in each class, weighted to account for the complexity of servicing the needs 
of the different classes of customers.”  (Tr. 881.)  Here, use of the adjusted customer allocation 
factor improperly attempts to combine the number of customers with the aggregation of larger 
loads for revenue allocation purposes by introducing a demand component based on total 
throughput to larger customers.  (Tr. 883.)  The result of this flawed methodology is that the 
customer counts for service classifications (“S.C.”) 1 and S.C. 3 are significantly and artificially 
reduced (i.e., to the “equivalent customer count” level) for cost allocation purposes.  (Tr. 882, 
884.)  Consequently, the S.C. 1 and 3 classes are under-allocated customer-related costs of 
distribution mains, while S.C. 6 customers are over-allocated costs.   
To illustrate, Mr. Baudino prepared an alternative ECOSS (“Alternative ECOSS”) that 
allocates the customer-related portion of distribution mains greater than 2 inches on the basis of 
distribution demand.  (Ex. 48.)  A comparison of the Alternative ECOSS to CNG’s ECOSS shows 
the drastic impact of the adjusted customer allocation factor: 38.69%  and 7.85% of customer-
related distribution mains greater than 2 inches are allocated to S.C. 1 and S.C. 3, respectively, 
with the adjusted customer allocation factor, but those figures increase to 45.97% and 9.02% under

the Alternative ECOSS.  (Tr. 886.)  On the other hand, S.C. 6 is disproportionately allocated 
24.93% of costs with the adjusted customer allocation factor, when that class would have been 
allocated 7.13% of such costs under the Alternative ECOSS.  (Id.) 
This improper allocation of costs materially impacts the affected classes’ rates of return 
(“ROR”) and, commensurately, the proper allocation thereto of any revenue increase that is 
approved in this proceeding.  For example, when the adjusted customer allocation factor is used, 
the ROR for S.C. 6 is only 4.51%, which is below the total target system ROR of 7.00%.  (Tr. 885; 
Ex. 49.)  However, under the Alternative ECOSS where the customer-related portion of costs of 
distribution mains greater than 2 inches is allocated on the basis of distribution demand, the class 
ROR for S.C. 6 jumps to 17.80% – substantially higher than the systemwide ROR.  (Tr. 885; see 
also Ex. 48.)  Under the Alternative ECOSS, the S.C. 6 ROR actually is closer to that of S.C. 3.  
(Id.)    
Importantly, use of the adjusted customer allocation factor also is a significant departure 
from established methodologies for allocating distribution main costs.  First, the adjusted customer 
allocation factor methodology differs substantially from the methodology used by CNG for the 
ECOSS in its last rate case, in which costs associated with mains greater than 2 inches were 
allocated based on Design Day demands.  (Tr. 882-883.)  Second, CNG’s proposed methodology 
is highly unusual in any circumstance – Multiple Intervenors’ expert witness Mr. Baudino testified 
that, in his nearly 40-year career in the energy industry, he has “never seen this type of adjustment 
allocator…”  (Tr. 884.)   
In his rebuttal testimony, Mr. Normand criticizes the use of a demand allocator to allocate 
the customer-related portion of distribution main costs, as shown in in the Alternative ECOSS.  
(Tr. 314.)  However, the Alternative ECOSS is presented “for illustrative purposes only to show

results thereof, it is clear that CNG’s ECOSS must specifically be adjusted to correct the artificial

B. 
The Commission Should Reject CNG’s Proposal To Allocate Across-the-
Board Percentage Revenue Increases To All Service Classes, And Should 
Adopt Multiple Intervenors’ Proposed Revenue Allocation In The Alternative 
 
Both CNG and Staff propose equal, across-the-board percentage revenue increases of 
33.5% for all service classes.  (Tr. 290, 1023.)  However, these proposals ignore the significant 
inter-class subsidies inherent in CNG’s current rates.  According to Multiple Intervenors’ analysis, 
under both CNG’s ECOSS and the witness Baudino’s Alternative ECOSS, several classes, 
including S.C. 1, are receiving substantial revenue subsidies from other classes (i.e., their class

RORs are much lower than the systemwide ROR), and current revenues for those classes would

current revenues would have to be increased by $850,921 in order for the class to achieve a 7.00%

Both Staff and CNG acknowledge that significant inter-class subsidies are inherent in 
CNG’s current rates.  For example, Mr. Normand testified that CNG’s ECOSS shows “the 
Company’s current rates produce inequities between the customer classes….” (Tr. 288-289.)  
Likewise, the Staff Rates Panel confirmed in its direct testimony that CNG’s ECOSS identifies 
inter-class revenue deficiencies and surpluses.  (Tr. 1022 (“Q.  Did Corning’s revenue allocation 
attempt to correct the revenue deficiencies or surpluses as indicated by the [CNG ECOSS]?  A. 
No….”).)   
Multiple Intervenors notes that, if all service classes have the same current rate of return, 
then equal, across-the-board revenue increases may be reasonable for CNG’s future test years.  (Tr. 
891.)  However, the evidence on the record demonstrates that this is not the case, and that certain 
classes are over-earning while other classes are significantly under-earning.  Neither Staff nor 
CNG explain or provide record evidence as to why a uniform revenue increase for all classes, 
without any attempt to correct existing inter-class subsidies, is reasonable given the circumstances, 
or comports with cost causation principles.  Accordingly, the Commission should reject both 
CNG’s and Staff’s proposals.

As an alternative, the Commission should instead adopt the revenue allocation 
methodology advanced by Multiple Intervenors, as follows: (1) CNG’s proposed per-class 
increases should be adjusted by that class’ positive or negative subsidies based on current rates 
(with the exception of S.C. 6, as explained below); (2) class increases should be limited to 1.5 
times the system average increase; (3) no class should receive a revenue decrease, given the large 
revenue requirement that CNG seeks in this proceeding; and (4) other reasonable adjustments 
should be made to class revenue increases given the first three steps.  (Tr. 889; see also Ex. 50.)   
With respect to S.C. 6, as explained in Point A, supra, the record in this proceeding 
demonstrates that the improper use of an adjusted customer allocation factor in CNG’s ECOSS 
significantly under-reports the class’ ROR relative to systemwide ROR, and therefore inflates the 
revenue increase that should be imputed on that class.  In comparison, the Alternative ECOSS 
demonstrates that the ROR for S.C. 6 is actually more consistent with that of S.C. 3.  (Tr. 885.)  
Thus, it would be reasonable and appropriate to adjust the revenue increase to S.C. 6 to equal the 
increase to S.C. 3.  (Tr. 890.; Ex. 50.)  To accomplish that while also avoiding excessive rate shock 
for other classes (i.e., S.C. 1), Multiple Intervenors proposes to reduce the S.C. 3 subsidy by 50% 
(resulting in a final revenue increase of 24.1%), and then apply that same percentage increase to 
S.C. 6.  (Id.)  Under this methodology, the rate increase to S.C. 1 still would be within a range of 
reasonableness – 1.25 times the system average increase.  (Ex. 50.) 
Multiple Intervenors’ recommended alternative revenue allocation, as presented by witness 
Baudino, is designed to move CNG’s service classes closer toward their allocated cost to serve by 
addressing subsidies inherent in current rates.3 (Tr. 891.)  Multiple Intervenors’ proposal also

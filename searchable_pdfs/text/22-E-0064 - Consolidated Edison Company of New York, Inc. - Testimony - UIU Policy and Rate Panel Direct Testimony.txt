II. 
SUMMARY OF RECOMMENDATIONS 
6 
Q. 
Would you please briefly summarize your recommendations? 
7 
A. 
Yes. Our recommendations, presented in the order in which they are discussed in our 
8 
testimony, can be briefly summarized as follows: 
9 
 
10 
A. 
 
CLCPA AND DEPRECIATION 
11 
We do not support the Company’s depreciation proposal to shorten gas service lives 
12 
by 5 years.  We recognize that the CLCPA may impact retirements, but adjustments to the 
13 
utility’s accounting need further scrutiny before determining what adjustments might be 
14 
needed to fairly balance the interests of current and future generations of customers. First, 
15 
the Company does not provide adequate data-driven information to support their proposals 
16 
and it does not tie their proposals, or their supporting arguments, to relevant planning 
17 
scenarios. Second, the Commission is actively inquiring into this issue on a statewide basis. 
18 
That inquiry will yield a wealth of information concerning future revenue requirements 
19 
and bill impacts under different scenarios. Once this data becomes available, it will be 
20 
feasible to meaningfully quantify appropriate adjustments to the depreciation rates and the 
21 
expected net salvage provisions applicable to specific accounts. 
22

8 
 
B. 
 
REVENUE ALLOCATION 
1 
While we have reservations concerning the Company’s Embedded Cost of Service 
2 
(ECOS) methodologies and its highly formulaic approach to revenue allocation, we are 
3 
largely willing to accept the Company’s proposed allocation of the revenue requirement, 
4 
with one important exception. Specifically, we recommend modifying the Company’s 
5 
revenue allocation proposal to reduce the share assigned to Service Classification (Service 
6 
Class or SC) -1 non-heating customers, to avoid exacerbating the rate disparities that exist 
7 
between large customers in this class and similar size customers in other Service Classes, 
8 
and to avoid imposing unreasonably large bill impacts on large SC-1 customers. 
9 
 
10 
C. 
 
RATE DESIGN 
11 
In the next electric rate case, in response to the CLCPA and in anticipation of 
12 
expected changes to the electric grid, we recommend the Company update and refine its 
13 
seasonal rate study to help the Commission and other interested parties to better understand 
14 
what rate design changes, if any, might be appropriate to accurately reflect evolving 
15 
seasonal peak load patterns.  An updated and improved seasonal rate study can also provide 
16 
better, more granular cost information to help evaluate what improvements can be made in 
17 
the price signals embedded in the Company’s tariffs with respect to the cost of using 
18 
alternative energy sources for winter heating. This improved seasonal study should 
19 
incorporate more granular information drawn from the wealth of data that is being collected 
20 
through the Company’s new Advanced Meter Infrastructure (AMI) meters, so that the 
21 
Commission and other interested parties can better evaluate potential improvements to the

B. 
Public Utility Depreciation 
15 
Q. 
What is the overarching purpose of depreciation? 
16 
A. 
The fundamental purpose of accounting depreciation is to achieve a matching of revenues 
17 
with costs over the life of an asset. This is similar to the way accountants try to match the 
18 
expenses incurred in production of a product with the revenue that is obtained from the sale 
19 
of that product – except over a much longer time scale. In essence, depreciation expense 
20 
allocates the initial cost of building or acquiring property over the property’s entire 
21 


--- Page 17 ---
Cases 22-E-0064 and 22-G-0065 
 
 
 
 
             DIRECT TESTIMONY OF THE 
 
   
 
 
UIU POLICY & RATE PANEL 
17 
 
economic lifespan, rather than treating it as an expense in the year in which the cost was 
1 
incurred. 
2 
While this matching principle applies to the financial statements of all types of 
3 
firms, the link between revenues and depreciation is unusually explicit and direct in the 
4 
case of public utilities, because utility rates are typically adjusted upward or downward in 
5 
response to changes in depreciation, which causes revenues to move in close alignment 
6 
with depreciation. In turn, depreciation can be periodically adjusted to take into account all 
7 
of the factors that influence the actual economic life of the utility’s investment, including 
8 
physical deterioration, technological obsolescence, and evolving market conditions. 
9 
This careful matching process is consistent with Generally Accepted Accounting 
10 
Principles, and it helps achieve the public policy goal of maintaining inter-generational 
11 
equity by helping to ensure that purchasers in one time period are not paying costs that are 
12 
primarily incurred for the benefit of an earlier or later generation of purchasers. If property 
13 
is expected to be used in providing service to customers over a life span of 80 years, it 
14 
would not be reasonable to insist on having customers pay the entire cost of the property 
15 
during the first 10 (or 20, or 30) years of that life span. Nor would it be fair to go to the 
16 
other extreme, allowing the initial generation of customers to contribute very little toward 
17 
the cost of acquiring the property, because this would force later generations of customers 
18 
to bear an unreasonably large share of the cost of purchasing and installing the property.  
19 
 
20 
Q. 
Is there more than one way to calculate depreciation? 
21 
A. 
Yes. Depreciation is normally calculated on a straight-line basis, but other options exist. 
22 
With straight-line depreciation, the initial cost of an asset (or an entire group of assets) is

1 
instance, if the property has an expected life of 20 years, a straight-line depreciation rate 
2 
of 5% would typically be used, expensing one-twentieth of the initial acquisition cost 
3 
during each year.  
4 
While straight-line depreciation is the most common approach, it is not the only 
5 
option. For tax purposes, the firm may be allowed to use accelerated depreciation, which 
6 
recognizes greater depreciation expense in early years and lower expense in later years. 
7

11 
A very different pattern is achieved when using the “annuity” approach to 
12 
depreciation. Here, the depreciation expense starts low and gradually increases. The goal 
13 
is to achieve a uniform total cost per year, considering both the depreciation expense and 
14 
the return on investment (the carrying cost of owning the property). Annuity depreciation 
15 
is somewhat analogous to the way a typical home mortgage works. During the first few 
16 
years, most of the homeowner’s monthly check goes toward the payment of interest, with 
17 
just a small portion going toward the repayment of principal. Over the years, the portion 
18 
going toward interest gradually diminishes and the portion going toward repayment of the 
19 
principal gradually increases, while the monthly payment remains constant throughout the 
20 
life of the mortgage.

2 
focuses instead on the total amount of production, or volume of service, which is expected 
3 
to be provided by the property. This approach makes the most sense where the total lifespan 
4 
is not predictable in advance, but the total volume of production is. Consider, for example, 
5 
a battery which can hold a charge for many years, provided it is never discharged, but 
6 
which physically deteriorates every time it is discharged and recharged. Another example: 
7 
equipment at a mine, where the pace of mineral extraction is going to vary widely, 
8 
depending on market conditions, and the total volume of minerals waiting for extraction is 
9 
well understood. In both cases, the production method of depreciation might achieve a 
10 
closer matching of revenues and expenses than any method of depreciation that is tied to a 
11 
specific assumed service life.

How do higher depreciation rates affect customers? 
8 
A. 
A utility’s existing customers are required to write a larger check to the utility each month 
9 
when depreciation rates are increased. As a result, the utility’s cash flows increase, which 
10 
may improve the firm’s credit metrics, making it easier to maintain or achieve a favorable 
11 
bond rating. If the firm can obtain debt financing at lower interest rates in support of its 
12 
construction program, this will eventually benefit customers. Similarly, if depreciation 
13 
rates are increased, accumulated depreciation will increase more rapidly, putting 
14 
downward pressure on the rate base, which will also eventually benefit customers.  
15 
It is important to realize, however, that the benefits from lower interest rates and a 
16 
smaller rate base tend to be spread out over many years, while the higher rates resulting 
17 
from higher depreciation expense is immediately felt by customers. Accordingly, from a 
18 
current customer’s perspective, an increase in depreciation expense is generally not 
19 
attractive, since it requires an immediate increase in the payments sent to the utility each 
20 
month, while the offsetting benefits show up much more slowly. Moreover, a significant

2 
Q. 
Can you briefly explain the revenue allocation process in a typical utility rate case? 
3 
A. 
Yes. The results of one or more ECOS studies are used to help guide decisions regarding 
4 
how proposed rate changes should be spread across the various service classifications.  The 
5 
specific process can vary, but ECOS results are generally examined to see whether certain 
6 
classes have been identified as having a high rate of return (ROR) – meaning they are 
7 
paying more than the costs that have been allocated to them (sometimes referred to as 
8 
“overearning”).  If a high ROR is identified in the ECOS study (or studies), an effort is 
9 
typically made to assign a somewhat smaller share of the rate increase or revenue 
10 
requirement to that service classification, thereby moving in the direction of greater 
11 
uniformity in the service classification RORs.  Similarly, if certain classes are identified as 
12 
having a low ROR (paying less than the costs that have been allocated to them, which is 
13 
sometimes described as “underearning”), a somewhat greater than average share of the rate 
14 
increase or revenue burden may be assigned to that class during the revenue allocation step.  
15 
Service classifications with an ROR that is close to the overall system average will 
16 
typically receive a very similar, or identical, percentage increase.33 
17 
In carrying out the revenue allocation process, tolerance bands are often used 
18 
around the system average ROR to decide whether the service classification ROR deviates 
19

Leak Prone Pipe Removal 
9. 
Leak-prone pipe is generally considered steel pipe 
that is unprotected, cast iron pipe, and some vintages of 
plastic pipe that can become brittle.  Data collected by the 
United States Department of Transportation, Office of Pipeline 
Safety, as well as our own Department, shows that corrosion is a 
leading cause of leakage and that unprotected, bare steel pipe 
is most susceptible to corrosion.  Leaks on underground piping 
create safety risks to the public and can potentially lead to 
incidents. 
10. 
Additionally, the removal of leak-prone pipe should 
drive down active leaks, lead to a decline in leakage rates on 
the distribution system, and reduce overtime and operating and 
maintenance cost. 
11. 
The Joint Proposal requires KEDNY to remove from 
service a combined 85 miles of leak-prone pipe in 2013 and 2014, 
with a minimum of 40 miles during each of those years.  This 40 
mile requirement is similar in scope to that required of KEDNY’s 
corporate relative Niagara Mohawk Power Corporation gas in the 
Joint Proposal in Case 12-G-0202.  
12. 
Based on my experience with KEDNY’s operations through 
my Staff responsibilities, and upon information and belief, 40 
miles per year, and a combined target of 85 miles over two 
years, is a reasonable and obtainable target.

Leak Management 
13. 
The Joint Proposal establishes new targets for KEDNY’s 
repairing of leaks on its system.  For 2013, the Joint Proposal 
target applies only to leaks classified as Type 1, 2 and 2A.  
For 2014 and thereafter, the performance metric takes into 
account all leaks on KEDNY’s system including Type 3 leaks.  
14. 
Potentially hazardous leaks are those classified as 
Types 1, 2A, and 2.  These are leaks that have defined repair 
timeframes under the pipeline safety regulations.  All other 
leaks not meeting the definition of a Type 1, 2A or 2 leak is 
categorized as a Type 3 leak for which there is no defined 
timeframe for its repair. 
15. 
Although Type 3 leaks are considered non-hazardous, 
any leak, including a Type 3 leak, is an escape of natural gas, 
and the pipeline safety regulations require an annual 
surveillance of the leak to ensure that it has not escalated to 
a potentially hazardous leak.  Leakage of any category costs 
ratepayers money in leaked or ‘lost’ gas.  Additionally, because 
every known leak in backlog is required to be surveyed, 
operations and maintenance expenses are incurred.  For all these 
reasons, it is important to Gas Safety Staff to hold New York’s 
gas utilities responsible for all leaks on their systems, not 
just those defined as potentially hazardous. 
16. 
Based on my experience with KEDNY’s operations through 
my Staff responsibilities, and upon information and belief, the 
targets established for KEDNY regarding leak management and 
repair are obtainable and provide corresponding benefits to the 
Company’s ratepayers.

Damage Prevention 
17. 
New York’s gas utilities perform many repairs each 
year caused by excavation damage to underground facilities.  Any 
damage to a pipeline can result in the uncontrollable release of 
natural gas and could potentially lead to a serious incident.  
In order to encourage utilities to improve damage prevention 
performance, targets for specific activities are established in 
utility rate plans that measure progress in minimizing damage to 
gas transmission and delivery systems.  Damage prevention 
performance is normalized statewide based in the level of one-
call tickets generated by excavators.   
18. 
The Public Service Commission’s regulations contained 
in 16 NYCRR Part 753 – Protection of Underground Facilities – 
require excavators to make a toll-free call to a one-call 
notification system and provide notice of their intent to 
perform excavation work.  The one-call notification system takes 
the pertinent information from the excavator and transmits it to 
its member utilities that may be affected by the excavation 
work.  Those utilities then mark the location of their 
facilities so the excavator can avoid damaging them.  A notice 
received by the utility is referred to as a One-Call ticket. 
19. 
The Joint Proposal measures the total overall damage 
caused by excavators against 1,000 one-call tickets.  KEDNY’s 
targets are 2.25 incidents per 1,000 calls for 2013, and 2.14 
incidents per 1,000 calls for 2014.   
20. 
Additionally, the company is charged with preventing 
damage on an even more stringent basis when damages are caused 
due to company mismarks of its facilities, or where the damage 
is caused by KEDNY, or company-contracted, personnel. 
21. 
Based on my experience with KEDNY’s operations through 
my Staff responsibilities, and upon information and belief, the

Emergency Response 
22. 
Emergency response measures evaluate a utility’s 
response to gas leak, odor and emergency calls generated by the 
public and non-Company personnel.  Each gas utility is required 
by the Department’s gas safety regulations to provide a monthly 
report of the total number of calls received and responded to in 
intervals of 15 minutes during normal business hours, weekdays 
outside of business hours and weekends and holidays.  These 
measures, in addition to the leak management and damage 
prevention measures, are included in the Gas Safety Performance 
Measures Report. 
23. 
The targets included in the Joint Proposal are 
statewide standards for these performance measures have been 
jointly established by Staff and utilities.  
Gas Safety Violations Metric 
24. 
One of the more important aspects of the Joint 
Proposal is the inclusion of the new Gas Safety Violations 
Metric.  The initial version of this metric was instituted in a 
recent Corning Gas rate case.  Since that time, it was expanded 
and included as part of the Niagara Mohawk gas joint proposal 
expected to be adopted by the Commission at its March 2013 
session. 
25. 
The metric came about because of Staff’s growing 
concern with the number of general violations of the 
Commission’s safety rules and regulations contained in 16 NYCRR 
Parts 255 and 261 that Staff inspectors continued to find during

The new

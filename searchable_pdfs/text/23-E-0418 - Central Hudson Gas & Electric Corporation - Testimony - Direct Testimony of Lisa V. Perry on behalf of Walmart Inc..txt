Q. 
HOW DOES THE COMPANY DETERMINE THE NATURE OF COSTS 
1 
INCURRED BY ITS CUSTOMERS AND HOW THOSE COSTS ARE 
2 
ALLOCATED 
AND 
RECOVERED 
FROM 
THE 
VARIOUS 
3 
CUSTOMER CLASSES? 
4 
As discussed by the Company's Cost of Service Panel, when preparing a COSS, 
5 
the Company first assigns costs to the portion of the Company's system that 
6 
created the relevant cost, which is generally production-related, transmission-
7 
related, or distribution-related.14 This process is call functionalization.15 Within 
8 
each function, the Company then classifies each cost as either demand-related, 
9 
energy-related, or customer-related.16 As explained by the Company, demand-
10 
related costs vary in proportion to the rate and are recovered on a $/kW basis, 
11 
while energy-related costs vary in proportion to the volume of electricity 
12 
delivered, and is expressed as a $/kWh charge.17 Lastly, the Company allocates 
13 
each functionalized and classified cost among the customer classes based on 
14 
either the class's contribution to peak demand ($/kW), contribution to the total 
15 
volume of electricity delivered ($/kWh), or contribution to number of total 
16 
customers.

Q. 
YOU MENTIONED THAT THE SECONDARY DEMAND AND 
1 
PRIMARY DEMAND RATES INCLUDE CUSTOMER, DEMAND, AND 
2 
ENERGY CHARGES. DOES THIS RATE STRUCTURE ALIGN WITH

HOW THESE COSTS ARE INCURRED? 
4 
A.  
No, they do not. As I briefly mentioned above, certain customers under the 
5 
Secondary Demand and Primary Demand rates, including Walmart, take only 
6 
delivery service from the Company, which are typically classified as demand-
7 
related costs, or costs that do not vary with the amount of electricity provided. 
8 
And, in fact, the Company states that it allocated costs functionalized as "power

11 
of these costs, in order to align recovery of those costs with how they are 
12 
incurred, delivery service charges should be recovered from customers who 
13 
take service under the Secondary Demand and Primary Demand rates through 
14 
the demand, or $/kW, charge and not through the energy, or $/kWh, charge, as 
15 
proposed by the Company.

Q. 
DOES THE RECOVERY OF DEMAND-RELATED COSTS THROUGH 
1 
ENERGY CHARGES DISADVANTAGE HIGHER LOAD FACTOR 
2 
CUSTOMERS? 
3 
A. 
Yes. The shift in demand-related costs from per kW demand charges to per kWh 
4 
energy charges results in a shift in demand cost responsibility from lower load 
5 
factor customers to higher load factor customers. This results in a misallocation 
6 
of cost responsibility as higher load factor customers overpay for the demand-
7 
related costs incurred by the company to serve them. In other words, higher load 
8 
factor customers are paying for a portion of the demand-related costs that are 
9 
incurred to serve lower load factor customers simply because of the manner in 
10 
which the Company collects those costs in rates.  
11 
Q. 
WOULD THE PROPER COLLECTION OF DEMAND-RELATED 
12 
COSTS THROUGH A DEMAND CHARGE PROVIDE THE COMPANY 
13 
BENEFITS? 
14 
A. 
Yes. By collecting more demand-related costs through the energy delivery 
15 
charge, the Company could be more susceptible to weather-related and other 
16 
fluctuations in usage than it would be if those costs were recovered through a 
17 
demand charge. A rate design that properly collects fixed costs through a $/kW 
18 
demand charge and energy-related costs through $/kWh variable charges 
19 
should provide greater revenue certainty and more stable utility earnings.

PRELIMINARY STATEMENT 
Family Energy, Inc. (“Family”), a licensed Energy Service Company (“ESCO”) 
serving residential and commercial customers in New York State, including many who 
reside in the gas distribution service territories of Brooklyn Union Gas Company and 
KeySpan Gas East Corp. (“National Grid” or the “Companies”), respectfully submits its

these proceedings to address a specific concern with the Companies’ pending rate filing—
the GGT.  Family takes no position with respect to any other disputed issues in this matter 
which do not pertain to the GGT.

As the GGT is proposed by the Companies, a residential or commercial customer 
could voluntarily enroll in a specialized service agreement whereby the Companies would 
procure renewable natural gas (“RNG”) for some or all of that customer’s gas supply.  (Tr. 
285).  Similar to Renewable Energy Credits (“RECs”) or Community Distributed 
Generation (“CDG”) subscriptions, where customers pay not for “green electrons,” but

service would not be purchasing and receiving RNG molecules, but rather, the attributes of

with the Companies that would require the participating customer to pay a flat monthly rate

of choosing between a flat rate or a percentage of monthly consumption.  (Tr. 57; 286-287).

equivalents (“FTEs”) at a cost of between $0.127 to $0.135 million per company per year to 
contract with RNG suppliers, manage the balance between purchases and sales, track 
quantities of green gas purchased and sold monthly, acquire environmental attributes, track 
costs and revenues, manage transaction system entries, and fulfill reporting requirements. 
(Tr. 142; Ex. 231). 
National Grid’s Proposal should be rejected for the following reasons, each of which 
is detailed further below:  (1) as a threshold matter, the Proposal lacks sufficient detail to 
allow the parties, ALJs, or Commission to properly evaluate its merits; (2) the Companies 
failed to meet their burden to justify the prudence of the Proposal; (3) the Proposal 
constitutes an impermissible form of “streaming” gas supply.

A. 
The Companies Failed to Sufficiently Describe Key Operational Details of the 
Proposal 
A utility bears the burden of proof to establish that proposed changes to its rates, 
rules, and regulations are just and reasonable.  16 NYCRR 61.1-4.  To do so, a utility must 
present “competent testimony” to support estimates of changes in revenues, expenses, or 
income, including details about the cost of rendering service, and other customary operating 
statistics.  Id. at 61.3.  In particular, a gas utility must establish its “gas purchasing policies” 
by competent testimony and also “insur[e] that gas costs for both the test period and rate 
year are prudent and from the least-cost reliable sources” as well as describe “gas supply 
contracts” and “costs for all sources of gas supply.”  Id at 61.3.  “Speculative or conjectural 
data are not acceptable and all estimates must be explained in detail and their bases 
definitely established.”  Id. at 61.4.  At this stage, National Grid’s Proposal is simply an 
“idea for an idea” — a shell of an undeveloped program — which lacks requisite mechanics 
and substance to properly evaluate.  For this reason alone, the GGT should be rejected. 
By way of example, a core element of the Proposal — the contract between the 
Companies and a participating customer — is conspicuously missing.  There is no evidence 
to suggest that such a contract exists in draft or final form, nor of what the key terms and 
conditions of that contract would entail.  (Tr. 316).  There is no evidence from which to 
evaluate whether and to what extent a residential or commercial customer would be subject 
to an early termination fee for canceling GGT service prior to the 12-month or 24-month 
term of the agreement.  (Tr. 316-317).  Neither is there any consideration of whether a 
residential customer would be afforded the right to rescind the agreement within three days 
of his or her enrollment, as required by the Home Energy Fair Practices Act.  National Grid 
also failed to address whether low-income customers would be eligible to enroll, and if so,

B. 
The Companies Failed to Meet their Burden to Justify the Prudence of the 
Proposal 
Not only did National Grid fail to meet its burden of providing competent testimony 
that is sufficiently detailed to describe its GGT proposal specifically, it also failed to justify 
the prudence of the Proposal as a concept generally.   
The fundamental justification for the Proposal is flawed.  The Companies claim that 
the Proposal is justified because a customer survey found that 34 percent of residential 
respondents and 20 percent of downstate commercial respondents indicated that they would 
be interested in a “green” option for natural gas supply.  (Ex. 39).  While that survey 
demonstrates consumer demand for decarbonization generally, it does not demonstrate 
demand, interest, or even awareness of RNG attributes, which are what the GGT would 
provide to participating customers.  As admitted by the Companies, the survey question 
does not further define the words “green.”  (Tr. 296).  A “green” product might include (1) a 
product that is “green” by virtue of a company procuring an equivalent amount of carbon 
offsets to decarbonize usage; (2) a product that is “green” by virtue of a company procuring 
an equivalent amount of renewable energy credits (“RECs”) to promote renewable energy 
resources; or (3) a product, such as the GGT, that assigns RNG attributes to customers.  
(Tr. 297).  In short, the Companies provided no evidence suggesting that consumers are 
aware of, let alone interested in, a specific RNG attribute option for their gas supply, which 
is what the GGT proposes to offer. 
Furthermore, the Commission has long emphasized the importance of retail market 
actors, such as Family Energy, to “spur innovation in the creation of value-added products, 
particularly energy efficiency services that regulated rates may not provide, and to create

C. 
The Proposal Would Constitute Impermissible Streaming of Gas Supply 
“Streaming” is an arrangement whereby a local distribution company (“LDC”) 
procures specific gas supplies dedicated to certain customers or markets.4  The PSC prohibits 
streaming except where non-participating customers would be worse off without the 
transactions occurring.5  This means that for an LDC to stream gas, it must be able to 
demonstrate that non-participants would be worse off economically without the streaming 
transaction.6  In other words, a streaming transaction must “generate non-participant 
benefits.”7  Furthermore, streaming arrangements that meet that criteria are only permissible 
for purposes of load retention, and are not allowed for the purpose of attracting new load or 
aggregating groups of customers.8  Streaming is intended to be a “solution of last resort,”9 
and to the extent it is permitted, the Commission requires quarterly customer-specific 
reporting of the date, duration, price, cost, and quantity or capacity for each streaming 
arrangement.

1. 
The GGT Constitutes Streaming 
In this case, the GGT would allow customers to voluntarily purchase RNG to offset 
the carbon associated with all or a portion of their gas usage.  The arrangement would be 
considered “streaming” because the Companies would be procuring specific RNG supplies 
dedicated to specific customers who participate in the program.  Similar programs to the 
GGT have been categorized as streaming arrangements.  In one instance, DPS Staff 
recommended that a ConEdison tariff which would have allowed the company to negotiate 
a separate rate with a dual-fuel sales service customer under S.C. 12 would effectively 
amount to “streaming” gas to a particular customer at a particular rate.10  As such, DPS 
Staff recommended that the Commission require that any negotiated interruptible sales 
service rate meet its standards for streaming—that the non-participants would be worse off 
without the transaction and that the company follow the Commission's reporting 
requirements.11  In another instance, Reserve Gas Co. Inc. proposed to establish a co-
generation sales service available to customers that wish to purchase gas for use in 
generation of electricity at a particular service site.12  In response, DPS Staff noted that “the 
supply of gas is intended to be from a dedicated source that Reserve would broker with a 
local producer on behalf of the customer.  This constitutes, technically, the streaming of gas 
to one customer, to the exclusion of all other customers.  It is a practice which has been 
disallowed by the Commission, except under some restricted aspects, because of the 
potential for discrimination among similarly situated customers.”13  In this case, the GGT is

2. 
The GGT Does Not Generate Non-Participant Benefits 
As a form of streaming, the GGT would be permissible only if it provided benefits to 
non-participating customers.14  In this case, the GGT does not provide benefits to non-
participating customers, let alone economic benefits.  In fact, as admitted by the Companies, 
it imposes costs on non-participating customers, who cross-subsidize the program. (Tr. 291).  
To implement the GGT, National Grid requests cost recovery for two full-time equivalents 
(“FTEs”), which will be accountable for all aspects of running the program.  (Tr. 291; Ex. 
231).  Their responsibilities include contracting with RNG suppliers, managing the balance 
between purchases and sales, tracking quantities of green gas purchased and sold monthly, 
acquiring environmental attributes, tracking green gas costs and revenues, managing 
transaction system entries, and fulfilling reporting requirements.  (Tr. 292).  Because the 
GGT is a form of streaming that imposes costs on non-participants, it should be rejected as 
an impermissible form of streaming under well-settled law established in prior Commission 
orders.  To permit the GGT would, therefore, be arbitrary and capricious.

attracting new load or aggregating groups of customers.  The Commission has previously

A. 
Longstanding Codes of Conduct Prohibit Regulated Utilities from Offering 
Competitive Supply 
Utility codes of conduct were most recently updated in the context of the 
Commission’s Reforming the Energy Vision (“REV”) initiative to reflect the principles and 
protections necessary as utilities evolve into distributed system platform (“DSP”) operators 
facilitating the creation and coordination of new markets, products, and services.16  The 
Codes of Conduct Order incorporated six guiding principles to address utility relationships 
with market actors: (1) no preferential treatment, (2) sharing of information, (3) functional 
separation of distribution planning and value-added service employees, (4) transparency, (5) 
avoiding market power conflicts, and (6) dispute resolution.  The Codes of Conduct Order 
directed the state’s investor owned utilities to file revised codes of conduct to reflect the 
requirements identified by the Commission as necessary for implementing the REV 
initiative.  National Grid’s revised codes were finalized on February 20, 2019 and approved 
by DPS Staff on March 12, 2019 (the “Companies’ Code of Conduct”).17 The Companies’ 
Code of Conduct are included as Exhibit 609.

B. 
Allowing the Companies to Offer the Proposal Through the Regulated Business 
Would Be Fundamentally Anti-Competitive and Stifle Competition 
Not only would the GGT be prohibited by the black letter of the Codes of Conduct, 
but allowing the Companies to engage in the Proposal would be fundamentally anti-
competitive and have the effect of chilling or stifling the competitive market—a market which 
the Commission is actively seeking to encourage and expand.   
As the Companies conceded on cross examination, “a competitive company could 
certainly offer a similar program” to the GGT.  (Tr. 293).  Yet, National Grid would be 
afforded an enormous advantage over a competitive supplier due to its position as a regulated 
monopoly distribution utility.  First and foremost, the Companies have the clear and explicit 
brand name advantage and customer relationships to edge out competitive companies 
offering the same or similar services.  That alone, is reason to ensure distinct separation 
between the non-competitive traditionally regulated services of a gas utility and competitive

As a threshold matter, National Grid’s Proposal fails to identify or discuss the key 
programmatic and contractual details that would be considered part of a standard 
application package if an ESCO were to seek authorization to offer the same or similar 
product.  Before an ESCO could offer a product such as the GGT, it would need to submit a 
retail access eligibility application (“RAAF”) which includes, among other things, draft 
sales agreements, quality assurance plans, sales and marketing training manuals, sample 
customer-facing forms and notices, sample bills, a description of procedures used to obtain 
customer authorization to enroll and access historic usage or credit information, samples of 
promotional or informational materials, a HEFPA compliance package, internal procedures 
to prevent unauthorized enrollments or improper charges, a list of contractors or 
subcontractors that may market the product or service, an environmental disclosure 
attestation, contact forms and consumer complaint procedures.  The ESCO would also need 
to comply with relevant capitalization criteria and financial assurance requirements, test and 
implement Electronic Data Interchange (“EDI”) protocols, enter into to operating 
agreements and billing services agreements with relevant utilities, and execute data security 
agreements and attestations.  As discussed above, National Grid’s Proposal does not include 
draft sales agreements, marketing plans or materials, consumer protection measures, or 
terms and conditions of customer contractual relationships. 
Moreover, the Commission specifically prohibits the type of value-added gas 
commodity product proposed by the Companies in the GGT.26  Value-added gas 
commodity products marketed and sold to mass market customers are limited to either (1) 
products that guarantee savings to the customer compared to the default utility rate, or (2)

products that offer a fixed rate priced at no more than five percent above the utility’s 12-
month trailing average default supply costs.  As the GGT does not meet one of those two 
exceptions, it is an impermissible product.  To the extent the Companies intend to offer the 
GGT to Assistance Program Participants (“APPs”), it would likewise need to guarantee 
savings to those customers, which it does not.27  The Companies should not be permitted to 
circumvent Commission orders simply by virtue of being a monopoly utility.  Market 
fairness demands equal treatment among providers of competitive products and services.  
The GGT is a product that should be offered only through the Companies’ unregulated 
competitive affiliates—not through a regulated gas distribution company.  But to the extent 
that the Companies are permitted to offer the GGT through their regulated entities, they 
should be required to conform to the rules and regulations imposed on ESCOs that offer the 
same or similar products and services.   
CONCLUSION 
For the reasons set forth above, the Commission should reject the proposed GGT.  
To the extent that the Companies are permitted to offer the GGT, it must be offered through 
an unregulated competitive affiliate—not the regulated gas distribution utility—consistent 
with longstanding codes of conduct and as a matter of fundamental fairness and consumer 
protection.  In the alternative, to the extent that the Commission determines that National 
Grid may offer the GGT through its regulated utility entities, it should be directed to 
conform to the Uniform Business Practices (“UBP”) and Commission orders imposing 
product and marketing restrictions on competitive supply products.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY 
NYSEG and RG&E Mass Market Managed Charging Program Proposal 
1.1   EXECUTIVE SUMMARY 
New York State Electric and Gas Corporation (“NYSEG”) and Rochester Gas and Electric 
Corporation (“RG&E”) (hereinafter “the Companies”) hereby submit this Mass Market Managed 
Charging Program Proposal (“Proposal”) for program years 2020 through 2025. The Companies 
are filing this Proposal in compliance with the New York State Public Service Commission’s 
(“Commission”) July 16, 2020 “Order Establishing Electric Vehicle Infrastructure Make-Ready 
Program and Other Programs” (“Order”1). 
Increased use and smart integration of Electric Vehicles (“EVs”) can improve asset utilization by 
increasing non-peak electricity use which has potential value to all ratepayers.  While the impact 
of EVs is not yet significant, to provide value to grid operations and to minimize the impact to 
existing capacity, integration of high levels of Distributed Energy Resources (“DER”), including 
flexible loads like EVs, at large scale will require visibility and the ability to coordinate these DER 
assets connected to the distribution system through secure communications and flexible 
interconnections. 
It is widely documented that 80% or more of residential EV charging occurs at home. However, 
less is known about the charging behavior of EV drivers when they do plug in at home. Many of 
the benefits afforded to the distribution system by EV charging are predicated on assumptions of 
off-peak charging. Conversely, at scale, simultaneous off-peak charging can have adverse effects 
on the distribution system. The Companies’ Proposal addresses these concerns through 
behavioral (passive) and non-behavioral (active) approaches to encourage off-peak charging and 
optimize charging load to maximize benefits to the distribution system.

How much energy a customer receives at home is directly related to the charging equipment 
they use, daily driving needs (mileage) and access to available charging opportunities such as 
workplace charging. A Level 1 (“L1”) charger is supplied with almost all new EVs sold in the U.S. 
These chargers connect to a typical residential 120V outlet and can deliver approximately 4 miles 
of charge per hour depending on the amperage rating of the charging plug. An L1 charger could 
meet the daily driving needs of an EV driver if they drive less than 30 miles per day but would 
require daily charging. Many EV drivers using L1 at home will supplement their charging at work, 
especially if it is offered free of charge. 
EV drivers may forgo installing Level 2 (“L2”) charging equipment at home due to the initial 
equipment cost, potential service upgrades and the costs to install a 240V outlet where they 
charge (i.e. garage). Costs for L2 equipment and installation can vary widely, but average 
approximately $600 for the L2 charger and $750 for installation of a 240V outlet. An L2 charger 
delivers more than 5 times the miles per hour charge of an L1 charger, averaging approximately 
25 miles per hour depending on the amperage rating of the charger. 
Conversely, at scale, L2 residential chargers can create unfavorable conditions for the local 
distribution system due to their greater power requirements. Typical residential L2 chargers 
have power ratings of approximately 7 kW, while a typical residential transformer is rated for 
approximately 25 kW and can serve 5 to 10 households2. Wide scale deployment of unmanaged 
residential L2 charging would generate the need for the upgrade of, or installation of additional, 
transformers and potentially feeder upgrades depending on loading conditions. Therefore, the 
Companies propose a Passive and Active Managed Charging Program to encourage and support 
off-peak charging by residential EV owners/operators. 
The Mass Market Managed Charging Program proposed recommends an approach based on a 
variety factors which the Companies believe will result in the greatest opportunity for program 
participation and success.  The Companies look forward to incorporating stakeholder feedback 
and adjusting the approach, as appropriate.

PROPOSAL DESCRIPTION 
2.1   PROPOSAL DESCRIPTION 
The Proposal is a comprehensive managed charging program for residential EV 
owners/operators that incorporates both passive and active features designed to encourage and 
optimize off-peak charging of light-duty EVs. The Proposal is voluntary for participants and 
provides incentives that scale in relation to participation as users choose to opt-in to Proposal 
features that require increasing levels of participant commitment. Proposal participation levels 
vary from basic to advanced and allow participants to commit themselves to managed charging 
activities they are comfortable with.  
At the basic level participants have a low level of commitment and provide the Companies with 
basic demographic and charging behavior information. They also agree to enroll in the 
Companies’ EV time-of-use (“TOU”) rate and receive behavior prompts to charge during off peak 
periods. At the intermediate level, participants commit to allow the Companies to receive 
charging data via a telematics device they install in their EV and/or through telematics data 
delivered through existing on-board EV telematic systems. Where telematics is not a viable 
solution, customers may also participate via a networked level 2 charger. At the intermediate 
level, participants are challenged to maximize off peak charging and may also participate in 
demand response. The advanced level will recruit participants to enroll in active managed 
charging. Participants who enroll in active managed charging will determine the level or state of 
charge required (charging power requirements) and the times their vehicle is available for 
charging (charging session duration). By defining these variables, the managed charging 
algorithm determines how much power to deliver each participant and during what periods. All 
interaction between participants and the Proposal will be automated through a web-based 
portal and/or mobile app. The portal/app allows participants to enroll in the Proposal, manage 
their participation, monitor their performance and receive feedback on their participation.

The charging data collected by the program is key. Identify technical specifications and

Data communications must work. There has been little downtime in communications for

Participants fall into a spectrum of technical ability and understanding of the software’s

Customers need reminders to start sessions as sometimes participants plug in but forget
to start a session through the website.
•
Participants desire an indicator to guarantee that their car will start charging during an
optimized (coordinated) session.
•
Participants indicated that a high level of accessibility to customer support would be a
good feature if the program becomes widely available to all NYSEG customers.
•
Certain vehicle make/models go into a sleep state if left parked, plugged-in, and idle,
resulting in the EV not accepting electricity from the charger and the EV not reaching the
desired charge. From our efforts to understand the underlying causes of this issue, it
appears that this is not caused by the OptimizEV platform but instead due to individual

Justification and Benefits:  
 
The program supports the growth of beneficial electrification and reduction in transportation-related 
emissions, while increasing equity and access to transportation electrification benefits across the 
service territory though increased electric mile rides for transit and school bus riders by providing 
access to lower cost EV charging infrastructure installations in School Bus, Municipal and Transit 
sectors, as well as private fleets, like delivery fleets.  
 
The purpose of the MHD EV MRP is to address the following customer needs and federal and state 
policy goals: 
1.    The transportation sector is New York’s largest source of greenhouse gas and NOx emissions, 
where MHD vehicles have a disproportionate share compared to other mobile sources.  While 
these vehicles only account for 4% of vehicles on the road, they account for ~25% of total 
transportation emissions, disproportionately impacting low-income communities and 
environmental justice areas located near major trucking corridors, ports and distributions 
hubs.  Addressing the needs of electrifying this part of the transportation network is 
imperative to reaching the State’s CLCPA goals. 
 
2.    In 2021, New York and other states signed on to the MHD Zero Emission Vehicle (“ZEV”) 
Memorandum of Understanding (“MOU”).  This Action Plan develops solutions towards the 
adoption of zero emission MHDs.  The Companies’ MHD EV MRP is aligned with moving 
NYS towards achieving the targets set by the MHD ZEV MOU, closely following the LD MRP 
that works to achieve targets set by the 2014 LD ZEV MOU. 
 
3.    In November 2021, the Federal Bipartisan Infrastructure Law funded the Clean School Bus 
Program, which is establishing programs towards deploying increased numbers of low and no 
emission school buses and related infrastructure.  The proposed MHD EV MRP supports 
federal policies and programs, specifically targeting public and municipal MHD fleet vehicles 
like school buses. 
 
4.   The Companies' Commercial and Industrial customers focused on goods and services delivery, 
with MHD domiciled within our service territories, have begun to set corporate goals, as well 
as acquire MHD EVs for their delivery fleets.  This program will directly support our customer 
needs for increasing charging infrastructure where vehicles reside. 
 
 
Risk of No Action:  
 
In the absence of an MHD EV Make-Ready Program less vehicles will be electrified.  The benefit of 
electrifying MHD transportation sector emissions has a two-fold impact on our low to moderate 
income customers: 1) an increased number of overall MHD EVs has the ability to greatly reduce GHG 
emissions and improve air quality in our neighborhoods with the highest diesel emissions; and 2) as a 
direct benefit to passengers and drivers from our community currently exposed to direct diesel 
emissions from the MHD vehicles in the school bus, transit bus and local municipal fleet sector.  
Neighborhoods that are already placed in high traffic throughfares have some of the poorest air quality 
primarily driven by transportation emissions.  These customers are those that are most reliant on 
public transportation with limited access to personal vehicles, and as a result, are both breathing air 
with poor air quality in the neighborhoods they live and breathing the emissions associated with being 
in close proximity to diesel .  Due to the higher upfront capital costs associated with adopting MHD

Description: 
 
The Municipal Curbside Charging Pilot is proposed as a program to assist and enable approximately 2-
4 municipal partners in planning, developing, and installing EV charging infrastructure for residents 
that are considered “garage orphans.”  One of the direct customer incentives to adopting EVs is related 
to its lower operating costs, including lower and less volatile “refueling” costs associated with 
electricity as a fuel versus petroleum-based options.  However, there is a premium for EV customers 
that charge “on-the-go” at retail and public locations.  Unfortunately, a customer class that is 
inadvertently left out of accessing charging options when electricity costs are low and off-peak are 
customers that do not have the same dedicated residential parking as a single-family home with a 
garage option.  The Companies propose a pilot to investigate the opportunities and barriers for our 
municipal customers towards the deployment of wide-scale curbside charging in dense municipalities 
and neighborhoods with higher rates of multi-family dwelling and therefore residential street parking. 
The Companies also consider these pilots as a secondary benefit that creates increased value in 
supporting EV adoption through placing visible EV charging at the curb, along public rights-of-way 
rather than behind a paywall.  There is the potential for these charging solutions to both act as visible 
advertising for EV charging, and possible economic generators for mixed-use urban neighborhoods 
close to central business districts.  The intent of this pilot is to create a program model within our 
service territory that can be replicable. 
 
The Companies will endeavor to support 100 L2 curbside charging ports by leveraging Make-Ready 
Program (“MRP”) incentives.  It is expected that make-ready costs may be as low as 25% to 50% of a 
traditional pedestal mounted charger and the savings will be made available to provide incentive 
toward other project costs, such as charger cost.  This program would broaden the qualified costs to 
include items outside the traditional MRP, including but not limited to, supporting municipalities with 
incentives towards equipment, signage and other related construction and program costs.

The Municipal Curbside Charging Pilot would assist municipalities in the Companies’ service areas to

Introduction 
This report outlines the use cases and lessons learned from four different battery energy storage projects 
recently developed by the New York State Electric and Gas Corporation (NYSEG) and Rochester Gas & 
Electric (RG&E).  The four battery storage projects discussed in this report are: 
• 
Aggregated Behind the Meter (BTM) Energy Storage Demonstration Project 
• 
Circuit deployed Battery Storage System (BSS) Pilot 
• 
Integrated Electric Vehicle (EV) Charging and BSS Demonstration Project 
• 
Peak Shaving Battery Storage Pilot 
The first project discussed is the Aggregated BTM project which is a NYSEG REV Demonstration project that 
has five batteries installed in various locations throughout the Ithaca area. Use cases and lessons learned 
discussed in this report include Customer Energy Demand Management, Aggregated Demand Response, 
Circuit and System Peak Reduction, and Inform on BTM Rate Design.   
The second project reviewed is the NYSEG Circuit Deployed Circuit deployed Battery Storage System which 
has one battery installed at the Brentwood site in Ithaca. Five use cases and lessons learned are ongoing 
under this project. Daily load leveling to Improve System Efficiency, Circuit Voltage Regulation, Circuit Peak 
Load Reduction, and NYISO Demand Response Participation, Increase Hosting Capacity and Solar PV 
Integration.  
The third project discussed is the RG&E Integrated EV Charging and BSS Demonstration project that has 
one battery installed at the Company’s Rochester service center located on Scottsville Road.  This project 
has four different use cases and lessons learned that include Building and Circuit Demand Reduction, 
Building Load Factor Improvement, Energy Arbitrage, and Infrastructure Impact and Rate Design.  
The fourth project reviewed is the RG&E Peak Shaving Battery Storage Pilot that has one battery deployed 
at the Company’s Station 127. Four use cases are under study in this project. Reduce Peak Electrical 
Demand, Improve Power Quality, Increase Loading Efficiency, and Reduce Operational and Maintenance 
Cost.

Aggregated Behind the Meter Energy Storage 
The NYSEG has developed a partnership with TESLA and installed a mixture of small (~50 kW), medium 
(~150 kW), and large (~300 kW) battery installations for a range of C&I customers within the footprint of 
the Energy Smart Community (“ESC”) located in Ithaca, NY.  The advanced grid architecture in the ESC 
footprint allows NYSEG and its market partner to maximize the benefits that these advanced technologies 
offer.  NYSEG planned on enrolling and aggregating up to six (6) customers in the battery storage offering, 
with a total capacity of approximately 1.06 MW and 4.2 MWh.  After two years, during the contract renewal 
period, five (5) customers choose to continue with the program resulting in a program offering total 
capacity of 1.01 MW and 3.74 MWh. Ideally, these customers will continue to have a mix of peak loads on 
the system with enough variation in load profiles to enable effective aggregation. Participating customers 
continue to have bill-saving benefits of the battery storage system without the upfront cost of installation 
and in return pay a nominal fee to NYSEG and its partner for these benefits. 
NYSEG is in process of understanding and observing the  performance and outcomes of each individual use 
cases.   
• 
The first use case is to validate that customer energy demand management can be performed using 
battery discharging and bill savings through demand management can be achieved for customer 
benefit.  
• 
The second use case is to verify that revenue can be generated from the NYSEG and New York 
Independent System Operator (“NYISO”) demand response markets.   
• 
The third use case will confirm whether system benefits can be gained from charging and 
discharging the battery for customer use when additional capacity is available after performing 
customer demand charge management and participating in demand response events.  
• 
The final use case is to validate whether alternative rates can be utilized to incentivize the use of 
BTM battery storage for some commercial and industrial customers. NYSEG and its market partner 
will also explore additional opportunities to provide electric system benefits and generate market 
revenue.

Current Key Lessons Learned 
• 
Informing and educating stakeholders about the battery and projects prior to needing their 
approval is a good practice.   
• 
Fire Alarm and Prevention Battery standards are still in development, which leads to design 
challenges at customer sites.   
• 
Communication and regular status meetings between all stakeholders are beneficial for updates 
and getting quick responses.  


--- Page 52 ---
Exhibit __ (ISE-8) 
Page 9 of 24 
 
• 
Improper installation of the meter meant to capture site load for the local battery controller can 
cause the battery to charge in a manner that reduces or eliminates the customer’s demand charge 
savings 
• 
Lack of proper historical data for a site can lead to sub-optimal demand charge savings  
• 
The age and condition of the customer’s infrastructure can be a serious barrier to the adoption of 
battery storage at a customer site.  In these cases, more comprehensive electrical upgrades (such 
as replacing panels, transformers, and/or cabling) are required to add a behind-the-meter battery. 
• 
Customer demand charge management requires the customer’s load to be metered in parallel with 
their utility meter.  This can pose a technical challenge particularly for primary metered customers.     
• 
Although customer communications may clearly show anticipated benefits, including financial bill 
savings, customers may not have the capacity, authority, or willingness to move forward with the 
BTM energy storage program. 
• 
Communications are a critical factor when collecting battery performance data.  Consistent data 
collection can become challenging due to communications equipment failure or lack of signal 
coverage and results in missing information that can impact performance decisions. 
• 
The additional calculation and processing time required to provide adjustments to customer bills 
to apply a subscription fee and a savings guarantee must be weighed with the expected benefit 
when designing the benefit structure of the program.    
• 
For scheduling aggregated demand-response event, notification must be sent 24 hours ahead of 
event to prepare battery for the event participation. 
• 
BTM Battery Storage customer contracts must balance customer retention and initial 
attractiveness to the host customer with the flexibility to adjust the structure and terms of the 
program business model 
• 
Clear and consistent communication of customer benefits, in this case bill savings, is critical to 
maintaining customer satisfaction with the battery storage program. 
• 
Host customers should be chosen carefully to ensure that customer benefits are significant enough 
to strongly incentivize continued customer participation 
• 
Utility BTM Battery Storage ownership models should allow for relocation of battery energy storage 
systems over the course of their useful life and replacement customers should be proactively 
identified in case an existing customer chooses to leave the program.

Circuit Deployed 
NYSEG has installed a 477kW, 1,890 kWh Distribution Circuit Deployed Battery Storage System 
interconnected to a 12.5 kV Circuit, Circuit 602 out of Cayuga Heights Substation, located within the Energy 
Smart Community (ESC) footprint in Ithaca, NY.  NYSEG plans to own and operate this BSS to demonstrate 
system benefits and participate in NY-ISO market programs.   
A BSS located on Cayuga Heights Circuit 602 was selected due to its relative low loading compared to its 
capacity rating and its location within the Ithaca ESC footprint. The ESC hosts several smart grid technology 
programs including advanced grid architecture such as telecommunications, ADMS and Advanced Metering 
Infrastructure (AMI), which will allow for the integration of the BSS into operational platforms.  Circuit 602’s 
capacity rating and loading profile also lends itself to a smaller scale BSS to be able to test and meaningfully 
demonstrate the multiple functional use cases proposed for this installation.  The capacity rating of this 
circuit is 10.3 MVA, while its highest summer peak load was approximately 4.3 MVA in 2016 (approximately 
42% loaded).  Although there is no forecasted capacity need on this circuit, it does make it ideal for the use 
cases being tested to allow NYSEG to learn without having a system constraint.    
The benefits of a distribution circuit sited BSS will be demonstrated through the following four (4) Use 
Cases:  
•

•

Daily Load Leveling to Improve System Efficiency  
Daily load leveling refers to storing energy during off-peak hours and delivering it during high demand hours 
to improve daily system efficiency.  The BSS improves system and circuit efficiency by reducing peak load 
on the supply circuit and substation. The charging and discharging of the BSS at different times and using 
different strategies can also help to level or smooth the circuit’s daily load curve which may increase system 
efficiencies, reduce losses and avoid other system level costs on a daily basis (e.g. reduce the need for 
peaking resources, lower fuel costs, lowers carbon emissions).  The BSS is intended to charge and discharge 
daily over its life to reduce peak demand in an effective and efficient manner. It can be seen in Figure 3 
below that the load has been maintained constant at the 2.5 MW level throughout the entire month of 
October 2020 except two times when battery ran out of energy while discharging. The battery is designed 
to discharge up to four hours to provide load leveling. On these two particular days, the load was higher 
for more than 4 hours continuously causing the battery to run out of energy.

NYISO Demand Response and Explore Participation in Other NYISO Markets  
The BSS can also be used to extract additional value by participating in NYISO Markets. The NYISO Reliability-
based Demand Response Programs are in place to pay customers for load reduction when the electric grid 
is stressed. The two programs are the Installed Capacity - Special Case Resource (“ICAP-SCR”) program and 
the Emergency Demand Response Program (“EDRP”).  These programs have minimum requirements of 
100kW of aggregated capacity for at least four hours when an event is called.  
The NYISO Economic-based Demand Response Programs give customers the opportunity to offer load 
reduction into New York’s electricity markets at any time, regardless of a reliability need, and these are: 
the Day Ahead Demand Response Program (DADRP) and the Demand-Side Ancillary Services Program 
(DSASP).  These programs have a minimum requirement of 1MW of aggregated capacity.  Therefore, this 
BSS is best suited to participate in one of the two NYISO Reliability based Programs.    
The EDRP Reliability Program also requires a monthly commitment for the resource to be available and 
provides a monthly payment.  Like the ICAP-SCR Program, notification for load reduction is provided on a 
“day ahead” basis and the minimum requirement is 100kW for four hours. Load reduction during an event 
with the EDRP Program is voluntary.  The payments through this program are only for energy based on the 
LBMP with a daily guarantee strike price recovery.  Since participation in this program is voluntary, penalties 
do not apply.  It is worth noting that in the last five years, there have been five (5) NYISO Reliability Demand 
Response events called for Zone C.  We have finished requirements aggregate all 5 systems to enable 
participation in Demand Response Market from Tesla in December 2021. Therefore, we are expecting to 
participate in the demand response market from 2022 onwards.  
 
Increase Hosting Capacity and Solar PV Integration (Future Use Cases)

quantity that are interconnected to the distribution system.  The location of the DER can also affect the 
interconnection requirements and hosting capacity.  For example, a large base load DER a considerable 
distance downstream on a circuit may be constrained during light load periods due to voltage regulation 
issues.  A BSS deployed on a distribution circuit can help mitigate some of these issues and increase the 
DER hosting capacity of the circuit and substation. The BSS be charged and discharged at cycles that 
increase utilization of intermittent resources.  For example, the BSS can act as a load during DER driven 
voltage regulation issues and during light load periods.

Current Key Lessons Learned 
• 
Our experience since commercial operation of identifying and working to resolve technical issues 
with the battery system continues to expand our technical knowledge of this system and has led 
to a greater appreciation for the current state of maturity of battery storage technology 
• 
Data collection on battery performance is critical and needs to be simplified as much as possible.  
During the initial data mining process, we identified an opportunity to combine data from two 
different systems. The developed solution was to install an additional system meter which will allow 
all data to be compiled in one single system simplifying data collection. 
• 
The performance and capabilities of large-scale battery systems are still being refined.  Operation 
and monitoring during the early stages of commercial operation revealed an issue with Powin’s 
batteries where load noise generated by the inverter was affecting the battery management 
system, preventing it from monitoring itself accurately causing frequent battery system 
disconnection. It was determined that the best solution to this issue is to replace the original 
batteries with Powin’s 2nd  generation batteries. 
• 
Coordination of breaker protection relays can be difficult when an inverter and battery are 
involved. Since this is newer technology and inverters are historically sensitive to issues 
encountered on adjacent circuits, especially ground faults, a detailed relay coordination study and 
modeling should be performed and reviewed prior to commercial operation. 
• 
Initial results continue to show the impact of the battery in significantly reducing the building peak 
demand and improve building load factor. Proper sizing of the battery upfront and adjustment to 
charge/discharge settings of the battery (by analyzing historical building load data) has been the 
key to achieve this performance. Effective peak demand reduction can reduce the monthly bills of 
the building.  
• 
Forecasting and predicting fleet charger utilization can be challenging and is influenced by multiple 
factors still being established.  Predicting EV charger utilization is closely tied with predicting duty 
cycles for vehicles that would use the chargers. In a fleet scenario, vehicle duty cycle and average 
vehicles miles travelled is generally predictable, making for a predictable pattern for vehicle 
charging at the site. Where vehicle duty cycles are less predictable, coupled with vehicles, like 
PHEVs that have an option to operate on electric or gasoline/diesel miles, EV chargers at a site are 
therefore less predictable. The biggest challenge for this project related to charger utilization to 
date has been driven by the work from home policy that was implemented at the start of the 
pandemic and is still partially in place. Currently, the EVs in RG&E’s fleet are pool vehicles to be 
utilized by employees who are primarily office based. Since office-based employees have been 
working from home there has been very little utilization of pool vehicles and therefore very little 
utilization of the EV chargers have been seen. 
Peak Shaving 
RG&E has installed a 2 MW, 10 MWh battery storage system interconnected to a 12.5 kV bus at RG&E’s 
Station 127 34.5/12.5 kV Substation in Farmington, NY as a demonstration project.  RG&E plans to own and 
operate this BSS to demonstrate the benefits of battery storage.

Station 127 serves approximately 5,000 customers and is forecasted to exceed its distribution load-serving 
capacity by 0.2 MW in the year 2020 and is expected to increase by approximately 2% every year thereafter. 
Station 127 is physically adjacent to a 115 kV/35 kV sub-transmission substation.   As a result of this 
proximity, the distribution substation has been subject to occasional power quality issues mainly associated 
with transmission switching operations and transmission level fault events being reflected into the 
distribution system. There are also many commercial customers connected to Station 127 which have 
equipment that can be susceptible to various power quality instances which may impact their operations 
from a reliability standpoint.  
The BSS located at Station 127 offers the ability for RG&E distribution operations to utilize an alternative 
solution to mitigate an expected capacity deficiency, manage the system reliability through low load 
periods, improve power quality and demonstrate the value of additional battery technology capabilities. 
RG&E operates the BSS to levelize the load, through the discharge of the battery during high-load periods 
and charging of the battery during low-load conditions.   
The benefits of a substation-sited BSS will be demonstrated through the following five (5) Use Cases:  
• Reduce peak electrical demand   
• Improve power quality   
• Increase transformer loading efficiency    
• Reduce operations and maintenance costs  
• Maintain a constant power factor

2.916 MVA above the PLBN rating by 2026.

Improve Power Quality (PQ) 
Customers observe various power quality instances which may impact their operations from a reliability 
standpoint.  Energy storage systems can be used to provide customer electric loads with ride-through 
capability in poor PQ environments. Storage systems can be used to protect sensitive production 
equipment from shutdowns caused by voltage sags or momentary interruptions. When a power quality 
event such as rapid change in voltage is detected on the substation, a site controller can receive commands 
to engage in power quality function modes, i.e. Dynamic Reactive Current Support Mode.  Based on the 
pre-defined dynamic reactive current support settings, battery status, and pre-defined operating priorities, 
the Battery Storage System Control (BSSC) issues a command to the Battery Management System (BMS) to 
deliver or absorb real power as necessary to mitigate the voltage event.  RG&E has tested how voltage 
support can be achieved by a BSS and how this function impacts the power quality of large customers 
served by the station.  Demonstration of the battery system to accommodate various operating schemes 
is intended to provide learning and experience that allows the development of future projects with similar 
objectives and is shown below. The graph below in Figure 10 shows that the battery discharged to maintain

Increase Transformer Loading Efficiency   
Various battery charging and discharging strategies can help to level or smooth the daily load curve which 
may increase system efficiencies, reduce losses and avoid other system level costs on a daily basis (e.g. 
reduce the need for peaking resources, lower fuel costs, and lowers carbon emissions).  Achieving a 
constant, leveled load profile can improve grid efficiency and RG&E is interested in learning how a BSS can 
operationally accomplish these goals.  As a secondary objective after reducing peak demand experienced 
by the substation transformer, RG&E is testing the ability of a BSS to shape the load by discharging at peak 
times and charging during low load periods.  Future BSS test and analysis will investigate and compare the 
losses to the savings that can be achieved from distribution and other loss reduction.  
The maximum peak typically occurs during 10:00 am – 2:00 pm, and the minimum load typically occurs 
during 1:00 – 4:00 am.  Therefore, a scheduled charge period will be implemented to meet the peaks 
observed and expected in this substation in the afternoon.  Based on the pre-defined schedule, battery 
status, and pre-defined operating priorities, the BSSC issues a time-stamped command to the BMS to begin 
charging the battery during minimum load period. When the pre-defined charging period has ended, the 
BSSC sends a signal to BMS, which commands the BSS to end charging.  When the measured load moves 
outside of a defined operating range, the BSSC issues a command to the BMS and subsequently commands 
BSS to discharge, until the measurement from the power quality meter indicates that the load has returned 
to the specified operating range. The entire system is automatic and hence provides the optimal charging 
discharging based on algorithm provided by Tesla named Opticaster. A demonstration of automatic 
charging and discharging of the battery on July 5 and July 6, 2019 from 11:00 am to 11:00 pm is shown 
below in Figure 11. The battery charged from midnight to early morning during minimum load hours and 
discharged between peak hours from 10:00 am – 2:00 pm. The Blue line indicates charging status.  Anything 
below the blue line means the battery is charging and above zero means the battery is discharging.

Description: 
 
RG&E is partnering with RED Rochester, LLC (hereafter “RED”) to blend hydrogen into RED’s power 
and heat generation units that run on natural gas. The goal of the project is to reduce RED’s 
greenhouse gas (“GHG”) emissions and evaluate the ability of the existing natural gas fired 
technologies to blend and combust hydrogen in a safe and efficient manner.  
 
This project will be divided into four phases: phases 1, 2, and 3 are isolated testing used to determine 
appropriate hydrogen blending concentrations in RED facilities.  Phases 1 and 2 consider blending into 
the two types of existing RED boilers, while Phase 3 would test hydrogen blending to RED’s future on-
site gas turbine.  Phase 1 is currently scheduled for implementation in Q2 2023, with phase 2 following 
in Q4 2023.  Phase 3 can be designed independent from the other three phases, as the high-pressure 
turbine will require a different hydrogen blending apparatus; the turbine is expected to be installed at 
the RED site in 2024, with Phase 3 testing following shortly thereafter.  As mentioned previously, the 
first three phases are considered testing phases, and depending upon the results of these phases, 
appropriate blending percentage will be determined that would result in proper sizing of the on-site 
hydrogen electrolyzer for permanent blending.  Finally, Phase 4 of the project will include on-site 
hydrogen production for permanent hydrogen blending to RED facilities.  While it is subject to the 
result of phases 1 through 3, at this point, based on the initial investigation of RED existing equipment, 
the Company anticipates a 20% blend of hydrogen could be feasible with minor upgrade of the existing 
equipment.  To provide on-site hydrogen production for the 20% blending target of phase 4, a 20 MW 
electrolyzer would be required.

Justification and Benefits:  
 
Alignment with New York Climate Action: The pilot project aligns with New York’s Climate 
Leadership and Community Protection Act and will provide a guiding model for other urban regions 
in the Companies’ territories, informing how green hydrogen can be scaled within other New York 
municipalities to reduce carbon emissions of industrial customers that are hard to electrify. 
 
Decarbonization of the natural gas infrastructure: A 20% blend of hydrogen into end-use thermal 
units at RED could potentially reduce the natural gas consumption by as much as 20% (depending 
upon the minimum heating value that need to be conserved), which would be translated into 360mcfh 
based on the 1800mcfh average usage, which translates into an annual CO2 reduction of over 170,000 
tons.  
 
Reducing peak consumption of natural gas: Blending of hydrogen into RED end-use equipment will 
displace conventional natural gas in the Company’s gas network.  Hydrogen blending can reduce the 
strain on gas systems during peak-day demand in an area which has historical congestion issues 
during the peak consumption hours/days. 
 
Creating in-house capabilities: Very few utilities have the in-house expertise to develop distribution-
scale hydrogen blending.  The opportunity for the Company gas engineers and operators to engage 
with the testing and training of this pilot project will equip the Company personnel with the expertise 
to develop future hydrogen projects with in-house resources.  
 
Key Data and lessons learned: Understanding the full scope of system retrofits required to make the 
natural gas distribution system and end-use applications hydrogen-ready, accurate calculation of the 
emissions impacts (including carbon, nitrogen and sulfur oxides) of hydrogen blending, the economic 
impact of making a natural gas system hydrogen-ready, as well as the acceptable hydrogen 
concentration in existing facilities and blending impacts on carbon emissions, will enable Gas Network 
leaders to make informed decisions about the future of hydrogen blending.  
 
Contributing to industry-wide learning: This project will add valuable qualitative and quantitative 
information to a growing body of research on hydrogen blending across New York State.  Few utility-
scale hydrogen blending projects have been pursued in the US to date, and the opportunity to pursue a 
project at an operational utility-scale heat and power unit will provide invaluable information for the

Description: 
The Demand Side LMI HVAC Management Pilot project will partner with NYSERDA and their award 
recipient Hub Controls to pilot a HVAC management device ("HubController") implementation in the 
LMI community to enable and evaluate energy and cost savings, carbon reduction, smart control, and 
energy usage insights.  The HubController’s innovative Automatic Energy Reduction ("AER") 
algorithms make it the only heating & cooling controls device proven to deliver residential energy use 
reduction without the need for consumer intervention.  Using tailored APIs, the HubController will 
share data and provide a level of control to the Companies to enable identification of beneficial energy 
efficiency programs for the customers.  The device collects 97,000 energy use data points per home 
per year and can tell if the home is poorly insulated, could use a heating system upgrade, or if the 
temperature is changed inefficiently. The AER algorithms, data gathering, and energy management 
capabilities are all wrapped up in a high consumer demand device i.e., a smart thermostat.  
Justification and Benefits: 
This pilot supports the City of Ithaca’s Green New Deal and Thermal Load Electrification Program 
goals. The goals for the City of Ithaca’s Green New Deal include:  
•
Achieve carbon neutrality community-wide by 2030.
•
Meet the electricity needs of City government operations with 100% renewable energy by 2025.
•
Reduce emissions from City vehicle fleet by 50% by 2025.
•
Ensure the benefits are shared among all local communities to reduce historical social and
economic inequities.
•
Facilitate a comprehensive public engagement process.

Description: 
The FICS Scalability Plan builds on the Companies’ REV demonstration pilot and seeks to further the 
principles investigated in that pilot.  The FICS demonstration project tests a new model for 
interconnecting Distributed Energy Resources ("DERs") to the distribution grid using Active Network 
Management ("ANM") rather than firm capacity.  ANM technology allows the utility to manage DER 
within grid constraints (e.g., voltage, overloads, etc.) using real-time sensing and controls, avoiding 
more expensive upgrades.  This model provides the potential to save on interconnection costs with 
minimal curtailment on DER, aiding economic viability.  In addition, ANM provides the potential for 
greater penetration of DER.  The FICS Scalability Plan will provide licensing for a Flexible

Justification and Benefits: 
As DER penetration increases on different parts of the distribution grid, the cost of integrating the 
marginal MW into the grid increases until eventually the cost of the marginal MW is greater than the 
revenue that can be generated by the MW. When this point is hit, additional DER often cannot 
interconnect to the grid because it is not economically feasible.  A way to continue to operate the grid 
safely while allowing more DER capacity to interconnect is needed to continue to enable DER growth 
in New York State. 
Risk of No Action: 
As DER penetration increases, more of the system will become bottlenecked by expensive transmission 
and distribution system upgrades.

DERMS project.

Justification and Benefits: 
EPRI is the Electric Power Research Institute and is a non-profit organization that performs research to 
advance environmentally responsible, safe, and reliable electricity for the benefit of the public.  EPRI is 
an independent research organization that performs objective, scientific research leading to progress in 
reliability, efficiency, affordability, health, safety, and the environment.  EPRI performs independent 
scientific based research to benefit the public.  The Companies’ membership dollars that go towards 
research are leveraged with the pool of funds by other EPRI member companies.  Participation in EPRI 
provides the Companies with access to technical expertise, training resources and industry collective 
experiences along with connecting members to a global network of peers and the Companies would be 
able to leverage this research and associated learnings to inform future capabilities and requirements 
to support high levels of DER integration including beneficial transportation electrification.

use of energy storage by providing tools, methods and best practices that support members and 
stakeholders to assess and design energy storage-fueled DG, and microgrid projects that are safer, 
more reliable, lower cost and with environmental and societal benefits.   
EPRI’s DER Integration program set provides members, among other items, support to analyze DER 
integration issues, predict, identify and address grid issues caused by increasing levels of DER

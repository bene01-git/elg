III. 
NEGATIVE REVENUE ADJUSTMENTS 
A. 
The Commission Has the Authority to Adopt Performance-based 
Ratemaking with Potential NRAs in a Litigated, One-Year Rate Order. 
1.   
The Commission’s authority to employ performance-based 
ratemaking with potential NRAs in this case is supported by both 
caselaw and past Commission orders. 
As a general matter, Central Hudson’s contention throughout its Initial Brief, that 
the Commission lacks the authority to employ performance-based ratemaking with the potential 
for negative revenue adjustments (NRAs) in this litigated proceeding, is directly contradicted by 
past Commission orders and practice.  In its 2007 rate proceeding, National Fuel Gas 
Distribution Corporation (NFG) equated potential NRAs to “penalties” and then argued that the 
Public Service Law (PSL) “contains no express provisions granting the Commission authority to 
assess penalties for service and safety standard violations.”13  The Commission found NFG’s 
position “untenable and unacceptable.”14  It held instead that the use of NRAs, even in the 
context of a one-year rate order, “is both practical and a proper use of [its] authority to ensure 
that safe and reliable service is maintained by the utility company.”15  As Central Hudson 
acknowledges, there is no case law to the contrary.16  Indeed, the Commission expressly 
reaffirmed itself when NFG made the same argument in its 2017 rate case.17 
Moreover, these two rulings are consistent with the Commission’s practice going 
back more than a decade.  The Commission has repeatedly employed performance-based 
ratemaking, with potential NRAs, in litigated one-year rate plans.  It did so when it perceived a

Any NRAs the Company may incur as a result of Staff’s 
recommended performance-based ratemaking would not be penalties 
within the meaning of PSL §25. 
The Company’s argument that potential NRAs constitute unauthorized penalties 
is meritless.  First, Staff notes that this argument has been raised and rejected by the Commission 
before: 
We have approved incentive programs in the context of accepting 
multi-year rate plans as “just and reasonable.” We can index 
portions of the Company's return for the rate year in this case to 
achievement of key measures of safety and service quality. Contrary 
to the Company's claims, linkage of rates of return to fully 
achievable levels of acceptable performance is not a “penalty,” but 
a lawful exercise of our authority to set “just and reasonable” rates.22 
Moreover, the courts have already concluded that the Legislature's adoption of 
detailed penalty provisions for violations of the Public Service Law, Commission Orders, and 
certain Commission regulations does not transform a Commission rate adjustment into a 
penalty.23  
In New York Telephone Company v. Public Service Commission, the 
Commission set rates based on a reduction in forecast maintenance expenditures for outside 
plant.24   It found that the maintenance expense had increased due to an unreasonable failure to 
replace outside plant.  During the ensuing litigation, New York Telephone argued that this 
adjustment was an unauthorized service penalty.  The Appellate Division, however, disagreed, 
notwithstanding that “the Commission did partially rely on service problems experienced by 
petitioner's subscribers in making its adjustment.”25  Similarly, the Commission may rely on the 
service and safety needs of Central Hudson’s customers in developing incentives without 
converting those incentives into a penalty. 
Moreover, Staff’s recommended potential NRAs are, on their face, distinct from 
the collection of a penalty.  Under PSL §25, the Commission commences a court action for

Staff’s Recommended Rate Plan is Not Confiscatory. 
Central Hudson’s claims that performance-based ratemaking as recommended by 
Staff in this case would result in a confiscatory rate order is similarly meritless.  It is well settled

Staff also disagrees with the Company’s proposed targets for the EV Adoption

earned, not something that is owed to the Company.  EAMs should motivate the Company with 
rewards only if they can achieve certain outputs that support state policy.  While EAMs should 
be a stretch target, they are positive only, and therefore not a risk to the Company for failure to 
meet those targets.  It follows that the Company should not be rewarded when it continues with 
business-as-usual performance. 
For these reasons, and the reasons set forth in its Initial Brief, Staff recommends 
that the Commission adopt its testimonial position concerning the EV Adoption EAMs.

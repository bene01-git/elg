PRELIMINARY STATEMENT 
New York State has taken a firm and clear stand that addressing human-induced climate 
change is vital and of the highest priority. The passage of the Climate Leadership and 
Community Protection Act (“CLCPA”) solidifies the path begun under the Reforming the 
Energy Vision (“REV”) framework and the State Energy Plan and continued in the State’s Green 
New Deal. The course is clear: New York must cut its carbon emissions on an extremely 
aggressive timeline—a 40% reduction from 1990 levels must occur in slightly less than six years 
and an 85% reduction must be achieved in roughly twenty-six years.1 Cutting emissions will 
require an unwavering commitment to abandoning the use of fossil fuels and decarbonizing the 
State’s economy as quickly as possible.   
The Public Service Commission’s (“PSC” or the “Commission”) role in ensuring that the 
targets in the CLCPA are met cannot be understated. As the regulator of the State’s energy 
utilities, the Commission must oversee the transformation of New York’s energy sector to meet 
our climate mandates. As is made crystal clear by the Scoping Plan, created by the State’s

LEGAL AND POLICY FRAMEWORK  
APPLICABLE TO THE JOINT PROPOSAL 
In evaluating a joint settlement proposal, the Commission must determine whether “the 
terms of the proposal, viewed as a whole, produce a result that is in the public interest.”7 The 
Commission must also evaluate and determine whether the settlement proposal will provide safe 
and adequate service at just and reasonable rates as required by the Public Service Law8 and 
complies with the CLCPA.: (1) complies with Sections 7(2) and 7(3) of the Climate Leadership 
and Community Protection Act (“CLCPA;”)9 ; (2) will provide safe and adequate service at just 
and reasonable rates10; (3) and is consistent with sound environmental, social and economic 
policies of the Public Service Commission, New York City, and New York State; (4) the 
settlement strikes a balance between “protection of the ratepayers,” “fairness to investors,” and 
“the long term viability of the utility;”11 and (5) the settlement will have results that are “within 
the range of reasonable results that would likely have arisen from a Commission decision in a 
litigated proceeding.”12 If one of these elements is not satisfied, the Commission should not 
approve the settlement. In addition, the Commission must take into account the fact that 
“settlement reflects the agreement by normally adversarial parties,”13 and give weight to the fact 
that a settlement is contested.14

I. 
THE CLIMATE LEADERSHIP AND COMMUNITY PROTECTION ACT 
PROVIDES NEW REQUIREMENTS FOR RATE ORDERS. 
New York passed the CLCPA out of a recognition that climate change is adversely 
affecting New York and a statewide reduction in greenhouse gas emissions will reduce those 
adverse effects.15 To this end, the CLCPA requires cutting economy-wide state greenhouse gas 
emissions to 60% of 1990 levels by 2030 and 15% of 1990 levels by 2050,16 mandates that a 
minimum of 70% of statewide electric generation be served by renewable energy by 2030, and 
all statewide electrical demand be served by zero-emission generation by 2040.17 Under CLCPA 
Section 7(2), every state agency, when making any “administrative approval[] or decision[],” 
must consider whether the approval or decision is inconsistent with or would interfere with 
attainment of the CLCPA’s statewide greenhouse gas reduction requirements, and if so, the 
approval or decision cannot go forward without a justification.18 The statute also calls for the 
adoption of measures to promote beneficial electrification in transportation and reduce energy 
consumption in residential and commercial spaces.19 These aggressive binding mandates for 
greenhouse gas emissions and the legislative push for beneficial electrification all demand a 
dramatic shift away from the business-as-usual operations of the utilities in this state.  
In addition to the CLCPA’s emissions mandates, the statute also includes provisions 
specifically addressing the disproportionate climate and environmental burdens that DACs 
experience. The CLCPA broadly defines DACs as “communities that bear burdens of negative 
public health effects, environmental pollution, impacts of climate change, and possess certain 
socioeconomic criteria, or comprise high-concentrations of low- and moderate- income

With regards to DACs, the Commission is obliged to consider burdens on DACs 
individually and as a whole,32 including environmental, social, and economic burdens.33 Before 
the Legislature passed the CLCPA, the Commission already had a legal duty to provide safe and

legally obligated to modify the term such that the disproportionate burden is eliminated.36 The 
Commission must also identify how the order will prioritize emissions reductions in DACs.37

modifying or rejecting any terms that do not comply with the law.

II. 
THE PUBLIC SERVICE LAW ALLOWS FOR THE CONSIDERATION OF 
ENVIRONMENTAL POLICY IN ADDITION TO SAFE AND RELIABLE 
SERVICE AT JUST AND REASONABLE RATES. 
The Public Service Law requires that the Joint Proposal will provide safe and adequate 
service at just and reasonable rates.38 Also, as stated above, in evaluating a Joint Proposal, the 
Commission must determine whether “the terms of the proposal, viewed as a whole, produce a 
result that is in the public interest.”39 In evaluating whether a settlement proposal is in the public 
interest, the Commission must consider several factors, including whether: (1) the settlement 
proposals are consistent with sound environmental, social, and economic policies of the Agency 
and the State; (2) the settlement strikes a balance between “protection of the ratepayers,” 
“fairness to investors,” and “the long term viability of the utility;” and (3) the settlement results 
are “within the range of reasonable results that would likely have arisen from a Commission 
decision in a litigated proceeding.”40 If any one of these elements is not satisfied, the 
Commission should not approve the settlement. In addition, the Commission must take into 
account the fact that “settlement reflects the agreement by normally adverse parties,”41 and give 
weight to the fact that a settlement is contested.42

New York’s future—who wish to see the building stock electrified and who support the Scoping 
Plan—are compelled to finance National Grid’s advocacy on energy and environmental policies. 
The Commission must take steps to protect New Yorkers’ First Amendment rights and ensure 
that utilities are not exploiting them for their own political gain.

CONCLUSION 
The Legislature’s establishment of aggressive mandates for greenhouse gas emissions 
reductions for New York State and lawmakers’ corresponding push for electrification require the 
Commission to embark on a dramatic shift away from the business-as-usual regulation of the 
operations of utilities. In light of all of the reasons outlined in this Statement of Opposition, the 
Joint Proposal, as a whole as well as in its specific terms, is at odds with both the CLCPA and 
the Public Service Law because the agreement permits the Company to use ratepayer dollars to 
invest in the Leak Prone Pipe Removal program and pay a premium for biomethane projects that 
are unsafe, unhealthy, unreliable, and further extend the State’s reliance on fossil fuel. A Joint 
Proposal that complies with shifts in New York State law would instead focus on the obvious 
need to reduce the demand for and use of gas and transition to electrification. Additionally, the 
Joint Proposal allows the Companies to continue their unlawful practice of charging ratepayers 
for activities that push their own political agenda.  
The Joint Proposal also fails to abide by legislators’ instructions to agencies to rationally 
and equitably distribute the costs of the economy-wide transition away from fossil fuels. The 
Joint Proposal locks in immense costs for fossil fuel infrastructure and increases the customer 
charge in a manner that will inevitably lead to an increase in energy insecurity for customers in 
National Grid’s service territory. Finally, despite the steep increases in residential rates, the Joint 
Proposal provides minimal protections and programs to address disproportionate impacts in 
disadvantaged communities. For the foregoing reasons, and because the Joint Proposal is 
inconsistent with the Scoping Plan, with Governor Kathy Hochul’s expressly stated climate and

INTRODUCTION 
While rate cases are complicated and technical matters, the battery storage issues in the 
instant matter are simple. Central Hudson Gas & Electric (“Central Hudson” or the “Company”) 
seeks to apply distribution rates to bulk storage projects interconnected to transmission facilities 
in its service territory and providing a service to the New York Independent System Operator 
(“NYISO”) market.  The proposed distribution rates make bulk storage uneconomic and impede 
state policy goals to deploy battery storage. This problem can be simply rectified by requiring 
Central Hudson to treat what it calls “transmission” facilities as such, applying federal policy by 
analogy.  This simple action eliminates perverse outcomes with respect to batteries in New York 
State. 
The Company’s proposal means that every single interconnection in its service territory 
will be subject to distribution rates1 – whether by way of the state-jurisdictional rates for which it 
seeks approval in the instant docket or by way of a Wholesale Distribution Service (“WDS”) 
tariff rate it intends to file with the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (“FERC”).  Either 
way, the Company asserts that its rates, based on a distribution-system regulatory construct and 
analysis, should apply to all batteries, including those interconnected at 69 kV, 115 kV and 345 
kV.  This is plainly unjust and unreasonable, discriminatory, and contrary to state policy. 
Central Hudson’s SC 13 rate applies to charging energy used by bulk storage based on 
the tariff before the Commission in this docket.2  Tr. p. 1687, ln. 4-19. Bulk storage facilities are 
not retail customers, and they are not end users of bulk charging energy; rather, that energy is 
stored within the system and later discharged back to the bulk power system. Application of

interconnections that are 69 kV or larger.3  This policy would exempt bulk storage from paying 
retail distribution rates, or derivations thereof, when providing a service to NYISO, consistent 
with the rate treatment that Order 841 accords federally-jurisdictional transmission-
interconnected bulk storage.

II. 
Central Hudson’s rates are contrary to state policy.

applicability to bulk storage are harmful to the State of New York and Central Hudson’s

3757, ln. 11-18.

III. 
Central Hudson’s rates are contrary to state law 
Central Hudson’s application of distribution rates to bulk storage further fails to consider 
impacts on its existing ratepayers or state law with respect to rate setting. As a result, Central 
Hudson’s treatment of bulk storage is unjust and unreasonable and is discriminatory against 
Central Hudson’s ratepayers, who will cover the costs of battery storage incentive programs on 
their electric bills but will not see the benefits of those programs in their utility jurisdiction.  
Rates must be just and reasonable. NY Public Serv. § 4; General Motors Corp. v. Public 
Service Com'n of State of N.Y. (3 Dept. 1983) 95 A.D.2d 876, 463 N.Y.S.2d 886, appeal denied 
60 N.Y.2d 557, 469 N.Y.S.2d 1025, 457 N.E.2d 808.  In this case, and as set forth in KCE’s 
direct testimony, Central Hudson’s proposed SC 13 rate does not meet this standard. The 
application of a rate designed to apply to large commercial and industrial customers to battery

CONCLUSION 
The Commission should direct Central Hudson to apply the policies underpinning Order 
841 to all 69 kV, 115 kV, and 345 kV facilities – facilities it has already termed “transmission.”  
Hearing Exhibit 429.  In other words, the Commission should require Central Hudson to develop 
a rate for bulk storage facilities.9  That rate should exempt bulk storage from paying retail 
distribution rates, or derivations thereof, when providing a service to NYISO, consistent with the 
rate treatment that Order 841 accords federally-jurisdictional transmission-interconnected bulk 
storage.

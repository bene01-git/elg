INTERRUPTIBLE RATES 
11 
 
12 
Q. What considerations must be taken into account in setting rates for 
13 
interruptible customers? 
14 
A. All the same considerations that apply to firm customer rates apply to 
15 
interruptible rates.  Interruptible rates must be just and reasonable, and the 
16 
Company must provide safe and adequate service.  The impact of changes in 
17 
the terms, conditions and rates applied to interruptible must be reasonable, and 
18 
adverse impacts must be mitigated to the extent feasible.  The interests of 
19 
interruptible customers must be considered, just as the interests of any other 
20 
customer or class of customer must be considered.

1 
Q. How has the Commission interpreted just and reasonable? 
2 
A. Historically, rates must be justified by the cost of providing service.  
3 
Regardless of the cost of service, extreme rate changes are judged 
4 
unreasonable.  The interests of all customers, including interruptible 
5 
customers, must be balanced and all Commission actions must be consistent 
6 
with the public interest.   
7 
 
8 
Q.  What are the public interest considerations here? 
9 
A. The interests of interruptible customers are equally as much part of the 
10 
public interest as the interests of firm customers.  Interruptible rates impact 
11 
the cost of providing many essential services, including medical and 
12 
educational services and other municipal services provided at taxpayer 
13 
expense.  To the extent interruptible service replaces fuel oil use in boilers, it 
14 
contributes to improved air quality.  Interruptible service utilizes unused 
15 
capacity on the transmission and distribution systems, and optimizes the value 
16 
of those facilities to the benefit of all ratepayers. All these considerations are 
17 
well known and beyond dispute.

18

5 
A. These more stringent requirements require significant capital expenditures

interruption periods.  Those cost increases directly reduce the value of 
8 
interruptible services.

Q. Why does the limit equal to firm service rates no longer adequately 
11 
protect the interests of interruptible customers? 
12 
A. In the past, it was expected that the same short term price volatility in the 
13 
fuel oil and natural gas markets that led the Commission to allow very low 
14 
interruptible rates would create occasional opportunities for utilities to charge 
15 
excessive delivery rates.  Limiting interruptible rates to the otherwise 
16 
applicable firm service rate was thought to be an adequate protection from that 
17 
sort of short-term pricing.  But what was intended as a consumer protection 
18 
has become the de facto justification for excessive rates.  In current 
19 
circumstances, KEDNY is able to negotiate interruptible contracts at 
20 
essentially the full firm rate.  Customer subject to that rate must maintain 
21

10
dual-fuel and interruption capability, without any benefit of lower rates or 
1 
guarantee of continuous service.   
2 
  
3 
Q. Absent a robust and competitive market for alternate boiler fuels, 
4 
what are the real alternatives to the use of interruptible fuel? 
5 
A. Many customers may choose to convert to firm gas service.  Those 
6 
customers will suffer stranded investment in dual-fuel equipments.  They may 
7 
also experience losses due to liquidation of interruptible supply contracts.  But 
8 
for other customers, there would be no alternative to paying those higher 
9 
prices.  The real alternatives for those customers who cannot convert are 
10 
deferred maintenance, reduced capital expenditures and/or curtailed tenant 
11 
services.  These are in fact the same unduly burdensome alternatives firm 
12 
customers face. 
13 
 
14 
Q.  Are the otherwise applicable firm rates an appropriate limit for 
15 
interruptible gas rates? 
16 
A. No.  Interruptible service is intrinsically less valuable than firm service 
17 
because of the added costs of maintaining interruption capability. Interruptible 
18 
rates must be lower than firm rates to encourage customers to take on the 
19 
added burden of dual-fuel capability, and to avoid stranded investment by 
20 
customers in dual-fuel facilities.

Q. Have you reviewed the gas delivery rates applicable to electric

21 
Q. How does DG compete with large scale generators?   
22

12
A. To the customer, the value of the DG is determined largely by the net 
1 
savings available by reducing electricity purchases, or by the net revenue 
2 
available by selling excess generation.  Those net savings or net revenues are 
3 
the difference between the revenue from the sale of electrical energy and the 
4 
cost of the fuel used to produce that energy.   If DGs experience higher fuel 
5 
costs, they will consequently receive less net revenue.  
6 
 
7 
Q. If DGs do not generally sell into the NYISO wholesale market, why is 
8 
it important that their gas delivery rates be the same as for wholesale 
9 
generators? 
10 
A. First, NYISO is now considering tariff revisions which will allow direct 
11 
participation in the market by at least some DGs, and other DGs can 
12 
participate in the current Special Case Resource program as well.  DG 
13 
customers may also sell energy under SC11 Buyback Service, which is priced 
14 
with reference to the NYISO day-ahead locational-based marginal price 
15 
(LBMP).  But more importantly, one goal of REV is to allow the most 
16 
efficient generation to be dispatched at all times.  While the industry has not 
17 
yet begun to dispatch large numbers of DGs, tariff rules must be written in a 
18 
way that ensures that the entire system, including DGs, is operated as 
19 
efficiently as control protocols allow. In the case of DGs, this may require 
20 
tariff rates that approximate expected market prices, rather full price 
21 


--- Page 13 ---
Testimony of John J. Dowling, P.E. 
 
 
13
formation by auction, as the NYISO administers for the wholesale market.  
1 
Whether DGs participate directly in wholesale electricity markets or operate 
2 
based on the expected value of those markets, they are disadvantaged by 
3 
higher gas delivery prices.  That is anti-competitive and unduly 
4 
discriminatory. 
5 
 
6 
Q. How does NYISO determine which generators will run in any hour? 
7 
A. The NYISO system dispatches generators based on marginal cost, 
8 
principally the variable fuel cost. All else equal, a generator that suffers higher 
9 
gas costs will be dispatched only after less costly generators are dispatched. 
10 
This is designed to insure that the least cost generation mix is dispatched at all 
11 
times.  If generators all purchase fuel at the same price the least cost dispatch 
12 
is also the most thermally efficient one.  Thus, the NYISO market is not only 
13 
economically efficient, it minimizes the environmental harm caused by fuel 
14 
use. But if some generators suffer higher gas costs that are not based on the 
15 
marginal cost of providing gas service, those generators may be forced out of 
16 
the dispatch by less efficient, but still cheaper generators.  This is 
17 
economically inefficient and environmentally irresponsible in the short run, 
18 
and will prevent the optimal development of DG in the long run.

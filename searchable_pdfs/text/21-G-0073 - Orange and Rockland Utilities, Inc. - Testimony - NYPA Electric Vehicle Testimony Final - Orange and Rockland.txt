18

19 
the operating cost of using electricity as a transportation “fuel” is complex due to utility 
20 
rate structures with demand and volumetric charges that often have optionalities, such as 
21 
time-of-use or opt-in standby rates, and can vary based on actual usage. In contrast,

Q. 
What is managed charging? 
4 
A. 
Managed charging often is described as the intelligent charging of EVs, where charging 
5 
can be shifted based on grid loads and in accordance with the vehicle owner’s needs. It 
6 
can refer to utility-managed charging or user-managed charging.  
7 
Under utility-managed charging, the utility would control when an EV charges, 
8 
subject to certain restrictions. The EV owner would specify the daily time(s) by which a 
9 
full charge is needed.  
10 
Under user-managed charging, the EV owner would be incentivized to charge at 
11 
times that the utility wants the vehicle to charge, such as off-peak periods. Customers 
12 
would receive an incentive for enrolling in the program and charging during off-peak 
13 
periods. There also may be penalties or higher rates for charging during peak periods. 
14 
Q. 
Why should the Company include a managed charging option for vehicle fleets in its 
15 
service territory? 
16 
A. 
Managed charging enables the utility to use its real-time insight into when curtailment is 
17 
needed to signal the charging station to slow down or stop charging a particular vehicle. 
18 
This arrangement is mutually beneficial for the customer and the utility. It provides the 
19 
vehicle operator with confidence that the vehicle will be charged when needed for use. It 
20 
benefits utilities by increasing their flexibility to manage grid load, thus shaving peak 
21 
demand and avoiding costly infrastructure upgrades, all of which benefit ratepayers. 
22

Furthermore, because electric fleet vehicles generally will require much more 
1 
electricity to charge individual vehicles due to the size of their batteries, high-speed and 
2 
high-power charging will be needed at the charging depots. This is particularly true for 
3 
the vehicles in a public transit agency’s fleet, which consist primarily of large buses. 
4 
When several of these chargers are used simultaneously, it will introduce a substantial 
5 
incremental electric demand to the charging depot. Charging fleet vehicle batteries during 
6 
the hours with the highest electric demand could impose constraints on the existing 
7 
electric distribution infrastructure. Conversely, charging fleet vehicles during low-
8 
demand hours can provide grid benefits by increasing the capacity utilization of 
9 
distribution and generation assets. Off-peak charging also may benefit the transit agency 
10 
by limiting impacts to dispatch scheduling by removing buses from service at times of 
11 
lower ridership. The costs and potential benefits arising from this incremental load make 
12 
behavioral strategy incentives, such as a managed charging option, effective tools to 
13 
promote low-demand period charging.

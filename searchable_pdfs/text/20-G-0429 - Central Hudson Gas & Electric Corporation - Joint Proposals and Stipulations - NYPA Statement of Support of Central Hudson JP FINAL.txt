INTRODUCTION AND DESCRIPTION OF NYPA’S INTEREST 
 
The New York Power Authority (“NYPA”) hereby files this statement in support of the 
Joint Proposal (“JP”) filed with the New York Public Service Commission (“Commission”) on 
August 24, 2021. NYPA is a signatory to the JP with respect to the electric service issues set 
forth therein by Central Hudson Gas & Electric Corporation (the “Company”) in Case 20-E-
0428.1 
 
NYPA is a corporate municipal instrumentality and a political subdivision of New York 
State (“the State”), authorized under the New York Public Authorities Law (“NY PAL”) to, inter 
alia, finance, develop and implement energy-related projects, programs and services for public 
entities in New York State.2  Consistent with the NY PAL, NYPA assists municipalities 
throughout New York in reducing their energy consumption from their street light facilities.  
Specifically, through the Smart Street Lighting NY Program created by the Governor in 2018, 
NYPA assists cities and towns in purchasing their street light facilities and retrofitting them with 
energy efficient Light Emitting Diode (“LED”) technology, dimming nodes, and other smart 
solutions to maximize energy efficiency and bill savings, including the adoption of “Smart City” 
devices that can be added to street lights.  Consistent with its statute, as well as New York State’s 
energy efficiency and greenhouse gas reduction goals set forth in the 2019 Climate Leadership 
and Community Protection Act (“CLCPA”),3 NYPA’s clean energy initiatives span the State, 
and include the installation, commissioning and financing of projects that promote energy

PRELIMINARY STATEMENT  
 
NYPA supports the electric service provisions of the JP as a fair and reasonable result 
consistent with the public interest.  As the introductory section illustrates, NYPA focuses on

lighting conversions resulting in efficiency gains for municipalities, smart city devices that can 
provide necessary services to those municipalities, and initiatives that promote the deployment of

ratepayers, revenue requirements and bill impacts, rate of return on equity, revenue allocation 
and rate design, electric reliability and resiliency and the myriad other provisions affecting the 
electric business of the Company and its customers.

A.  Street Light Issues 
 
 
1.  Adoption of a Street Light Dimming Pilot 
 
NYPA’s testimony urged the Company to adopt a dimming tariff.  NYPA explained that 
the network lighting control (“NLC”) nodes that are installed as part of a municipality’s LED 
conversion are updated photo sensors that activate the dimming of street lights, and also act as 
high-quality meters that can measure the savings from dimming.7  Furthermore, NYPA testified 
that street light dimming strategies could reduce energy usage by up to an additional 25 percent 
as compared to pre-LED energy usage levels.8  NYPA further noted that because NLC nodes are 
not yet PSC-approved metering equipment, the Company should adopt unmetered billing based 
on a customer’s pre-approved operating schedule for dimming.9   
The Company did not embrace NYPA’s dimming tariff proposal,10  but the JP reached a 
compromise by establishing the Streetlight Dimming Energy Efficiency Pilot (“Pilot”).11  Under 
the Pilot, up to 3 municipalities will be recruited by NYPA to participate, provided they satisfy 
the requirement to have a minimum of 30 dimmable LED street lights.  The energy consumption 
data of the LED street lights recorded by the NLC nodes over a one year period will be reported 
to the Company, and will be used to calculate the dimming savings, as compared to a baseline 
charges using pre-set operating hours for each month. Participant municipalities will receive an

2.  Smart City Technologies Working Group 
NYPA’s testimony advocated for a standardized and streamlined process for installation 
of smart street lighting technology or smart city devices.  These devices, in addition to the NLC 
nodes that perform the street light dimming function discussed above, can enable numerous other 
public benefits such as such as air quality monitoring, weather detection, traffic monitoring and 
digital connectivity.  Such functions are viewed by municipalities as desirable benefits of 
converting their street lights to LEDs.  Thus, knowing how these smart city devices can be 
installed can encourage LED adoption.  The reverse is also true: if smart city devices do not have 
a clear path to being installed, this can inhibit municipalities from moving forward with their 
LED conversion plans.12  While it is possible to retrofit smart city technologies after LEDs have 
been installed, it is far less expensive to install them at the same time as the LED conversion 
occurs.13  NYPA noted that the Company did not have a process for adopting such devices onto 
their poles, instead referring only to its existing forms of agreement that relate to attaching 
fixtures for the telecom or cable industry participants,14 but which are unsuited for smart city 
technologies.  Finally, NYPA advocated that the Company adopt unmetered billing for these 
devices, as they all have a low and predictable power draw, and can be billed based on their 
nameplate ratings.15

CONCLUSION 
In sum, the JP balances utility and ratepayer interests and is consistent with sound 
environmental, social and economic policy, in accordance with the Commission’s settlement 
guidelines.  For these reasons, and the other reasons set forth in detail in this statement, the 
Commission should find that the JP is in the public interest and adopt it in full and without 
modification.

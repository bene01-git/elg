STAFF INITIAL BRIEF 
I. 
INTRODUCTION 
 
 
In a ruling at the evidentiary hearing that was held on May 20, 2024, 
Administrative Law Judges (ALJs) Maureen Leary and James Costello authorized parties to file 
post-hearing briefs regarding the specific issues raised at the hearing.  In this Post-Hearing Brief, 
Staff will address: (1) the role of Biomethane Interconnections within the Climate Leadership 
and Community Protection Act (CLCPA) and the cost recovery protections in the Joint Proposal; 
(2) the Joint Proposal’s vast improvements to the Companies’ Non-Pipeline Alternatives (NPAs) 
framework; and (3) how leak survey technology provides safety and reliability benefits for both 
the Companies and customers. 
 
 
The provisions of the Joint Proposal pertaining to Biomethane projects are 
reasonable because they provide the Companies with the flexibility to pursue these projects, 
which can contribute to the emission reduction goals of the CLCPA, while also protecting 
customers from the financial risks associated with these projects.  The Joint Proposal includes 
numerous enhancements to the Companies NPA framework to help increase the likelihood that 
the Companies will be able to develop NPAs.  Further, the Joint Proposal’s leak survey 
requirements will help to increase public safety and reduce emissions. 
 
 
Accordingly, Staff continues to support the Joint Proposal as a balanced 
resolution to the issues in these cases; the terms of which provide meaningful solutions and

III. 
ARGUMENT 
A. Biomethane Interconnections Will Lead To Emission Reductions 
At the evidentiary hearing, We Act cross examined the Companies witnesses 
concerning their proposed biomethane interconnection projects.  More specifically, We Act 
questioned whether the Companies track the carbon dioxide that is emitted from the thermal 
oxidizer, whether the flaring conducted at the biomethane sites emits carbon dioxide, and about 
the risk of methane leaks from the interconnection pipes.8   
The Joint Proposal contains reporting requirements pertaining to Biomethane 
interconnections to provide increased transparency for these projects.  The Joint Proposal 
requires certain reporting prior to when construction starts to allow for an assessment of costs, 
benefits, materials, and an accounting of upstream Greenhouse Gas (GHG) emissions avoided by 
the biomethane the Companies’ will procure as a result of the interconnection.  Further the Joint 
Proposal allows the Companies to defer the revenue requirement impacts associated with the 
capital costs, which are capped at $13.195 million for KEDNY and $9.868 million for KEDLI, 
for new interconnections with biomethane production facilities.  Should these projects not come 
to fruition, customers are insulated from paying for these interconnection projects.  The Joint 
Proposal also states: 
To the extent that the Companies purchase biomethane from interconnected 
biomethane facilities, the prices paid for such supplies should be consistent with 
the market price of natural gas supplies purchased at similar locations and 
consistent with the Companies’ existing gas supply portfolio.  The prices paid

B. The Joint Proposal Includes Vastly Improved NPA Processes  
 
 
In the hearing, ALJ Leary asked, “is every NPA customer driven… or are there 
NPAs that you can actually implement… that are not dependent on… a customer choosing [to 
participate in the NPA], but it would be Company driven?”13  Simply put, there is no such thing 
as project which would be recognized as an NPA project as discussed in the Gas Planning Order 
absent customer and/or third-party market buy-in and participation.14  NPA projects require 
coordination between the utility, customers, and third-parties.  
Work is underway to improve the NPA development process.  Processes for 
developing NPA projects are presently under development statewide in Case 20-G-0131, the Gas 
Planning Proceeding.15 In the meantime, the Joint Proposal includes numerous enhancements to 
the existing NPA process and expands the scope of traditional capital projects that can be 
avoided through successful implementation of NPA projects.16  Specifically, the Joint Proposal 
continues the NPA-related mechanisms first established in the 2021 Joint Proposal,17 and 
includes eight meaningful improvements to the Companies’ NPA project implementation 
process, including prioritization of NPA projects within Disadvantaged Communities; a new, 
more rigorous process for developing NPA opportunities with the highest likelihood of success; 
new or expanded NPA use-cases that the Companies must pursue; strengthened customer 
outreach and education efforts through use of implementation contractors and lessons learned 
from experience with other NPA projects and other utilities; and both improved forward-looking 
implementation planning and backward-looking reporting requirements to ensure that the NPA 
development process is improving as quickly as possible.18

C. 
Leak Survey Technologies Increase Safety and Reduce Emissions 
 
 
At the evidentiary hearing, ALJ Leary examined Staff about the Enhanced High 
Emitter Methane Detection Program.19  Leak detection technology plays a crucial role in 
maintaining the safety of the gas system, which the Commission’s rules acknowledge.  The 
Commission’s Pipeline Safety Regulations require gas corporations to leak survey their gas 
systems and to classify leaks found based on the hazard they present to life and property.20  The 
prescribed leak classification system grades leaks based on a likeliness of the gas emission to 
accumulate into a combustible gas-air mixture.  A Type 1 leak representing the greatest potential 
of accumulation into the explosive range, thus endangering life and property.  In contrast, a Type 
3 leak represents a leak that is very unlikely to be able to accumulate into the combustible range.  
The leak classification system does not grade leaks in term of volumetric release of methane 
primarily because the tools or devices used to detect methane only do so by measuring 
concentrations of methane, in parts per million or percent of gas-in-air or percent of lower 
explosive limit, at a given sampling point. 
 
 
In order to quantify the rate of the release, at a minimum, the size of the orifice, or 
hole in the pipe, and the pressure on both sides of the orifice must be known.  The rate of release 
cannot be determined using values provided by traditional leakage survey and detection 
technologies since those technologies only quantify the volume of methane present at a single 
point in space and time.  Therefore, advanced leak detection technologies have been in 
development to attempt to quantify the rate of release.  To do this, advanced leak detection 
technologies use gas detection technology to collect data about a gas plume or gas migration 
pattern to be able to derive or calculate the rate of release.   
 
 
The Companies proposed, in their direct testimony,21 an Advanced Leak 
Detection Program.  This program was modified into the Supplemental Leak Surveys described

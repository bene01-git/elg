Q. 
Would you provide a few brief introductory comments concerning ECOS studies? 
3 
A. 
An ECOS study is often used as a tool in utility rate proceedings to apportion rate 
4 
base and operating expenses among the various customer classes.  This data is 
5 
used in conjunction with existing revenues to estimate the overall system ROR and 
6 
the ROR earned from each service classification as defined in the study.  
7 
Sometimes, the focus is on historical revenues; other times forecasted revenue 
8 
increases for the Rate Year (pro-forma) are used to estimate the overall system 
9 
ROR.  And, sometimes the RORs from both the pro-forma and historical ECOS 
10 
studies are considered.  Regardless of the specific approach that is used, the 
11 
analyst must exercise discretion and judgment in apportioning costs.  Some of 
12 
these judgments have been the subject of much debate and controversy 
13 
throughout many decades. For example, the National Association of Regulatory 
14 
Utility Commissioners Gas Distribution Rate Design Manual, published in June 
15 
1989 (the NARUC Gas Manual) discusses the general judgements that can be 
16 
used in an ECOS model. That manual states on its page twenty that “[t]here is no 
17 
scientifically correct method of making necessary allocations. A certain amount of 
18 
judgement must be used in any cost of service study. Consequently, cost allocation 
19 
studies should only be utilized as a general guide or as a starting point for rate 
20 
design.”  
21 
The end result of an ECOS study is to estimate the overall system ROR and 
22 
individual service classification RORs.  The ECOS study results are particularly

1 
A. 
BACKGROUND 
2 
Q. 
Can you briefly explain the revenue allocation process in a typical utility rate case? 
3 
A. 
Yes.  The results of one or more ECOS study guides decisions regarding how 
4 
proposed rate changes should be allocated across various service classifications.  
5 
The specific process can vary, but ECOS results are generally examined to see 
6 
whether certain classes have been identified as having a high ROR (paying more 
7 
than the costs they have been allocated – sometimes referred to as “overearning”).  
8 
If a high ROR is identified, an effort is typically made to assign a somewhat smaller 
9 
share of the rate increase or revenue requirement to that service classification, 
10 
thereby moving in the direction of greater uniformity in the service classification 
11 
RORs.  Similarly, if certain classes are identified as having a low ROR (paying less 
12 
than the costs that have been allocated to them, which is sometimes described as 
13 
“underearning”), a somewhat greater than average share of the rate increase or 
14 
revenue burden may be assigned to that class during the revenue allocation step. 
15 
In carrying out the revenue allocation process, tolerance bands are often 
16 
used around the system average ROR to decide whether the service classification 
17 
ROR deviates far enough from the average ROR to merit special treatment.  The 
18 
width of the tolerance band (e.g., +-10%, 20%, 30%, etc.) can vary widely, but the 
19 
effect is generally the same – to ameliorate disputes concerning the ECOS results, 
20 
because service classifications that fall within the tolerance band generally receive 
21 
the same percentage change.  
22

1

2

4 
Finally, some restrictions are typically imposed on how much any service 
5

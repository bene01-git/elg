I. 
Introduction 
 
The Public Utility Law Project of NY, Inc. (“PULP”),1 in accordance with the provisions 
of §22 of the Public Service Law and §3.7 of the Public Service Commission’s (“PSC” or 
“Commission”) Rules and Regulations,2 seeks rehearing of the December 16, 2016 Order3 in this 
proceeding. The Commission’s rules state that a “[r]ehearing may be sought only on the grounds 
that the Commission committed an error of law or fact, or that new circumstances warrant a 
different determination.”4 Additionally, a petition for rehearing must separately identify and 
specifically explain and support each alleged error of fact or law, or new circumstance, cited as 
the reason(s) necessitating a rehearing.5 PULP argues below that certain of the Order’s findings 
are not supported by the facts in evidence, are contrary to law, and that new circumstances have 
arisen during the pendency of the rate case that should have been introduced into the underlying 
proceeding before the Commission issued its December 16 Order. PULP urges the Commission 
to rehear these issues and modify its December 16 Order as discussed below.  
Specifically, the Order lacks any statutory or public policy basis for its decisions that:  (1) 
the Brooklyn Union Gas Company d/b/a National Grid NY (“KEDNY”) and KeySpan Gas East 
Corporation d/b/a National Grid (“KEDLI”) (collectively, the “Companies”) should now be 
permitted to recover the bulk of their Site Investigation and Remediation (“SIR”) costs through 
base rates6; (2) contravene the market-incentive concept, which the Commission insists is one of 
the foundation concepts of its Reforming the Energy Vision (“REV”), shareholders should be 
insulated from sharing in future SIR costs; and (3) the new rates established in its December 16

Commission Rationale Founded on Errors of Fact

MGP site clean-up and KEDNY and KEDLI have been complying with their obligations to

authority in this regard.17 Second, the Companies are allowed to seek a change to a time frame

power to grant.18  Therefore, it is not accurate for the Commission to correlate the Companies 
compliance with DEC obligations as evidence that an incentive wouldn’t provoke the Companies 
to accelerate the timing of clean-up of a particular site provided by a DEC consent order. For 
example, since the DEC order of consents allow for multiple requests for modifications to the

The “used and useful” test would be more protective in such cases as this one, since the 
manufactured gas plant sites (MGPs) that provide much of the toxic contamination underlying 
the cost of cleanup of the Gowanus and Newtown sites have been little more than a burden to the 
Company and its predecessors in interest for a significant period, and have neither been used nor 
useful for existing ratepayers. Thus, the company could not have included them in the rate base, 
and would have paid 100% of the cleanup costs. 
 
Under the “prudent investment” standard, which the Commission applied in this case, the 
Company may receive a return upon monies “prudently invested” into its capital plant.  The 
Commission did not define in its December 16 Order what “prudent investment” might mean, 
however the late Justice Scalia defined “prudent investment” as "capital reasonably expended to 
meet the utility's legal obligation to assure adequate service.”29 Under such a definition, the 
Gowanus and Newtown sites would have qualified for inclusion in the rate base of utilities 
earlier in the chain of legal responsibility than the Company, but it is difficult at best to see how 
purchase of those sites was monies prudently invested into the capital plant to provide/assure 
adequate service.

ELECTRIC FORECAST METHODOLOGY 
1 
Q. 
What methodology did the Company use to develop 
2 
its electric delivery volume forecast? 
3 
A.  According to pages 7-8 of the EFP’s initial 
4 
testimony, the Company bases its forecasts of 
5 
delivery volumes for all service 
6 
classifications, or SCs, except SCs 5, 6, and 
7 
13, on econometric models.  An econometric model 
8 
consists of two parts.  The first part is a 
9 
regression model that relates the delivery 
10 
volume to a set of independent variables.  These 
11 
independent variables can include the number of 
12 
customers, price of electricity, personal income 
13 
or employment, weather variables, and a proxy to 
14 
account for the effects of the COVID-19 
15 
pandemic.  These independent variables normally 
16 
cannot explain all variations in delivery volume 
17 
because not all influencing variables are known, 
18 
quantified, or available with measurable data.  
19 
In addition, a time series also possess 
20 
characteristics of non-stationarity or serial 
21 
correlation, which may result in large 
22 
forecasting errors.  Therefore, the second part 
23 
of the econometric model is introduced to 
24 


--- Page 10 ---
Cases 22-E-0064 & 22-G-0065 
    Staff Forecasting Panel 
9 
 
capture the remaining variations left by the 
1 
included independent variables, as well as to 
2 
address these characteristics that may produce 
3 
forecasting errors.  This part may consist of 
4 
one or more autoregressive integrated moving 
5 
average, or ARIMA, terms, depending on data 
6 
patterns, and is statistically determined.  The 
7 
combined model is referred to as an ARIMAX 
8 
model, where the “X” represents the independent 
9 
variables included in the model. 
10

A. 
Yes. Con Edison developed an ARIMAX model for 
13

ELECTRIC POST-MODEL ADJUSTMENTS 
8 
Q. 
Please discuss your post-model adjustments to 
9 
your electric volume forecast. 
10 
A. 
Energy efficiency, photovoltaic, or PV, solar 
11 
generation, and electric vehicles, or EVs, 
12 
collectively referred to as distributed energy 
13 
resources, or DERs, have impacted the actual 
14 
volume data we used to develop our electric 
15 
volume forecast.  Therefore, the impact of 
16 
historical actual DER savings has already been 
17 
reflected in the model used to forecast delivery 
18 
volume.  However, the incremental impact of DERs 
19 
may not be fully captured in the forecast to the 
20 
extent that future DER activity might grow 
21 
beyond the historical rate.  This potential 
22 
discrepancy is analyzed by comparing the 
23

I oppose the Joint Proposal (JP) ﬁled on 24 August 2021 in these proceedings and urge the Ad-
ministrative Law Judges (ALJs) to recommend to the Public Service Commission (PSC) that it
reject that JP because:
• The JP fails to offer rates that are just, reasonable, and non-discriminatory,

The Geothermal Rate Impact Credit
It is a well established tenet of proper rate design that rates charged to consumers should reﬂect
the costs caused by those consumers’ use of the electric system. It is also well established, and
non-controversial, that those consumers who rely on geothermal heat pumps for heating and
cooling impose lower costs on the electric grid than do consumers who rely on more traditional
heating and cooling solutions. Geothermal heat pump users also impose lower costs than are
imposed by those consumers who rely on air-coupled heat pumps for both heating and cooling.
In recognition of the lower costs required to serve geothermal heat pump users, the previous JP
and resulting Commission order required that the Company provide a Geothermal Rate Impact
Credit (GRIC) to offset some portion of the excessive charges imposed on those consumers by
the existing rate structure. While the amount provided was signiﬁcantly less than the “reverse
cost shift” calculated by NYSERDA and conﬁrmed by others, the GRIC was considered an ac-
ceptable temporary mitigation of the acknowledged rate inequity. The expressed intent was to
terminate the GRIC upon the Company’s offering a new rate which would more equitably allo-
cate costs. However, the Company has taken no steps to offer such a new, more equitable rate
and now proposes not only to reduce the GRIC but also to end it – whether or not a new, more
equitable rate is provided.
The Commission is charged with ensuring that rates are just,
reasonable and non-
discriminatory. The inequitable rates charged to geothermal heat pump users fail to meet any
of these requirements. The reduction, and eventual elimination of the GRIC, will move the of-
fered rates even further from those which are authorized by law.
The source of the inequity is the simple fact that geothermal heat pump users are included
in the same rate classes as others who do not use geothermal heat pumps. This is the case
even though the average load proﬁles of geothermal users are dramatically different from most
consumers in the same rate class. Thus, the failure to distinguish geothermal heat pump users
from other users violates generally accepted tenets of rate class design which require that rate
classes be composed of members who are generally similar in the costs that they impose on the
grid.
Because geothermal heat pumps are electrically powered and more efﬁcient than traditional
systems, buildings with geothermal heat pumps will generally present much lower peak de-
mand loads during the cooling season and increased electrical consumption during the shoul-
der and heating seasons. These differences have at least two signiﬁcant impacts on the cost-
causation of geothermal heat pump users:
• Less expensive distribution capacity is required to serve geothermal systems during the
periods of system-wide demand peaks which drive the costs of utility infrastructure.
• Increased electricity consumption by geothermal heat pumps during off-peak periods al-
lows the costs of ﬁxed assets to be distributed across a larger number of units of either
consumption or demand.

The aggregate effect of these differences is reﬂected in calculations of Rate Impact Metrics
which have often shown that the introduction of geothermal heat pumps tends to reduce the
rates that are paid by all consumers, including those who do not use geothermal heat pumps.
(i.e. The RIM value will be greater than 1.)
The rates are unjust. A just rate is one that allocates to individual ratepayers obligations to
pay which reﬂect the cost of providing service to those ratepayers. However, the existing rates,
even with the GRIC at its current, unreduced level, requires that geothermal heat pump users
pay more than their fair and just share of the costs of providing service. Geothermal heat pump
users are forced to subsidize the electric bills of those who have yet to adopt sustainable, clean
heating and cooling technologies. Thus, geothermal heat pump users are forced to carry the
burden of subsidizing costs which would more equitably be allocated to users of fossil fuel sys-
tems.
Additionally, the Company’s plan to reduce the GRIC and to eliminate it, whether or not new
rates are provided that eliminate the inequity addressed by the GRIC, is unfair to consumers
who responded to the urging of NYSERDA and others by adopting geothermal heat pumps with
the expectation that the rate inequity was being addressed by the GRIC and would continue
to be addressed in the future through the introduction of new rates. Such a “bait-and-switch”
should not be tolerated by the Commission. Utility regulation is incompetent and unjust if it
results in such signiﬁcant variability of consumer costs.
The rates are unreasonable. The GRIC only addressed one part of the rate inequity. Whether or
not the GRIC is provided, as long as the inequity exists to any degree, the operating costs of elec-
trically powered heat pumps will remain higher than they should be and thus consumers will
be somewhat discouraged from adopting this technology. But,the removal of the GRIC with-
out providing another method to reduce the inequity means that ratepayers, when evaluating
options for HVAC system replacement, are more certain to ﬁnd that the burden of subsidizing
the costs of others raises the operating costs of geothermal heat pumps to a level that makes
such systems unreasonably costly. When an inequitable rate distorts the market to such a de-
gree that otherwise sensible and wise decisions become unreasonable, even though they would
conform to the urging of state agencies such as NYSERDA, we must recognize that the rates are
themselves unreasonable.
At a time when it is essential that we progress as rapidly as possible in order to achieve the goals
established by the CLCPA, it makes little sense to continue to impose rates that distort the mar-
ket by increasing the operating costs of sustainable, clean technologies such as geothermal heat
pumps. Similarly, such rate design also tends to work against the express purpose of the Com-
mission’s Energy Efﬁciency Orders that have allocated hundreds of millions of dollars to utility
programs encouraging the installation of heat pumps. By artiﬁcially and arbitrarily increasing
operating costs above what they should be, inequitable rates will force many consumers who
would have otherwise gladly accepted up-front incentives, to refuse adoption of geothermal
heat pumps. Thus, these rates thwart the Commission’s intent and tend to make achieving the
CLCPA goals less likely. Consumers are wise enough to recognize that the beneﬁt of reduced
up-front costs can be quickly offset by increased operating costs and thus will tend to reject
up-front incentives unless lower, more equitable rates are provided.

Even as unjustly high rates for geothermal heat pump use tends to discourage the adoption
of such systems, the subsidization of others by those inequitable rates tends to encourage the
continued use of inefﬁcient and emitting systems which rely on air-coupled cooling and fossil
fuels. In as much as these inequitable rates make it more economic, and thus more reasonable,
for consumers to resist expending the capital to adopt geothermal heat pumps, the current rates
have an unreasonable distortionary effect on the market and consumer behavior.
The rates are discriminatory. By forcing one class of user to pay more than their fair and just
share of the costs of providing electric service, these rates discriminate against that class. If
such discrimination were purely accidental, if that class that was discriminated against couldn’t
be readily identiﬁed, one might tolerate that discrimination. But, that is not the case in this
instance. The usage proﬁles of those with geothermal heat pumps is so distinctly different from
that of others in their rate class, that even without their identifying themselves through some
formal application process, they would be easily identiﬁed with reasonable accuracy. (Note: I
do not propose such a method be used.)
Continuation of the current discriminatory policy is unwarranted since means to mitigate that
discrimination are easily available. Maintaining the GRIC, in its current form as a once-yearly
payment to registered class members, imposes little administrative burden on the Company.
The addition of a new, optional rate that would more equitably allocate costs would, once in-
corporated in the Company’s billing systems, impose no signiﬁcant burden.
The Commission should not tolerate the continuation of signiﬁcant discrimation without some
showing that reducing that discrimination would impose some undue burden. That has not,
and cannot be, shown in this case.
Recommendation to the Commission: The Company’s proposal to reduce and eventually
eliminate the GRIC should be denied. Additionally, the Commission should compel the Com-
pany to promptly prepare, for the Commission’s review, a proposal for a new, optional rate
whose intent and effect will be to reduce the inequitable excessive allocation of costs to users
of geothermal heat pumps.
Misallocation of Energy Efﬁciency Incentive Funding
The current GRIC is funded from RDM funds. This makes sense because, while users of geother-
mal heat pumps will typically have higher than average annual electricity consumption, the es-
timates of expected electricity sales are dominated by the consumption of “average” users who
do not yet typically use this technology. Thus, it is likely that some portion of the above average
consumption by heat pump users will increase the quantity of RDM funds that are available for
redistribution in ratepayers’ beneﬁt in future rate cases. Given that we can identify a speciﬁc
source of greater than average revenue collection, and given that we should wish to reduce the
scale of that excess revenue collection, it makes sense to fund the annual GRIC payments from
the RDM accounts which are inﬂated by excess payments by geothermal heat pump users. Do-
ing this has little, if any, impact on other ratepayers since the alternative would be to establish
more equitable rates which would probably result in users of geothermal heat pumps paying
even less than they do after the GRIC is provided.
The Company proposes to end the use of RDM funds to pay GRIC costs and instead use funds

that the Commission authorized for the purpose of providing incentives to encourage the in-
stallation of heat pumps. Thus, the Company proposes to use funds, intended to provide tem-
porary motivation to change consumer behavior, for the purpose of addressing long-term in-
adequacies in rate design. This is inappropriate.
It should be recognized that the GRIC, or any eventual replacement rate, is not and should not
be considered an “incentive” even though one effect of its provision may, in fact, be to incen-
tivize geothermal heat pump adoption by reducing costs through a more equitable allocation.
The GRIC is intended to address an inequity in rate design that should be addressed even if the
Commission was indifferent to consumers’ adoption of this technology. Rates are intended to
allocate costs with justice and in a manner which is both reasonable and non-discriminatory.
On the other hand, the purpose of incentives is not one of cost-allocation, but rather one of
achieving goals for the modiﬁcation of consumer behavior. This distinction between rates and
incentives is essential to maintain if the rate making process is to be well regulated and rational.
Recommendation to the Commission: The Company should be ordered to maintain the cur-
rent source of funding for the GRIC (i.e. RDM funds) and chastened to avoid using Commis-
sion approved incentive funds when addressing cost-allocation issues more appropriately ad-
dressed via rate making.
Irresponsible Cost-Recovery (Depreciation)
It is obvious to any well-informed, honest, and rational observer that the impact of the CLCPA
and other measures, social forces, etc. will cause a signiﬁcant reduction in the utilization of
the Company’s gas assets in the future. However, the Company proposes in this JP to maintain
the ﬁction that the "expected average useful life" of the bulk of its gas assets remains largely
unchanged from that in previous years. Thus, the Company implicitly denies what is obvious
to all others, and to itself: If the rate of cost recovery is not accelerated the Company will ac-
cumulate an increasing quantity of stranded assets that will create the risk of both losses for its
shareholders and unreasonable increases in the cost of service for its ratepayers. The Company
compounds the problem by insisting on continuing the practice of “straight-line” depreciation
in future years. This practice ensures that once depreciation is, in fact, accelerated, remain-
ing gas customers will be required to pay the costs of assets which were earlier abandoned by
others.
Gas assets will be stranded. There remain only 29 years until 2050, before which State law
(the CLCPA) requires that statewide GHG emissions must be reduced by 85%. A substantial
portion of those emissions are currently produced as a side-effect of burning fossil fuels, such
as the gas provided by the Company. In fact, more than 15% of current emissions can be at-
tributed to gas consumption, thus, that consumption must be reduced even if all other sources
of GHG emissions are eliminated before 2050. So far, neither the Company, nor any other gas
provider, nor the American Gas Association, nor any respected researcher, has presented any
compelling evidence that the Company’s gas assets will remain in use at the levels anticipated
when it was installed. Fantastic theories have been presented that propose that “Renewable
Natural Gas (RNG)” might displace the Company’s current methane gas, however, no broadly
accepted statement that sufﬁcient supply of such RNG has been presented. Others claim that
Hydrogen can be mixed with methane to reduce the degree of emissions. However, no one

has shown that we are capable of providing sufﬁcient supply of electricity to allow providing
sufﬁcient Hydrogen to achieve the CLCPA’s goals. In any case, the use of hydrogen is likely to
require wholesale replacement of consumer equipment, meters, regulators, and much of the
gas distribution network. Thus, even if technically feasible, responsible planners will assume
that Hydrogen would be, at best, a terribly inefﬁcient and economically ruinous alternative to
methane gas.
Given the absence of an alternative, low- or no-emission alternative to methane, we must as-
sume that the Company’s gas assets will be taken out of service earlier than currently estimated,
even though today’s estimates may have once been considered reasonable. The result will be
an accumulation of costly stranded assets whose costs will be borne by those who receive no
beneﬁt from them.
Straight-line depreciation is no longer appropriate. The Company proposes to continue us-
ing the long-traditional, but no longer appropriate, method of straight-line depreciation for its
gas assets. Straight-line depreciation allocates costs evenly across the expected useful life of
the asset. This method was an appropriate and reasonable cost-allocation method in previous
years when it was reasonably expected that assets would be fully utilized over their entire useful
life. However, even basic accounting texts teach that straight-line depreciation is not appropri-
ate for assets whose use will decline over time. For assets whose use will decline, it is much
more appropriate to use a depreciation method that explicitly allocates costs in proportion to
the beneﬁt received over time. For an asset whose use will decline over time, “Unit of Produc-
tion (UoP)” depreciation, which when applied to gas assets, would allocate costs for each future
year according to the portion of total gas deliveries which are completed in that year. Using UoP
depreciation, ratepayers in each year would pay for that portion of the total value provided by
the gas assets that they enjoyed during the year. Of course, it can be observed that straight-line
and UoP would produce largely the same results if assets were used at a constant rate over their
life. However, the two methods produce signiﬁcantly different results when asset utilization
declines.
The impact of using straight-line depreciation for an asset whose customer base is declining
can be shown with very simple math: Assume that an asset has an expected useful life of 85
years and that during that period, the number of users will decline at a constant, straight-line
rate, until in the last year only 1/85 of the customer base remains. In the 85th year the remaining
customers will be allocated the same aggregate costs (1.18% of the total) as was allocated to 85
times as many customers during the ﬁrst year of the asset’s life. Thus, the customers in the ﬁnal
year will have allocated to each of them an average share of costs equivalent to 85 times that
allocated to the average ratepayer in the ﬁrst year. Similarly, if an asset is expected to be in use
for only 30 years, yet its customer base decreases by 1/30th each year,ratepayers in the ﬁnal
year will be allocated 3.33% of the total costs – an individual share 30 times the costs allocated
to individual ratepayers during the ﬁrst year. Clearly, straight-line depreciation is unsuitable
when it is expected that the utilization of assets will be reduced over time.
The UoP method correctly allocates costs according to the portion of beneﬁt received in each
year. Thus, for an asset with a 30 year life and customer loss of 1/30th per year, the total quantity
of gas supplied over the 30 year period would be 15.5 times that delivered during the ﬁrst year.
Thus, in the ﬁrst year, rate payers would be allocated 6.45% of the total cost of the asset since

that is the portion of its expected value that they will consume. In the 30th year, ratepayers
would only be required to pay for 0.22% of the asset’s costs since they only beneﬁt from 0.22%
of its total beneﬁt during that year.
Some will object to UoP saying that it is difﬁcult to estimate, in advance, the declining utiliza-
tion of an asset over a period of 30 or more years. There is, of course, truth to this complaint.
However, it should be recognized that one of the reasons that we entrust the utilities to man-
age our energy resources is that they have regularly professed themselves competent to manage
those resources over time. Thus, it would be reasonable to require that they do the best they are
able to, perhaps in concert with proceedings such as Gas Planning, and then update their esti-
mates on a regular basis as the actual rates of decline become more evident. We may ﬁnd that
the normal three-year cycle of rate cases provides sufﬁcient opportunity to update estimates. If
the Company believes that updates in every rate case would be too infrequent, then the Com-
mission should consider requiring them to update them more frequently, perhaps yearly.
Long term estimates are always inaccurate, however, given the scale of difference between the
use of straight-line and UoP, it seems reasonable to assume that some inaccuracy of estimation
can be tolerated without producing too much of an aggregate harm for ratepayers and share-
holders.
The use of UoP depreciation ensures that the per-unit or per-therm cost of delivering gas can
stay stable over the life of the asset. This is in contrast to the straight-line method which would
require an 85X increase in delivery charges over 85 years, or a 30X increase over 30 years. In fact,
straight-line depreciation requires such dramatic increases in the per-unit costs that even if the
gas commodity itself were to somehow become available completely free, the costs of gas ser-
vice would still rise to an unreasonable level. The Commission must not tolerate cost-recovery
methods which imply the need to impose such signiﬁcant burdens on future rate payers. Gas
shareholders should also object to such a proposal because it is clearly unlikely to create a cost-
recovery regime that can be long tolerated by either the Commission or the public. To maintain
the value of their assets, shareholders should support changing the methods of allocating costs
for recovery purposes.
UoP depreciation prevents the intergenerational cost shifts that are inevitable with straight-
line depreciation. Of course, the elimination of that burden on future generations means that
current generations will need to pay a more equitable and greater share of total costs. Clearly,
increasing rates in the near term will be seen as negative for many and doing so may require
that those who are currently ﬁnancially constrained (LMI) may need to receive assistance to
compensate for the increased costs. Nonetheless, if the Commission is to uphold its require-
ment to ensure just and reasonable rates, it seems to me that it has little choice but to insist on
UoP depreciation or something like that. It is just that ratepayers should pay their fair share of
costs and, if we do not abandon straight-line depreciation, the result will be increasingly unrea-
sonable gas rates in the future.
If the Commission permits the Company to continue its current cost-recovery methods, the re-
sult is likely to have a discriminatory impact on low and moderate income customers. This
is because the replacement of gas service with more sustainable, clean alternatives such as
geothermal heat pumps, requires that one have access to substantial capital resources or debt

to cover the up-front costs of conversion. Given this, it is inevitable that those who are ﬁrst to
abandon gas will be those who are more fortunate. But, in abandoning gas service, those more
fortunate consumers will be leaving behind stranded assets whose costs will be recovered from
the less-fortunate remaining customers. This means, in essence, that low and moderate income
customers, because they can’t afford to switch to cheaper systems, will be forced to pay for as-
sets which were installed to serve the needs of wealthier and long-gone customers. Clearly, this
must be recognized as an inequitable and discriminatory result.
Recommendation to the Commission: The Commission should require that “Unit of Produc-
tion,” or some similar method appropriate for use with assets whose use will decline, be used
instead of straight-line depreciation when allocating the costs of gas assets. Additionally, the
Commission should require that the Company must adjust the expected useful lives of its as-
sets to more appropriately reﬂect the expected impact of the CLCPA and other forces that will
inevitably reduce gas consumption between now and 2050.

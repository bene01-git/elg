A. The Joint Proposal Advances Innovative Rate Design that Encourages Building 
Electrification and Addresses Energy Affordability 
 
The Joint Proposal creates a process for the Company to adopt simpler, more affordable 
rates that benefit heat pump customers, broaden the availability of its price guarantee program,

2. Alternative Rate Designs Can Lower Energy Costs for Heat Pump 
Customers 
Electric rates are designed to charge customers for their individual contributions to 
system costs. This typically includes a fixed charge for the delivery of service to customers, as 
well as a volumetric ($/kWh) charge determined by a customer’s actual use of the service. 
Alternative rate design can help customers that electrify by installing a heat pump to pay rates 
that are better aligned with their individual contributions to systemwide costs.8 
Electric utilities often use the costs incurred during the period of highest demand—
summer months—to calculate the fixed and volumetric rate of service for the entire year. Heat 
pump customers, however, will naturally have an increased load in the winter months as they use 
electricity for home heating. Those customers will therefore be paying during the winter months 
to heat their homes at a rate that is based on a summer demand peak, and thus is not reflective of 
their individual contributions to system costs.9 Rate designs that emphasize fixed charges and 
deemphasize volumetric charges, and that acknowledge both seasonal and time-of-use dynamics, 
can lead to more predictable charges, incentivize energy-saving behaviors, and encourage 
building electrification and energy efficiency measures. In this proceeding, EDF proposed two 
new rates—a Seasonal Heat Pump Rate and a Time-of-Use (“TOU”) Heat Pump Rate, which 
could save the average electric heat pump customer an estimated $439 to $507 a year in 
electricity costs, yielding as much as $131 million in cumulative bill savings over the rate

4. The Company Will Improve Customer Outreach & Education Regarding 
Alternative Rate Options 
Innovative rate designs can be a critical tool in saving New Yorkers money on energy and 
facilitating voluntary building electrification, but such approaches are only impactful if 
ratepayers understand what alternative rates are available and how to use them. Con Edison has 
installed over 43,000 heat pumps since 2020, and 14,018 in 2024 alone—indicating the success

heat pump customers are enrolled in SC 1 Rate IV, a residential rate that is expected to save 
money for heat pump users.17 In testimony, Alliance for a Green Economy (“AGREE”) 
explained that though SC 1 Rate IV has been “cost-saving for some, [the rate option] suffers 
from low enrollment due to limited awareness, complexity, and barrier for low-income 
customers.”18

adopt a heat pump and switch off of gas. The Joint Proposal ensures improved customer outreach 
efforts that provide residential ratepayers more information on rate options, including 
personalized recommendations. The Company commits to developing: a personalized rate 
calculator that uses real hourly data to find the lowest-cost rate option for individual households;

III. 
THE COMMISSION MUST ESTABLISH A STANDARD FOR EVALUATING 
GREENHOUSE GAS EMISSIONS IN RATE CASES 
Reducing GHG emissions is urgent because scientific evidence overwhelmingly 
demonstrates that climate change is causing immediate, devasting impacts, and that these harms 
will worsen dramatically unless we mitigate climate warming.28 The CLCPA mandates that New 
York State adopt measures to reduce statewide GHG emissions by 40% by 2030 and 85% by 
2050 (below 1990 levels) and achieve economywide net-zero emissions by 2050.29 CLCPA 
Section 7(2) requires that “all state agencies,” in “considering and issuing” administrative 
approvals and decisions, “shall consider whether such decisions are inconsistent with or will 
interfere with the attainment of the statewide greenhouse gas emissions limits”—and if so, the 
agency must adopt mitigation measures to address the related emissions.30

A. The Current State of GHG Emissions Accounting in Rate Cases 
In a 2021 order, the Commission confirmed that Section 7 of the CLCPA applies to its 
decision in utility rate cases.31 In a May 2022 order, the Commission stated that “it must 
establish clear and consistent statewide guidelines for GHG emissions reporting requirements to 
ensure that the State’s major electric and gas IOUs (collectively, the Utilities) are on track to 
meet the CLCPA targets,” and further directed New York utilities in future rate case filings to 
“include an assessment of the GHG emissions impacts of each specific investment, capital 
expenditure, program, and initiative included in their rate filings.”32  
The Commission has sought to evaluate rate case settlements pursuant to Section 7(2), 
but it has done so only through narrative analysis, and has not deployed quantitative analysis to 
verify the overall GHG emissions impact of a given utility rate plan. In its August 2025 order

otherwise. There is no reason why the emissions inventory proposed by the Joint Utilities should 
be considered more accurate than another approach advanced by EDF, especially when the 
Commission has never ruled on either approach.  
These differing approaches demonstrate the importance of using a consistent, clear 
approach to estimating and tracking GHG emissions. This is essential in order to reasonably 
compare different program options or track changes over time.

B. Statewide Policy Progress on GHG Emissions Accounting 
In a May 2022 order, the Commission directed the utilities “to work with DPS Staff to 
develop a proposal regarding the content of a GHG Emissions Inventory Report that includes an 
inventory of total gas system-wide emissions, following the methodology required in the CLCPA

report in December 2022, and after it was noticed for public comment, EDF filed comments in 
September 2023.42 EDF stated that the Commission, in addition to requiring annual GHG 
reporting, should “adopt a framework for reporting GHG emissions associated with rate case 
filings, as well as Joint Proposals (since rate cases in New York are typically resolved through 
multi-party settlements referred to as Joint Proposals).”43 The Commission has not acted on the 
Joint Utilities’ Proposed Methodology for Annual Greenhouse Gas Emissions Inventory 
Reporting.

C. Commission Action is Needed 
As the foregoing discussion demonstrates, the issue of GHG accounting falls within the 
range of reasonable likely outcomes that would have resulted in a fully litigated proceeding. In 
the absence of a consistent, statewide framework, the Joint Proposal’s provisions on GHG 
accounting represent meaningful progress on this issue. Going forward, and to improve the 
outcome of future rate cases, the Commission must also adopt a framework for quantifying GHG 
emissions associated with rate filing and settlements, to ensure that its decisions in rate cases are 
consistent with CLCPA Section 7. 
IV. 
CONCLUSION 
Several elements of the Joint Proposal advance environmental, clean energy, and 
affordability priorities that will benefit Con Edison ratepayers. The Joint Proposal satisfies the 
public interest standard and the Commission’s Settlement Guidelines and should therefore be 
approved.

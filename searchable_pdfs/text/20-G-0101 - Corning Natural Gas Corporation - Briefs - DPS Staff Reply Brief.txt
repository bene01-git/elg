I.  INTRODUCTION 
 
 Department of Public Service Staff (Staff) respectfully submits this reply brief pursuant 
to the briefing schedule set by Administrative Law Judges (ALJs) Sayre and Mullany in this 
proceeding.  On April 16, 2021, initial briefs were filed in these proceedings by Corning Natural 
Gas Corporation (Corning or the Company), Department of Public Service staff (Staff), Multiple 
Intervenors (MI), the Public Utility Law Project (PULP), the Utility Intervention Unit of the 
Department of State (UIU), and Mr. Bob Wyman (Mr. Wyman). 
The Staff reply brief will not discuss each litigated issue presented in the parties’ initial 
briefs.  This should not be construed as a concession, in whole or in part, of those issues not 
presented herein.  Staff’s silence in this brief on issues in contention merely indicates that we 
believe that the record is complete and that we have adequately addressed the parties’ arguments 
and evidence in our initial brief. 
In the interests of brevity, we have generally addressed in each section of this reply brief 
the veracity of comments contained in the parties’ briefs.  In addition, we have endeavored to: 
indicate instances of and clarify issues  “muddied” by a party; indicate instances where a party 
attempts to introduce non-record “evidence” in its initial brief; and, indicate instances where the 
parties have misconstrued or misrepresented our testimony and positions in these proceedings.

II.  ISSUES RELATED TO AUSTERITY  
The Company claims in its initial brief that Staff’s recommendations to the Commission 
in this proceeding are driven by outcomes, not the impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic (COVID-
19 or Pandemic) (Corning Initial Brief (CIB), at 6-9).  Corning claims an example of a “results-
oriented” approach is Staff’s argument that union employees wage increases be capped at 1.34% 
due to the long-term effects of the Pandemic, while the Staff Forecasting Panel states that the 
impacts would be temporary (CIB, at 7).  Corning has taken these words out of their context and 
use in Staff’s testimony on the forecast of gas sales to residential and commercial customers.  
In its testimony, the Staff Forecasting Panel was referring to the COVID-19 impact on 
commercial sales when making that statement.  Staff’s primary concern regarding the need for 
austerity is based on the impacts of a revenue requirement increase on residential customers who 
need natural gas to heat their homes and cook their food (Tr. 1053, Ln 20 to 1054, ln. 9).  Indeed, 
the Staff Forecasting Panel points out that COVID-19 does not impact the Panel’s residential 
sales forecast because those sales are mainly driven by customer growth which is relatively 
stable even during economic downturns, and not impacted by economic conditions.   
 
Corning also claims that Staff’s “results-oriented approach” impacts its request for 
recovery of labor expense (CIB, at 6-7, 15-16).  Yet, Staff’s argument in its pre-filed testimony is 
simply that, for union employees, a new collective bargaining agreement had yet to be executed, 
and there was the “potential” that COVID-19 could impact the negotiated salary increases for 
union employees.  As such, Staff recommended that the Commission use the current inflation 
rate (Tr. 688, Ln. 3-19).   It is the Commission’s long-standing policy that the amount and type 
of labor a regulated utility needs is decided in the first instance by the utility, subject to its 
obligation to provide safe and adequate service at a reasonable cost.  It is also the general policy

III.  REVENUE ISSUES AND CLAIMS REGARDING DEPRECIATION 
 
In his initial brief, Mr. Wyman posits a theory that   
“As gas sales in New York decline, those who produce and transport gas into New 
York, and to the Company, will find that their own fixed asset costs must be 
recovered from a declining sales volume.  The expected result should be increases 
in the per-unit allocation of fixed asset costs by the Company’s suppliers which will 
drive up gas commodity costs at the same time that rates are being increased due to 
the CLCPA’s impact on the Company’s own cost-recovery needs.”  
(Mr. Wyman Initial Brief, at  4).  
 
Staff views this as extremely unlikely given the fact that the pipelines that transport gas 
to Corning’s service territory charge rates regulated by the Federal Energy Regulatory 
Commission (FERC) and cannot exceed those rates absent a showing of cost justification.  Given 
that those pipelines serve customers in many other states, some of whom are currently converting 
coal fired power plants to natural gas fired plants, and the fact that Corning is a de minimus 
percentage of their total revenue, it is unlikely that FERC would agree that the loss of Corning’s 
throughput would justify a rate increase.  In addition, natural gas commodity prices are indexed 
and established through regional markets, thus, the lack of Corning’s throughput would have 
negligible impact on the market.  Thus, Mr. Wyman’s claim that this is a basis to modify 
depreciation rates now must be rejected by the Commission. 
Mr. Wyman also argues that Corning’s access to capital is also at risk as “…new 
existential risk, which is quite novel for gas companies, will be reflected in increased cost of

IV.  EXPENSES 
A. 
Tax Cuts for Jobs Act Double Recovery Issue 
In its initial brief at page 95, the Company states:  
“Corning asks that the make whole provision of the Commission’s Order granting 
an extension of the suspension period consider factors including, but not limited to 
expiration sur- credits and surcharges and relevant tax rates.  The objective, of 
course, should be consistent with the Commission’s objective of ensuring that 
customers and the Company will be in the same position in which they would have 
been had rates gone into effect as of February 1, 2021.”   
 
Staff agrees with this statement and understands that Corning is referring to the passing 
back of benefits associated with reduction of corporate tax rate from 35% to 21%.  The revenue 
requirement imputes the benefits of this reduction for the entire Rate Year.  Although rates will 
not be set until after the Rate Year has begun, the make whole provision ensures that ratepayers 
will receive the full year of benefits over the remaining months of the Rate Year.  However, 
there is a tax sur-credit in place that is passing the benefits back as well and that sur-credit will 
not be terminated until new rates go into effect.  As such, it is possible that ratepayers are 
receiving excess benefits associated with this tax rate change.  The Commission should direct 
Corning to include any double recovery concerns in its annual sur-credit reconciliation fillings 
required pursuant to the Commission’s order in Case 17-M-0815.4

B.  Tax Deferral 
In its initial brief, Corning incorrectly states that Staff’s proposed tracking adjustments to 
deferred taxes totaling $10,817 are not included in Staff’s revenue requirement calculation (CIB, 
at 55).  These amounts are included in Staff’s revenue requirement and are a combination of the 
tracking adjustments for all the regulatory deferrals for which Staff recommended the 
Commission change the amortization period from a three year amortization period to five years 
(Exhibit 37 (SAP-1), Schedule I, at 3-4).  The Commission should, therefore, disregard the 
Company’s claim that these adjustments were not included in Staff’s revenue requirement. 
 
 
C.  Clarification regarding Uncollectable Deferral 
The Company agrees with Staff’s calculation of the Rate Year uncollectible expense.  
(Tr. 121, Ln 5-7) .  On Page 27 of Corning’s Initial Brief the Company states, “both the Staff 
Accounting Panel and Corning are also in agreement that as an additional recovery mechanism to 
address uncollectible accounts, Corning should be permitted to file an accounting petition to 
defer the difference of the Rate Year and actual results.”  Staff agrees that a deferral petition may 
be necessary because of an increase of uncollectible expense, but it should be subject to the 
Commission’s policy regarding such deferrals. (Tr. 818, Ln 5 – Tr. 819. Ln 3)   Therefore, Staff 
recommends that the Commission clarify in its rate order that any deferral petition for 
uncollectible expense resulting from the economic impacts from the Pandemic will be subject to 
the Commissions policy regarding deferral petitions, which requires the standard three-prong 
deferral test for eligibility.

V.  FINANCIAL ISSUES 
A. 
Long Term Debt 
In its initial brief, Corning indicated for the first time that its Chief Financial Officer, 
Company witness Charles Lenns, has been in communication with its lender, M&T Bank, 
regarding prevailing interest rates.  According to the Company, M&T Bank indicated, via email, 
that it would be willing to charge Corning an interest rate of 4.57%, as of April 12, 2021.  
According to Corning, this was derived by adding the then-current yield on the 10-year Treasury 
Bill to a quoted spread of 290 basis points.  Corning notes that this interest rate of 4.57% is 
slightly above the 4.45% cost of long-term debt proposed by the Company, and that this updated 
cost should be used for the purposes of calculating the long-term debt in this proceeding (CIB at 
68). 
Staff takes issue with this attempt to update long term debt.  The email between Company 
witness Lenns and M&T Bank, referenced in Corning’s initial brief, is not in the record.  This is 
especially problematic in this case because Corning has previously claimed to forecast its future 
borrowing costs based on guidance from M&T Bank.  Staff noted in testimony that Corning 
initially forecasted that the interest rate on its future debt issuances would be fixed at 4.75% (Tr. 
1145, Ln. 14).  Corning claimed that 4.75% was the midpoint of a range provided by M&T Bank 
of 4.5% to 5.0%, providing a letter from M&T Bank dated February 11, 2020 as evidence 
(Response to DPS-266, Exhibit 40 (FP-1)).  However, nowhere in this letter was a range of 4.5% 
or 5.0% mentioned as the rate at which M&T Bank would be willing to lend to Corning, nor did 
the letter identify how M&T Bank would determine that interest rate (Tr. 1145, Ln. 24; Tr. 1146, 
Ln. 7).  Corning’s previous forecast of its future borrowing was not supported by 
communications from M&T Bank (Tr. 1146, Ln. 10).

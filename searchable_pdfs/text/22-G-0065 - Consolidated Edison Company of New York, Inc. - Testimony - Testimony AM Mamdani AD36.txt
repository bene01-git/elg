Q. Describe your office.
A: My office fights for a future in which each and every New Yorker lives a dignified life and where the

Q: Please describe the necessity for language access and precedent.
A: Executive Order 26.1 in March 20214 directs executive state agencies that provide direct public
services to offer language assistance services (translation and interpretation) to people with Limited
English Proficiency (LEP). EO 26.1 amended Executive Order 26, originally issued in October 2011.
There are millions of New Yorkers who do not speak English as their primary language and have limited
ability to read, speak, write, or understand English. For these New Yorkers, their LEP status presents
potential barriers to accessing important government programs and services.
This standard applies to all executive state agencies. It was created to increase public participation and
access. For example, all information about the Emergency Rental Assistance Program (ERAP) –– which
crucially provided $2.3 billion for rental and utility arrears (with an additional $800 million through this
year’s budget) –– was available in the 10 most common languages.
As of March 15, 2022, 403,045 households5 were in arrears for their Con Edison bills, totaling
$840,159,546. In other words, these thousands of households are already unable to afford their Con
Edison bills –– and they will be most impacted by the rate increase. This group is the same population
that ERAP was targeted to serve. Thus, just as ERAP was compliant with Executive Order 26.1, all
information regarding the rate filings should be as well.
Q: What does Executive Order 26.1 require?
A: Each executive state agency that provides direct public services is required to offer interpretation
services to individuals in their primary language with respect to the provision of services or benefits.

Climate Action
Q: What is the Climate Leadership and Community Protection Act (“CLCPA”)?
A: The Climate Leadership and Community Protection Act was signed into law on July 18, 2019. The
CLCPA calls for a 40% greenhouse gas emissions reduction by 2030 and 85% reduction by 2050. The
CLCPA requires that 70% of New York's electricity come from renewable sources by 2030 and 100% zero
emission generation by 2040.
Q: Is the CLCPA applicable to utility rate filings?
A: Yes. The Public Service Commission’s August 12, 2021 Order Approving the Joint Proposal with
Some Modifications in Cases 19-G-0309 and 19-G0310 established that Sections 7(2) and 7(3) of the
CLCPA apply to rate case decisions.
Q: What does Consolidated Edison claim about its compliance with the CLCPA?
A: The Company claims that its rate filings are consistent with the CLCPA’s emissions goals and does not
disproportionately burden disadvantaged communities.
Q: Do you agree?
A: No. The proposed investments in gas facilities and infrastructure will expand and prolong the lives of
fossil fuel infrastructure and result in increasing, rather than decreasing, greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions
now and in the future. Energy is by far the largest source of GHG emissions in NYS, with 76% resulting
from combusting fossil fuels, both to generate electricity and for heating and appliances in buildings, and
fugitive emissions in production, distribution, and at the site where the energy is used.10
The project of greatest concern to our constituency is the investment of $70.4 million over the next five
years in its Astoria LNG Plant. The Company describes five areas of investment: 1) $14 million for

Q: Please describe the legal history of LNG storage in New York City.
A: New LNG facilities were banned in all of New York State in 1976 following an accident at a Staten
Island storage facility that killed 40 workers. In 2015, New York State lifted the ban, but New York City
did not, keeping its statutory moratorium in place because of the public safety risk LNG presents in high
density areas. The Astoria LNG facility was built before the ban went into place so the facility has been
given a certain level of exemption protection since the structures predate applicable zoning restrictions.
The Company could not build the Astoria LNG facility today.
This is similar to the fracked gas power plant that NRG was trying to replace in Astoria. This proposal
received widespread public opposition, and was stopped by the Department of Environmental
Conservation.
Q: Please elaborate on the relation between the NRG power plant and the LNG storage facility.
A: In 2019, NRG restarted an old approval process for a fracked gas replacement plant. The new NRG
plant went through the majority of its approval process between 2009, when the plant was proposed, and
2012 when the project was paused. After NRG’s first attempt at this project, the landmark 2019 Climate
Leadership and Community Protection Act (CLCPA) became law and New York began requiring new
energy construction to undergo the more stringent and community-involved Article 10 process.
When restarting their process, they received an exemption from the Article 10 process, as well as what is
fundamentally an exemption from the latest climate regulations since the majority of their approvals
happened under 2012 climate regulations, not 2019 climate laws.
The Astoria plant that NRG was proposing to replace will be shut down by 2023 due to new pollution
limits passed by New York State. Although NRG was arguing that their new project was less polluting
than the existing plant, it is clear that we cannot make any investments in extending the life of fossil fuels
compliant with our climate goals.
On Oct 27, 2021, the DEC rejected NRG’s proposal to build a fracked gas peaker plant in Astoria – a
decision based on the 6,600 public comments they received and an analysis of the environmental impact
and necessity of the project.11 Leading up to this decision, our office worked with community members to

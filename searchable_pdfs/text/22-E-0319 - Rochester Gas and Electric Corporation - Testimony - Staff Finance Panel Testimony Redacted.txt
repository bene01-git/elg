FAIR RATE OF RETURN DISCUSSION 
6 
Q. 
Earlier you mentioned that the fair rate of 
7 
return you recommend will be used to establish 
8 
the Companies’ electric and gas revenue 
9 
requirements.  Please explain what the Panel 
10 
means by revenue requirement. 
11 
A. 
In the context of Commission rate proceedings, 
12 
the revenue requirement is the dollar amount 
13 
required by a company to provide service during 
14 
a rate year.  It is the amount that will allow 
15 
the company to recover all of its reasonably 
16 
expected operating costs, including taxes and 
17 
depreciation.  In addition, the revenue 
18 
requirement includes a fair return that will 
19 
allow the utility the opportunity to recover the 
20 
cost of funds supplied to it by investors.  The 
21 
funds provided by these investors are needed in 
22 
order for the company to finance its long-term 
23 
utility assets and working capital requirements, 
24 


--- Page 11 ---
Case 22-E-0317, et al. 
 
Staff Finance Panel 
 
 
10 
 
which in the rate-setting context is referred to 
1 
as its rate base. 
2 
Q. 
Generally speaking, what is a fair rate of 
3 
return for a regulated utility? 
4 
A. 
A fair rate of return for a regulated utility is 
5 
one that enables the utility to provide safe and 
6 
adequate service to its customers, while at the 
7 
same time assuring the company continuing 
8 
support in the capital markets for both its debt 
9 
and equity securities, at terms that are 
10 
reasonable given the company’s level of risk.  
11 
The fair rate of return, therefore, allows the 
12 
utility to recover its prudently incurred cost 
13 
of debt, while providing its common equity 
14 
investors the opportunity to earn a return that 
15 
is commensurate with the risk of their 
16 
investment.   
17 
Q. 
How is a fair rate of return calculated? 
18 
A. 
The fair rate of return for a utility company is 
19 
calculated through a weighted average of the 
20 
individual cost components of its expected 
21 
capitalization during the rate year.  The two 
22 
primary sources are long-term debt and common 
23 
equity.  Customer deposits, while typically a 
24 


--- Page 12 ---
Case 22-E-0317, et al. 
 
Staff Finance Panel 
 
 
11 
 
very small component, is generally reflected in 
1 
a company’s capitalization because it is a 
2 
relatively permanent and stable source of 
3 
capital employed by utilities.  It should be 
4 
noted that preferred stock is also sometimes a 
5 
source of capital, although generally in much 
6 
smaller proportions than either long-term debt 
7 
or common equity.  Since the Commission uses a 
8 
fully forecasted rate year, it is also important 
9 
that the rate year capitalization reflects the 
10 
utility’s projected capital requirements and 
11 
that it is consistent with the goal of achieving 
12 
the optimal cost of capital, particularly as it 
13 
relates to the use of leverage.   
14

How are the cost rates of the individual cost 
15

16 
A. 
The cost rates associated with the Company’s

long-term debt, preferred stock and customer

deposits are relatively simple to ascertain.  
19 
Both the embedded cost of the long-term debt and 
20 
preferred stock components can be readily

calculated by examining their contractual terms,

e.g., the interest payments for the long-term

The projected impact of the cost of any new or

incremental long-term debt or preferred stock 
3 
instruments, however, requires estimates using 
4 
relevant market data.  The cost rate for 
5 
customer deposits is simply a matter of applying 
6 
the cost rate that is currently prescribed by

Q. 
Is the cost of common equity typically more 
14 
expensive than the cost of debt for a utility? 
15 
A. 
Yes.  Even though both lenders and investors 
16 
supply the utility with the funds it needs to 
17 
build and operate its system, the common equity 
18 
investors only earn a return after the payment 
19 
of all other expenses.  Because these investors 
20 
run the risk that their achieved returns will 
21 
not equal their expectations, the return 
22 
required by a given utility’s common equity 
23 
investors is nearly always higher than that of

the utility’s long-term debt holders.  
1 
Exceptions may exist during periods of 
2 
disturbances in the market, such as during the 
3 
recessionary period of 1980 to 1982, at which 
4 
point the economy was beset with very high 
5 
inflation and volatile interest rates.  During 
6 
that time, utility bond yields were at least as 
7 
high as the equity returns the Commission 
8 
allowed, and far above the returns allowed by 
9 
most state regulatory commissions.

Q.  Please describe what is meant by the Panel’s 
6 
reference to ring-fencing?  
7 
A. 
Ring-fencing describes situations where legal 
8 
and operational mechanisms have been deployed to 
9 
insulate the utility from the potential risks 
10 
posed by the parent’s riskier, non-regulated 
11

13

14

15 
covenants.  In addition to restrictive 
16 
covenants, two other devices, a “golden share” 
17 
and a non-consolidation opinion, have been found 
18 
to be effective by the rating agencies.   
19

A “golden share” is a special class of preferred 
21 
stock to be held by a trustee approved by the 
22

would be independent of the utility and its

12 
Q. 
Please explain the basic theory underlying the 
13 
DCF methodology. 
14 
A. 
The DCF approach can be applied to any 
15 
investment instrument that has an intrinsic 
16 
value.  The DCF approach, as it relates to 
17 
common stock, recognizes that companies create 
18 
value for their stockholders by using their 
19 
earnings in a number of ways, the most important 
20 
of which, by far, is through the payment of cash 
21 
dividends.  Alternatively, earnings that are 
22 
retained by companies can be used to create 
23 
value by investing in capital projects designed 
24 


--- Page 63 ---
Case 22-E-0317, et al. 
 
Staff Finance Panel 
 
 
62 
 
to increase future profits.  The retained 
1 
earnings can also create value by retiring debt, 
2 
which reduces interest expense, thereby 
3 
resulting in greater cash flow available to 
4 
stockholders, and by buying back some of the 
5 
company’s common stock, which increases future 
6 
earnings on a per share basis. 
7 
 
 
It is important to note that, while 
8 
earnings drive companies’ dividend payout 
9 
policies, the value of the companies’ common 
10 
stock is always equal to the present value of 
11 
all future dividends.  This is because the 
12 
earnings that are retained will only have value 
13 
to stockholders when they are paid out to 
14 
stockholders in the future.  Underlying this 
15 
principle is the strong assumption in capital 
16 
market theory that companies earn the same 
17 
return on retained earnings as the market 
18 
demands on their common stock.   
19 
 
 
The DCF theory assures us that stocks only 
20 
have value because of the cash flows that 
21 
current investors receive or the anticipation of 
22 
cash flows that future investors hope to 
23 
receive.  Also, fundamental to the DCF 
24 


--- Page 64 ---
Case 22-E-0317, et al. 
 
Staff Finance Panel 
 
 
63 
 
methodology, is the notion that cash in the 
1 
future is not worth as much as cash today.  Due 
2 
to reasons such as the preference of individuals 
3 
to consume today rather than in the future, and 
4 
because of effects of expected inflation and 
5 
productivity on expected future cash flows, the 
6 
DCF discounts the future expected cash flows 
7 
according to investors’ return requirements. 
8 
Q. 
Why does the Panel place primary reliance on the

A. 
The main reason the DCF methodology continues to

be the preferred approach for determining a

immediate return requirements, as observed in 
14 
current stock prices and recent dividends, are 
15 
readily quantifiable.  The other principal

methodology, the CAPM, only relies tangentially,

through the use of utility beta values, upon 
18 
direct observations of actual utility investor 
19 
behavior.  The primary challenge in applying the 
20 
DCF is determining the rate of growth in future

dividends that investors expect.

Given the relatively mature and stable

CAPITAL ASSET PRICING MODEL 
16 
Q. 
Please describe the basic theory underlying the 
17 
CAPM. 
18 
A. 
The principle behind the CAPM theory is that 
19 
there is no premium, in terms of expected 
20 
return, for bearing risks that can be eliminated 
21 
through diversification.  According to the CAPM, 
22 
rational investors will hold a portfolio of 
23 
stocks, generally 60 or more, such that the 
24 


--- Page 75 ---
Case 22-E-0317, et al. 
 
Staff Finance Panel 
 
 
74 
 
overall risk of the portfolio, in terms of 
1 
variability of its returns, closely approximates 
2 
that of the market as a whole.  Thus, the only 
3 
risk that matters in the CAPM equation is said 
4 
to be systematic risk, or risk that cannot be 
5 
diversified away. 
6 
Q. 
Please explain systematic risk. 
7 
A. 
Systematic risk is a risk common to all equity 
8 
securities that cannot be eliminated through 
9 
diversification.  It is measured by a variable 
10 
denominated as “beta.”  Systematic risk is also 
11 
known as market risk, which is the uncertainty 
12 
inherent to the entire market or market segment.  
13 
Changes in interest rates, recessions, wars and 
14 
pandemics are all examples of systematic risks. 
15 
Q. 
What is unsystematic risk?   
16 
A. 
Unsystematic risk, on the other hand, is risk 
17 
that is specific to a particular stock.  While 
18 
it is assumed that most stocks tend to go along 
19 
with the general market, at least to some 
20 
extent, factors that are specific to an

II. GAS ISSUES
A. Overall Compliance with the Climate Leadership and Community Protection
Act
Q: What is the Climate Leadership and Community Protection Act (“CLCPA”)?
A: The Climate Leadership and Community Protection Act is groundbreaking climate and
environmental justice legislation signed into law on July 18, 2019. The CLCPA calls for a
statewide 40% greenhouse gas (“GHG”) emissions reduction by 2030 and 85% GHG emissions
reduction by 2050. The CLCPA requires that 70% of New York's electricity come from renewable
sources by 2030 and requires 100% zero emission electricity generation by 2040.
Q: Is the CLCPA applicable to utility rate cases?
A: Yes. The Public Service Commission’s August 12, 2021 Order Approving the Joint Proposal
with Some Modifications in Cases 19-G-0309 & 19-G0310 established that Sections 7(2) and
7(3) of the CLCPA apply to rate case decisions.
Q: Why are investments in gas infrastructure inconsistent with the requirements of the
CLCPA?
A: ​Natural gas has the highest GHG emissions level of all heating fuels, including coal, when
methane leakage is taken into account.1 Methane is a GHG with a global warming potential 86
times higher than that of carbon dioxide over a 20-year period.2 The Department of
Environmental Conservation cites 3.14% as the likely methane leakage rate along New York’s
gas distribution system.3 According to calculations by the nonprofit research and policy institute
Physicians, Scientists and Engineers (PSE) for Healthy Energy, a methane leakage rate of
3.14% represents a CO2 equivalent (CO2e) GHG level that is 98% higher than the CO2 level.4
In other words, when methane leakage is taken into account, the GHG emissions footprint of
natural gas used in New York’s buildings nearly doubles. PSE for Healthy Energy calculates that

regulators do not have conflicting directives from the Legislature, and have the authority

Astoria LNG
Q: Please describe the legal history of LNG storage in New York City.
A: New LNG facilities were banned in all of New York State in 1976 following a tragic accident at
a storage facility on Staten Island in 1973 that killed 40 workers. In 2015, New York State lifted
the ban, but not in New York City, which kept its statutory moratorium in place because of the
public safety risk LNG presents in high density areas. The Astoria LNG facility was built before
the ban went into place, so the facility has been given a certain level of exemption since the
structures predate applicable zoning restrictions.The Company would not be legally permitted to
build the Astoria LNG facility today.
Q: Is Consolidated Edison’s Astoria LNG plant located in, or would it impact,
Disadvantaged Communities?
A: Yes. The communities next to ConEd’s Astoria LNG plant, which is located in an industrial
compound on the waterfront with several other fossil fuel facilities, are Disadvantaged
Communities, as are the communities across the river from ConEd’s compound in the South
Bronx. Because of the heavy concentration of power plants and other fossil fuel infrastructure
and sources of pollution, this region is known as Asthma Alley.

Q: Are Public Service Commission utility rate case proceedings fundamentally fair to
intervening parties, particularly community intervenors?
A: No. Utility rate cases in New York State are characterized by procedural injustice and fail to
ensure the full and informed involvement of communities in the energy decisions that will affect
them.
Q: What are some of the procedural mechanisms that deprive affected communities of
meaningful participation in the rate case process?
A: A number of interconnected procedural mechanisms prevent affected communities from
participating meaningfully in the rate case process, including insufficient public notice
requirements, the power of utility companies to set the rate case agenda, steep information
asymmetry between the parties, and communities’ lack of resources and technical expertise.
Q: Why are public notice requirements insufficient?
A: After initiating a rate case in New York, a utility company need only publish notice of the
proposed rate increase in local newspapers once a week for four consecutive weeks at some
point before the effective date of the increase. The newspaper publication need not contain any
information about the proposed infrastructure projects that would be driving the rate increase,
the ongoing rate case, or how concerned ratepayers can intervene in the rate case. Thus, only if
a ratepayer happens to read their county newspaper on one of the four days of publication
would the ratepayer encounter the notice. In the event that a ratepayer actually encounters the
notice, the form and content of the notice—which are typically dense and inaccessible to a
layperson—discourage engagement. The inaccessibility of the notice prevents an average
ratepayer from clearly understanding what the notice is, in theory, intended to convey: that the
ratepayer will likely see an increase in their monthly energy bills. Even if the ratepayer walks
away with this minimal understanding, they are left in the dark as to what proposed capital
projects are driving the increase in their energy bills or how to get involved in the
decision-making process.
Public notice requirements are therefore woefully inadequate. Many ratepayers who are
extremely concerned about rising energy bills, environmental injustice, and the climate simply
have no idea that Consolidated Edison has even proposed a rate hike or that this utility rate
case proceeding is currently underway.
Q: Who sets the agenda of utility rate cases? Why is the power of agenda-setting
important and what does it mean in this context?
A: In New York State, a utility company initiates a rate case proceeding by filing a request with
DPS. This initial filing, which contains the company’s proposed rate increase and justification for
the increase, forms the basis of all future testimony and negotiations in the case. In other words,
utility companies have the power to set the rate case agenda and frame the terms of the debate.

As the scholar Robert Entman has written, “[f]raming essentially involves selection and salience.
To frame is to select some aspects of a perceived reality and make them more salient…in such
a way as to promote a particular problem definition, causal interpretation, moral evaluation,
and/or treatment recommendation.”41 Thus, framing “operates by selecting and highlighting
some features of reality while omitting others.”42 The omission of problem definitions, causal
interpretations, and recommendations is just as significant as inclusion, according to Entman,
because “[r]eceivers’ responses are clearly affected if they perceive and process information
about one interpretation and possess little or incommensurable data about alternatives.”43 In the
news media context, writes Entman, “the frame…is really the imprint of power—it registers the
identity of actors or interests that competed to dominate the text.” News texts therefore “[r]eflect
the play of power and boundaries of discourse over an issue,” and ideas that transcend these
“boundaries of discourse” are “unlikely to influence policy.”44
The power of framing and agenda-setting is apparent in rate case proceedings. The frame of a
utility company’s rate filing is “the imprint of power,” reflecting the “boundaries of discourse”
about the proposed rate hike. When community groups intervene in rate cases and raise
concerns about the environmental justice and climate implications of utility companies’
proposals, these concerns are largely ignored by the company and DPS because they “breach
the bounds” of what the company has determined to be “acceptable discourse” about the rate
proposal. In other words, since environmental justice and climate change are not on the agenda
set by the utility company, these issues of central concern to affected communities are not given
the weight they deserve in utility rate case proceedings.
Finally, another aspect of the rate case process exacerbates the problem of agenda-setting by
the utility company: the utility company’s prior rates are used as the baseline for proposed
increases. Rather than assessing rates anew, the utility company and regulators assume prior
rates as the starting point for an increase. The lack of any opportunity to challenge these base
rates is another function of the utility company’s agenda-setting power. Leading energy scholar
Heather Payne has advocated for the use of a budgeting method called zero-based planning in
utility rate cases.45 With zero-based planning, rate increases would not be connected to prior
rates.46 Instead, the ratemaking process would “start[] from zero,” all assumptions would be
explicitly acknowledged and vetted, and “automatic, incremental increases” would be
“specifically disallow[ed].”47 According to Payne, since “[t]he use of [zero-based planning]
requires the justification of every dollar spent,” this budgeting method would “further[] the goal of

Q: Describe the problem and implications of information asymmetry in utility rate case
proceedings.
A: It has long been noted that investor-owned utilities like Consolidated Edison have a perverse
financial incentive to build unnecessary and harmful capital infrastructure at the expense of
ratepayers and frontline communities. Information asymmetry compounds this problem during
rate case proceedings because “the regulated monopoly utility is in complete control of the
information that would indicate when a solution other than spending utility capital and adding
that amount to the rate base would be beneficial to consumers.”49 That is, since utility
companies have a financial incentive to invest in capital infrastructure and are often in exclusive
possession of information about alternatives to capital investment that would better serve the
interests of ratepayers, these alternatives may not even make it onto the table of rate case
proceedings. When it comes to utility ratemaking and the range of potential options for meeting
customers’ needs, regulators and intervenors necessarily operate at a severe information
disadvantage because they ‘don’t know what they don’t know.’
The problem of information asymmetry in rate cases is compounded by utility companies’
regular invocation of confidentiality to avoid sharing information. Under New York regulations, a
utility company may request “trade secret” or “confidential commercial” status for information
submitted to DPS if the information “would be likely to cause substantial injury to the competitive
position” of the company.50 Factors to be considered in making this determination include “the
extent to which the disclosure would cause unfair economic or competitive damage” and “the
worth or value of the information to the [company] and the [company’s] competitors.”51 As Payne
has written, however, “[t]hese are regulated monopoly businesses. Simply by definition, they are
not in competition with anyone—a regulated monopoly utility is a non-competitive business.
Therefore, any claim that a regulated monopoly utility needs to have business information kept
confidential for competitive reasons is absurd. There is no competition.”52
Finally, one of the clearest manifestations of non-transparency in utility rate cases is the
confidential settlement negotiation process. All settlement negotiations between the parties are
strictly confidential: community members affected by the outcome of the rate case have no seat
at the table unless they are registered parties, which the vast majority of community members
are not (in large part due to the time-and-resource-intensive nature of the rate case process).
Thus, community groups cannot relay the contents of settlement talks to the community
members they represent and solicit input or feedback without violating the confidentiality rules of
the negotiations. Community members therefore remain in the dark not only about the utility
company’s plans and intentions, but also about regulators’ positions and the extent to which
regulators challenge—or fail to challenge—the company.

Q: Describe the problem and implications of communities’ lack of resources and
technical expertise in utility rate case proceedings.
A: In an open acknowledgment of the generally limited or nonexistent role of the public in utility
rate cases, the vice chairman of the Montana Public Service Commission noted in 2017, “A
regulator should not be mistaken: the parties appearing before him are not the public, and the
interests of stakeholders together do not constitute the public interest.”53 In theory, community
members affected by the outcome of a rate case can weigh in on proposed distribution rates
and capital infrastructure projects by becoming parties to the proceeding, submitting testimony,
gathering information through the discovery process, cross-examining the utility company and
regulators during evidentiary hearings, and participating in settlement negotiations. In reality,
however, the typical energy consumer lacks the time, resources, and technical expertise needed
to meaningfully participate in a utility rate case.
The rate case process is “difficult for the average citizen to understand,” and mounting an
effective challenge to a utility company’s rate filing requires a “high degree of specialized
knowledge” about engineering, accounting, economics, law, environmental science, and the
utility industry.54 Well-resourced rate case parties can hire experts and attorneys, but community
groups and members of the public rarely have sufficient resources for expert consultants or
legal representation. Unlike in other administrative proceedings in New York, the state does not
provide funds for community intervenors in utility rate cases to pay for experts or lawyers.
Participation in utility rate cases is also a time-intensive endeavor. Rate cases can span 11
months. A typical community member working for 40 hours or more per week simply does not
have the time to review and digest rate case documents, draft Information Requests, prepare
testimony, and participate in months of negotiations.

undermine the Joint Proposal and, together, they provide no justification for rejection of the Joint 
Proposal as whole.2

B. 
The Joint Proposal is Consistent With the CLCPA  
1. WE ACT/AGREE Misstate the Law Because DEC Air Permitting 
Decisions Are Inapplicable to Utility Rate Case Decisions  
WE ACT/AGREE wrongly rely on DEC air permitting decisions to articulate the CLCPA 
framework that applies to utility rate cases.  In particular, they ignore the Commission’s caselaw 
and rely on case-specific DEC determinations to assert that the standard for section 7(2) 
compliance is whether a term would make CLCPA compliance more difficult or costly, that the 
Commission must evaluate each Joint Proposal term for CLCPA compliance on a standalone 
basis, and that the Commission is forbidden from looking across electric and gas provisions to 
mitigate terms potentially inconsistent with the CLCPA.34  Although the Joint Proposal satisfies 
WE ACT/AGREE’s proposed framework, the claim that their framework applies to utility rate

7. WE ACT/AGREE Misstate the Law Regarding Clean Energy Benefits  
WE ACT/AGREE misstate the law related to the overall benefits of spending on clean 
energy and energy efficiency programs.  Specifically, the CLCPA requires a minimum of 35 
percent of such benefits to accrue to disadvantaged communities.  WE ACT/AGREE wrongly 
assert that the 35 percent requirement applies to utility rate plans.  The law’s plain language, 
however, makes clear that it applies to statewide programs initiated by state agencies, not utility 
rate cases.  For example, the law is directed to “State agencies, authorities and entities,” not 
utilities.  Under the principle of ejusdem generis, a general word at the end of a series is limited 
by the previous specific words; thus, the general word “entities” covers only public organizations 
that are like State agencies and authorities.  In addition, the law requires agencies to consult with 
the Environmental Justice Working Group and the Climate Action Council, neither of which are 
parties to utility rate cases.  And while consultation with these entities makes sense when an 
agency is planning its programs, it does not fit well into the procedural rules that govern utility 
rate cases, which require that decisions be based on the record developed in that proceeding.  
Moreover, the Commission has never understood this part of the law as applying to utility rate 
cases and has never applied it as such.

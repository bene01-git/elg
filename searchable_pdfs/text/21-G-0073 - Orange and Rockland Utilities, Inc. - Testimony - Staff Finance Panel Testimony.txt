FAIR RATE OF RETURN 
1 
Q. 
Earlier, you mentioned that the fair rate of 
2 
return you recommend will be used to establish 
3 
the Company’s respective electric and gas 
4 
revenue requirements.  Please explain what you 
5 
mean by revenue requirement. 
6 
A. 
In the context of Commission rate proceedings, 
7 
the revenue requirement is the dollar amount 
8 
required by a company to provide service during 
9 
the Rate Year.  It is the amount that will allow 
10 
the company to recover all of its reasonably 
11 
expected operating costs, including taxes and 
12 
depreciation.  In addition, the revenue 
13 
requirement includes a fair return that will 
14 
allow the utility the opportunity to recover the 
15 
cost of funds supplied to it by investors.  The 
16 
funds provided by these investors are needed in 
17 
order for the company to finance its long-term 
18 
utility assets and working capital requirements, 
19 
which in the rate-setting context are referred 
20 
to as its rate base. 
21 
Q. 
Generally speaking, what is a fair rate of 
22 
return for a regulated utility? 
23 
A. 
A fair rate of return for a regulated utility is 
24 


--- Page 13 ---
Cases 21-G-0073, et al.    
 
STAFF FINANCE PANEL 
 
 
12 
 
one that enables it to provide safe and adequate 
1 
service to its customers, while at the same time 
2 
assuring it continuing support in the capital 
3 
markets for both its debt and equity securities, 
4 
at terms that are reasonable given the company’s 
5 
risk.  The fair rate of return, therefore, 
6 
allows the utility to recover its prudently 
7 
incurred cost of debt, while providing its 
8 
common equity investors the opportunity to earn 
9 
a return that is commensurate with the risk of 
10 
their investment. 
11 
Q. 
How is a fair rate of return calculated? 
12 
A. 
The fair rate of return for a utility company is 
13 
calculated through a weighted average of the 
14 
individual cost components of its expected 
15 
capitalization during the rate year.  The two 
16 
primary sources are long-term debt and common 
17 
equity.  Customer deposits, while typically a 
18 
very small component, are almost always 
19 
reflected in the expected capitalization as well 
20 
because they are a relatively permanent and 
21 
stable source of capital employed by utilities.  
22 
It should be noted that preferred stock is also 
23 
sometimes a source of capital, although 
24 


--- Page 14 ---
Cases 21-G-0073, et al.    
 
STAFF FINANCE PANEL 
 
 
13 
 
generally in much smaller proportions than 
1 
either long-term debt or common equity.   
2 
 
Since the Commission uses a fully forecasted 
3 
rate year, it is also important that the rate 
4 
year capitalization reflects the utility’s 
5 
projected capital requirements and that it is 
6 
consistent with the goal of achieving the 
7 
optimal cost of capital, particularly as it 
8 
relates to the use of leverage. 
9 
Q. 
How are the cost rates of the individual cost 
10 
components typically calculated? 
11 
A. 
The cost rates associated with a utility’s long-
12 
term debt, preferred stock and customer deposits 
13 
are relatively straightforward to ascertain.  
14 
Both the embedded cost of the long-term debt and 
15 
preferred stock components can be readily 
16 
calculated by examining the contractual terms, 
17 
e.g., the interest payments for the long-term 
18 
debt and the preferred dividends for the 
19 
preferred stock.  The projected impact of the 
20 
cost of any new or incremental long-term debt or 
21 
preferred stock issuances, however, requires 
22 
estimates using relevant market data.  The cost 
23 
rate for customer deposits is simply a matter of 
24 


--- Page 15 ---
Cases 21-G-0073, et al.    
 
STAFF FINANCE PANEL 
 
 
14 
 
applying the cost rate that is currently 
1 
prescribed by the Commission. 
2 
 
The cost of common equity is neither contractual 
3 
nor prescribed by the Commission.  Its 
4 
calculation is further complicated by the fact 
5 
that it cannot be directly observed, and instead 
6 
requires estimation and the opinion of analysts.  
7 
Q. 
Is the cost of common equity typically more 
8 
expensive than the cost of long-term debt for a 
9 
utility?   
10 
A. 
Yes.  Even though both lenders and common equity 
11 
investors supply the utility with the funds it 
12 
needs to build and operate its system, the 
13 
common equity investors only earn a return after 
14 
the payment of all other expenses.  Because 
15 
these investors run the risk that their achieved 
16 
returns will not equal their expectations, the 
17 
return required by a given utility’s common 
18 
equity investors is nearly always higher than 
19 
the concurrent yield requirements of investors 
20 
in that utility’s long-term debt instruments.  
21 
Exceptions may exist during periods of 
22 
disturbances in the market, such as during the 
23 
recessionary period of 1980-1982, at which point 
24 


--- Page 16 ---
Cases 21-G-0073, et al.    
 
STAFF FINANCE PANEL 
 
 
15 
 
the economy was beset with very high inflation 
1 
and volatile interest rates.  During that time, 
2 
utility bond yields were at least as high as the 
3 
equity returns the Commission allowed and far 
4 
above the returns allowed by most state 
5 
regulatory commissions.   
6 
Q. 
How can a utility’s cost of common equity be 
7 
measured? 
8 
A. 
The return requirements of a utility’s common 
9 
equity investors can only be gleaned through a 
10 
cost of equity analysis.  Generally, the 
11 
Commission has favored market-based 
12 
methodologies, such as the Discounted Cash Flow, 
13 
or DCF, and the Capital Asset Pricing Model, or 
14 
CAPM, to estimate the return required by common 
15 
equity investors.

15 
Q. 
Please explain the basic theory underlying the 
16 
DCF methodology. 
17 
A. 
The DCF approach can be applied to any 
18 
investment instrument that has an intrinsic 
19 
value.  The DCF approach, as it relates to 
20 
common stock, recognizes that companies create 
21 
value for their stockholders by using their 
22 
earnings in a number of ways, the most important 
23 
of which, by far, is through the payment of cash 
24 


--- Page 70 ---
Cases 21-G-0073, et al.    
 
STAFF FINANCE PANEL 
 
 
69 
 
dividends.  Alternatively, earnings that are 
1 
retained by companies can be used to create 
2 
value by investing in capital projects designed 
3 
to increase future profits.  The retained 
4 
earnings can also create value by retiring debt, 
5 
which reduces interest expense, thereby 
6 
resulting in greater cash flow available to 
7 
stockholders, and by buying back some of the 
8 
company’s common stock, which increases future 
9 
earnings on a per-share basis. 
10 
Q. 
What principles support the DCF? 
11 
A. 
It is important to note that, while earnings 
12 
drive companies’ dividend payout policies, the 
13 
value of the companies’ common stock is equal to 
14 
the present value of all future dividends.  This 
15 
is because the earnings that are retained will 
16 
only have value to the stockholder when they are 
17 
paid as dividends in the future.  Underlying 
18 
this principle is the strong assumption in 
19 
capital market theory that companies earn the 
20 
same return on retained earnings as the market 
21 
demands on their common stock.   
22 
 
The DCF theory assures us that stocks only have 
23 
value because of the cash flows that current 
24 


--- Page 71 ---
Cases 21-G-0073, et al.    
 
STAFF FINANCE PANEL 
 
 
70 
 
investors receive or the anticipation of cash 
1 
flows that future investors hope to receive.  
2 
Also fundamental to the DCF methodology is the 
3 
sentiment that cash and/or earnings held in the 
4 
future do not hold the same worth as cash or 
5 
earnings held in the present.  Due to the time 
6 
preference of people to spend their cash today 
7 
rather than waiting for the effects of inflation 
8 
and productivity to impact future cash flows, 
9 
the DCF discounts the future expected cash flows 
10 
according to investors’ return requirements. 
11 
Q.

12 
results of the DCF methodology? 
13 
A. 
The main reason the DCF methodology continues to 
14 
be the preferred approach for determining a 
15 
company’s cost of equity is that investors’ 
16 
immediate return requirements, as observed in 
17 
current stock prices and recent dividends, are 
18 
readily quantifiable.  The other principle 
19 
methodology, the CAPM, only relies tangentially 
20 
on direct observations of actual utility 
21 
investor behavior through the use of utility 
22 
beta values.

the relatively mature and stable nature of the

utility industry, such estimates can be derived

with a reasonable degree of certitude.  Also, 
5 
rational utility investors expect the growth in

future dividends to generally track the changes

in output, or growth in the overall economy, as

Q. 
Would you please describe the basic theory 
11 
underlying the CAPM? 
12 
A. 
The principle behind the CAPM theory is that 
13 
there is no premium, in terms of expected 
14 
return, for bearing risks that can be eliminated 
15 
through diversification.  According to the CAPM, 
16 
rational investors will hold a portfolio of 
17 
stocks, generally 60 or more, such that the 
18 
overall risk of that portfolio, in terms of the 
19 
variability of its returns, closely approximates 
20 
that of the market as a whole.  Thus, the only 
21 
risk that matters in the CAPM equation is said 
22 
to be “systematic” risk, or risk that cannot be 
23 
diversified away.   
24

Cases 21-G-0073, et al.

Q.  Please explain systematic risk. 
1 
A.  Systematic risk is a risk common to all equity 
2 
securities that cannot be eliminated through 
3 
diversification.  It is measured by a variable 
4 
denominated as “beta.”  Systematic risk is also 
5 
known as market risk, which is the uncertainty 
6 
inherent in the entire market or market segment.  
7 
Changes in interest rates, recessions, wars, and 
8 
pandemics are all examples of systematic risks. 
9 
Q.  What is unsystematic risk? 
10 
A. 
On the other hand, unsystematic risk is the risk 
11 
that is specific to a particular stock.  While 
12 
it is assumed that most stocks tend to go along 
13 
with the general market, at least to some 
14 
extent, factors that are specific to an 
15 
individual stock are said to affect its 
16 
“unsystematic” risk. 
17 
Q.  How does the CAPM measure an individual stock’s 
18 
risk? 
19 
A.  According to the CAPM, the appropriate way to 
20 
measure an individual stock’s risk is through a 
21 
correlation of its return relative to the market 
22 
as a whole, known as beta.  A stock with a beta 
23 
of 1.0 has a return that mirrors the return of 
24 


--- Page 82 ---
Cases 21-G-0073, et al.

81 
 
the “market,” usually taken to mean the S&P 500, 
1 
as a whole.  Betas of less than 1.0 indicate 
2 
that the stocks are less volatile than the 
3 
market as a whole.  Utility stocks, given the 
4 
moderating influence of regulation and the 
5 
accompanying perception of reduced risk, 
6 
typically have betas below 1.0.  Therefore, the 
7 
CAPM informs us that investors will only be 
8 
compensated for their actual risk, as measured 
9 
by beta.  In the case of estimating the return 
10 
requirements of utility investors, the CAPM 
11 
result will reflect the degree to which utility

stocks are less volatile than the market as a

whole.

COMPANY’S SINGLE STAGE DCF MODEL 
15 
Q. 
Please explain the Panel’s concerns with the 
16 
single-stage DCF model. 
17 
A. 
The underlying theory of the single-stage DCF 
18 
model assumes that dividends will grow at a 
19 
constant rate into the indefinite future.  
20 
However, based on historic observation, we know 
21 
that such constant long-term growth rarely, if 
22 
ever, happens in any industry.  Companies can go 
23 
through periods of slow growth or even shrinkage 
24 


--- Page 112 ---
Cases 21-G-0073, et al.    
 
STAFF FINANCE PANEL 
 
 
111 
 
on a short-term or near-term basis, and very few 
1 
companies can maintain long-term growth rates 
2 
well above the GDP for an extended period of 
3 
time.  This is even less likely to occur in the 
4 
highly regulated utility industry.  Thus, while 
5 
a constant long-term growth rate needs to be 
6 
assumed in order to derive an ROE estimate in a 
7 
DCF calculation, there are typically short-term 
8 
fluctuations in the true growth rate.  It is, 
9 
therefore, unlikely that recently evidenced and 
10 
presently expected near-term growth rates will 
11 
continue indefinitely into the future.  To 
12 
impute these figures for the long-term does not 
13 
yield valid results.  The Panel eschews the use 
14 
of a single-stage DCF analysis, preferring a 
15 
two-stage DCF process, which allows us to 
16 
account for short-term and long-term growth

FAIR RATE OF RETURN 
1 
Q. 
Earlier, the Panel mentioned that the fair rate 
2 
of return that it recommends will be used to 
3 
establish the Company’s revenue requirement.  
4 
Please explain what the Panel means by revenue 
5 
requirement. 
6 
A. 
In the context of Commission rate proceedings, 
7 
the revenue requirement is the dollar amount 
8 
required by a company to provide service during 
9 
a rate year.  It is the amount that will allow 
10 
the company to recover all of its reasonably 
11 
expected operating costs, including taxes and 
12 
depreciation.  In addition, the revenue 
13 
requirement includes a fair return that will 
14 
allow the utility the opportunity to recover the 
15 
cost of funds supplied to it by investors.  The 
16 
funds provided by these investors are necessary 
17 
for the company to finance its long-term utility 
18 
assets and working capital requirements, which 
19 
in the rate-setting context are referred to as 
20 
its “rate base.” 
21 
Q. 
Generally speaking, what is a fair rate of 
22 
return for a regulated utility? 
23 
A. 
A fair rate of return for a regulated utility is 
24 


--- Page 10 ---
Case 20-G-0101  
FINANCE PANEL 
 
 
9 
 
one that enables it to provide safe and adequate 
1 
service to its customers, while at the same time 
2 
assuring the company continuing support in the 
3 
capital markets for both its debt and equity 
4 
securities, at terms that are reasonable given 
5 
the company’s risk.  The fair rate of return, 
6 
therefore, allows the utility to recover its 
7 
prudently incurred cost of debt while providing 
8 
its common equity investors the opportunity to 
9 
earn a return that is commensurate with the risk 
10 
of their investment. 
11 
Q. 
How is a fair rate of return calculated? 
12 
A. 
The fair rate of return for a utility company is 
13 
calculated as the weighted average cost of the 
14 
individual components of its expected 
15 
capitalization during the rate year.  The two 
16 
primary sources are long-term debt and common 
17 
equity.  Customer deposits, while typically a 
18 
very small component, are almost always 
19 
reflected in the expected capitalization as well 
20 
because they are a relatively permanent and 
21 
stable source of capital employed by utilities.  
22 
Preferred stock and short-term debt are 
23 
sometimes a source of capital as well, although 
24 


--- Page 11 ---
Case 20-G-0101  
FINANCE PANEL 
 
 
10 
 
in smaller proportions than either long-term 
1 
debt or common equity.  Since the Commission 
2 
uses a fully forecast rate year, it is also 
3 
important that the rate year capitalization 
4 
reflects the utility’s projected capital 
5 
requirements and is consistent with the goal of 
6 
achieving the optimal cost of capital, 
7 
particularly as it relates to the use of 
8 
leverage. 
9 
Q. 
How are the cost rates of the individual cost 
10 
components of capitalization typically 
11 
calculated? 
12 
A. 
The cost rates associated with a utility’s long-
13 
debt, short-term debt, and customer deposits are 
14 
relatively straightforward to ascertain.  The 
15 
embedded cost of debt, both long-term and short-
16 
term, can be readily calculated by examining the 
17 
contractual terms; i.e., the interest payments 
18 
for the debt.  The projected impact of the cost 
19 
of any new or incremental debt, however, 
20 
requires estimates using relevant market data.  
21 
The cost rate for customer deposits is simply a 
22 
matter of applying the cost rate that is 
23 
currently prescribed by the Commission.  The 
24

11 
 
cost of common equity, however, is neither 
1 
contractual nor prescribed by the Commission.  
2 
Its calculation is further complicated by the 
3 
fact that it cannot be directly observed, and 
4 
instead requires estimation and the opinion of 
5 
analysts.  
6 
Q. 
Is the cost of common equity typically more 
7 
expensive than the cost of long-term debt for a 
8 
utility?   
9 
A. 
Yes.  Even though both lenders and common equity 
10 
investors supply the utility with the funds it 
11 
needs to build and operate its system, the 
12 
common equity investors only earn a return after 
13 
the payment of all other expenses.  Because 
14 
these investors run the risk that their achieved 
15 
returns will not meet their expectations, the 
16 
return required by common equity investors is 
17 
usually higher than that of the utility’s long-
18 
term debt holders.  Exceptions may exist during 
19 
periods of disturbances in the market, such as 
20 
during the recessionary period of 1980 to 1982, 
21 
at which point the economy was beset with very 
22 
high inflation and volatile interest rates.  
23 
During that time, utility bond yields were at 
24

12 
 
least as high as the equity returns the 
1 
Commission allowed, and far above the returns 
2 
allowed by most state regulatory commissions.

Q. 
How can a utility’s cost of common equity be 
4

DISCOUNTED CASH FLOW (DCF) METHODOLOGY  
2 
Q.  Please explain the basic theory underlying the 
3 
DCF methodology. 
4 
A.  The DCF approach can be applied to any 
5 
investment instrument that has an intrinsic 
6 
value.  The DCF approach, as it relates to 
7 
common stock, recognizes that companies create 
8 
value for their stockholders by using their 
9 
earnings in a number of ways, the most important 
10 
of which, by far, is through the payment of cash 
11 
dividends.  Alternatively, earnings that are 
12 
retained by companies can be used to create 
13 
value by investing in capital projects designed 
14 
to increase future profits.  The retained 
15 
earnings can also create value by retiring debt, 
16 
which reduces interest expense, thereby 
17 
resulting in greater cash flow available to 
18 
stockholders, and by buying back some of the 
19 
Company’s common stock, which increases future 
20 
earnings on a per-share basis. 
21 
Q. 
What principles support the DCF? 
22 
A. 
It is important to note that, while earnings 
23 
drive companies’ dividend payout policies, the 
24 


--- Page 58 ---
Case 20-G-0101  
FINANCE PANEL 
 
 
57 
 
value of the companies’ common stock is equal to 
1 
the present value of all future dividends.  This 
2 
is because the earnings that are retained will 
3 
only have value to the stockholder when they are 
4 
paid as dividends in the future.  Underlying 
5 
this principle is the strong assumption in 
6 
capital market theory that companies earn the 
7 
same return on retained earnings as the market 
8 
demands on their common stock.  Also fundamental 
9

Q. 
Please describe the basic theory underlying the 
10 
CAPM. 
11 
A. 
The principle behind the CAPM theory is that 
12 
there is no premium, in terms of expected 
13 
return, for bearing risks that can be eliminated 
14 
through diversification.  According to the CAPM, 
15 
rational investors will hold a portfolio of 
16 
stocks (generally 60 or more) such that the 
17 
overall risk of that portfolio, in terms of the 
18 
variability of its returns, closely approximates 
19 
that of the market as a whole.  Thus, the only 
20 
risk that matters in the CAPM equation is said 
21 
to be “systematic” risk or risk that cannot be 
22 
diversified away.   
23 
 
 
Systematic risk is a risk common to all 
24 


--- Page 68 ---
Case 20-G-0101  
FINANCE PANEL 
 
 
67 
 
equity securities that cannot be eliminated 
1 
through diversification.  It is measured by a 
2 
variable denominated as “beta.”  Systematic risk 
3 
is also known as market risk, which is the 
4 
uncertainty inherent in the entire market or 
5 
market segment.  Changes in interest rates, 
6 
recessions, wars, and pandemics are all examples 
7 
of systematic risks. 
8 
 
 
On the other hand, “unsystematic” risk is 
9 
risk that is specific to a particular stock.  
10 
While it is assumed that most stocks tend to go 
11 
along with the general market, at least to some 
12 
extent, factors that are specific to an 
13 
individual stock are said to affect its 
14 
“unsystematic” risk. 
15 
 
 
According to the CAPM, the appropriate way 
16 
to measure an individual stock’s risk is through 
17 
a correlation of its return relative to the 
18 
market as a whole, known as beta.  A stock with 
19 
a beta of 1.0 has a return that mirrors the 
20 
return of the “market,” usually taken to mean 
21 
the S&P 500, as a whole.  Betas of less than 
22 
1.0, which are typical for utility stocks given 
23 
the moderating influence of regulation and the 
24

Case 20-G-0101  
FINANCE PANEL 
 
 
68 
 
accompanying perception of reduced risk, 
1 
indicate that the stocks are less volatile than 
2 
the market as a whole.  The CAPM informs us that 
3 
investors will only be compensated for their 
4 
actual risk, as measured by beta.  Thus, in the 
5 
case of estimating the return requirements of 
6 
utility investors, the CAPM result will reflect 
7 
the degree to which utility stocks are less 
8 
volatile than the market as a whole.

18 
taxes, or restrictions on financial

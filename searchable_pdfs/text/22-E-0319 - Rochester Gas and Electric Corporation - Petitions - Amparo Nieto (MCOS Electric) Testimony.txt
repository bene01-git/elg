12

14

III. 
OBJECTIVES AND ELEMENTS OF MARGINAL COST STUDIES 
1 
A. Objectives 
2 
Q. 
Please describe the main goals of undertaking the MCOS Studies. 
3 
A. 
The MCOS study results are useful primarily to inform changes in rate design needed to 
4 
meet efficiency goals.  Efficiency in electric delivery service is achieved when the utility 
5 
can provide adequate grid reliability at the least overall cost, helping electricity rates to 
6 
stay as low as possible.  Marginal cost-based rates are also helpful to maintain equitable 
7 
allocation of revenue requirement within a given class and across classes.  This is 
8 
increasingly important as customers will exhibit more flexible loads with the increasing 
9 
adoption of DERs. 
10 
Q. 
Please summarize your view on the key guiding principles regarding use of marginal cost 
11 
information for rate design.  
12 
A. 
Efficient pricing requires designing rates so that they mirror as close as possible the 
13 
structure of the Companies’ marginal cost of service.  This calls for initially setting all 
14 
rate components at the corresponding marginal unit cost, then reconciling those charges 
15 
to fully recover the class revenue target.  This second step involves allocating non-
16 
marginal, sunk costs to the various rate charges and it is critical in rate design as it 
17 
directly impacts the extent to which rates can provide efficient price signals.  Fixed cost 
18 
allocation should ideally be done in a way that least distorts customer decisions regarding 
19 
the use of the grid, i.e., using more electricity and/or adopting on-site generation, relative 
20 
to the decisions the customer would make if rates were equal to marginal costs.  To 
21 
achieve that outcome, ideally the volumetric components of the rate would be reflecting 
22

needed to accommodate their load.

MARGINAL COST METHODS BY COMPONENT 
8 
A. Marginal Upstream and Distribution Costs  
9 
Q. 
Please describe the steps in calculating marginal costs of the upstream grid. 
10 
A. 
To estimate the cost of distribution substations and upstream lines and substations, I 
11 
reviewed NYSEG’s and RG&E’s capital expansion plan for the study period and filtered 
12 
out other investments that are merely driven by the need to modernize aging 
13 
infrastructure and replacements that do not add capacity.  Planned investments for 
14 
distribution substations, stated in 2022 dollars, were divided by added project capacity.  I 
15 
then converted this cost estimate to a dollar per kW of demand, using added load carrying 
16 
capability for every increment of kW of capacity, as per planning standards.  Forward-
17 
looking marginal cost estimates require considering the expected annual peak load 
18 
growth rates during the years the rates are going to be in effect.  Thus, to recognize that 
19 
only some parts of the service territory are experiencing load growth, I then applied an 
20 
adjustment factor based on the total peak load share of expected capacity-expanding areas 
21 
by year 2027.

B. Marginal Distribution Facilities Costs 
6 
Q.    
How did you estimate the marginal cost of local electric distribution facilities? 
7 
A. 
Transformers and local conductors are sized based on the maximum demands that the 
8 
customers connected to them can be expected to impose over the service life of those 
9 
facilities.  The planners’ expectation is that local distribution facilities will not be 
10 
expanded in response to month-to-month or year-to-year variations in actual usage, so 
11 
long as there is no change in the customers’ design demand.  These costs are not marginal 
12 
with regard to volume, but with major changes in customer ‘design demand’ that require 
13 
local distribution facility capacity to be expanded.  Thus, the appropriate approach to 
14 
identify these elements of the study is a review of the Companies work orders that 
15 
involve connection to a variety of customers in each rate class.  The Companies provided 
16 
sample of connection costs for urban, semi-rural and rural customers, single phase and 
17 
polyphase for residential and commercial customers for years 2017-2021.  Based on a 
18 
review of this information I computed a typical cost per kW of design demand, using the 
19 
transformer capacity, for each of the Companies’ rate classes.  The next step was to 
20 
annualize the local facilities investment for each Company using an economic carrying 
21 
charge, adding O&M expenses and appropriate marginal loading factors.

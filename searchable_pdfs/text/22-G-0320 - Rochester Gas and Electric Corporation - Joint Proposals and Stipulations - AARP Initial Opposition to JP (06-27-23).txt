 INTRODUCTION 
AARP New York opposes the JP for the reasons discussed below.  Three years ago, on July 15, 
2020, AARP New York filed its opposition to the electric portion of the joint proposal in 
NYSEG/RG&E’s previous rate proceedings (Cases 19-E-0378 et al.).  This paragraph that 
appears in the introductory section is largely relevant today.   
Significant components of the Electric Joint Proposal are harmful to the majority of 
residents of the NYSEG and RG&E service territories, which includes tens of 
thousands of AARP members 50 years of age and older, many of whom are low- to-
moderate income. Their struggles have been exacerbated by the profound impacts of 
the pandemic. Both COVID-19, according to a consensus among epidemiologists and 
other public health experts, and the virus’s financial and economic impacts, according 
to a consensus among economists, are likely to last several years after the 
Commission’s October 2020 session.  
 
Unfortunately for NYSEG’s and RG&E’s electric ratepayers, this period would extend 
beyond May 1, 2022, the day 18 months after the session on which the JP’s 24% 
electric delivery increase for NYSEG’s residential customers and 13.2% electric 
delivery increase for RG&E’s residential customers would be fully realized. These 
increases are likely understated due the operations of the Revenue Adjustment 
Mechanism and the Revenue Decoupling Mechanism.  
 
Even were the ALJs and the Commission to continue, as expected, to find solace in 
the Commission’s flawed guidance and practice pertaining to review of settlement 
agreements, surely the Commission’s “public interest” standard cannot be satisfied by 
a joint proposal that: (1) promotes double-digit residential electric delivery rate 
increases during this period; (2) implements a half-billion dollar project, the cost-
effectiveness of which is largely dependent upon the elimination of almost 200 utility 
jobs in areas of high unemployment; (3) fails once again, following the example of 
several previous joint proposals, to rapidly improve NYSEG’s second-class electric 
reliability by not funding industry-standard vegetation management protocols; and (4) 
rewards the Companies with a high ROE while perpetuating management practices 
found lacking in Commission-sponsored management audits and Staff testimonies.  
 
 The differences between the 2020 electric joint proposal and the current JP are significant, but not 
in a good way.  Like previous joint proposals, this JP (1) recommends an overly generous return on 
equity and earnings sharing scheme, (2) offers inadequate attention to vegetation management and 
associated poor reliability and high storm costs, (3) promotes unsupported capital expenditure 
project and program budgets, and (4) contains insufficient outreach and education measures to

increases cannot be in the public interest.

The JP’s Section VIII Is Not in the Public Interest Because It Fails To Adequately Address 
Severe Reliability Problems. 
The Companies claimed in their statement supporting the 2020 joint proposal resulting in the 
current rate plan (at 7-8): 
Specifically, the Joint Proposal reflects needed investments in: enhanced vegetation 
management to reduce service outages caused by trees and branches; capital 
investment to replace aging infrastructure; workforce (line and field personnel) to

The enhanced electric distribution vegetation management programs provided for 
under the Joint Proposal are particularly important for improving system reliability 
given that most outages in recent years have been caused by trees or branches. This 
is especially true for NYSEG, which is the only major utility in the state not granted 
sufficient funding to implement a five-year-cycle vegetation management plan. 
Other benefits of the enhanced electric distribution vegetation management 
programs include:  reducing storm restoration time and cost; improving customer 
satisfaction; improving public relations and interactions; improving worker and 
public safety; and reducing customer trim requests and associated cost.  
Yet, despite the hopeful claims, NYSEG failed to meet that joint proposal’s System Average 
Interruption Frequency Index (“SAIFI”) reliability targets in each year of the rate plan.  Negative 
revenue adjustments are not adequate to compensate customers for the inconvenience and harm 
resulting from loss of service.  This JP does not provide adequate funding to bring NYSEG up to 
the vegetation management standards practiced by the other electric utilities in New York, thereby 
making it more likely that customers will continue to pay high storm restoration costs..

Q. Thank you. Please proceed with your presentation. 
1 
 
2 
A. On August 27, 2020, employees of Central Hudson Gas and Electric Corporation testified 
3 
before the Public Service Commission on the impact of vegetation on electricity reliability and 
4 
the removal of trees that pose a threat to the transmission and distribution wires. The company 
5 
reported that the number of “danger trees” was “significantly higher than expected,” due to 
6 
emergent tree diseases, insect infestation, and invasive species. Specifically, pests include the 
7 
Emerald Ash Borer (affecting ash trees), the Wooly Adelgid (hemlock trees), Dutch Elm Disease 
8 
(elm trees), White Pine Decline (pine trees), and Gypsy Moths/Oak Wilt (oak trees). These 
9 
pests weaken trees and increase the likelihood that they will fall, possibly taking dangerously 
10 
sited distribution wires down with them.  
11 
 
12 
Trees have helped sustain a stable climate on Earth since the end of the last Ice Age 12,000 
13 
years ago. Forests are essential to the maintaining the Earth’s albedo—the reflectiveness of the 
14 
atmosphere that bounces solar radiation back from the planet—and for extracting carbon from 
15 
the atmosphere through photosynthesis. The increase of carbon in the atmosphere is a primary 
16 
cause of the “greenhouse effect” that traps solar heat and warms the Earth, resulting in climate 
17 
change and extreme weather. Preserving and expanding our forests is critical to slowing and 
18 
mitigating climate change. According to the International Union for the Conservation of Nature, 
19 
“Halting the loss and degradation of natural systems and promoting their restoration have 
20 
the potential to contribute over one-third of the total climate change mitigation scientists say is 
21 
required by 2030” (https://www.iucn.org/resources/issues-briefs/forests-and-climate-change). 
22 
 
 
23 
Unfortunately, trees are victims of climate change as well as solutions to it.  Climate change is a 
24 
primary driver of both the expansion and vigor of invasive tree pests identified by Central 
25

Hudson; in addition, warming temperatures weaken native trees, rendering them more 
1 
vulnerable to pests (Linnakoski Riikka, et al: Editorial: Forest Health Under Climate Change: 
2 
Effects on Tree Resilience, and Pest and Pathogen Dynamics, Frontiers in Plant Science 10 
3 
(2019) https://www.frontiersin.org/article/10.3389/fpls.2019.01157. The US Forestry Service 
4

invasive species along with arrival of new invasive species. Changing environmental conditions

can affect forest pest biology directly, or indirectly through impacts on tree physiology” (Laurel

Haavik, Northeastern US Forest Pests. https://www.fs.usda.gov/ccrc/topics/northeastern-us-

forest-pest). Specifically, “there is good evidence that some recent outbreaks of bark beetles

and defoliating insects are influenced by climate change and are having a large impact on

ecosystems as well as on communities of forest insects” (Pureswaran, D.S., Roques, A. &

Battisti, A. Forest Insects and Climate Change. Curr Forestry Rep 4, 35–50 (2018).

https://doi.org/10.1007/s40725-018-0075-6).

15 
According to Central Hudson’s August 22 testimony, the number of “danger trees” that have 
16 
been removed to protect distribution wires has increased each year. In 2017, 1,859 trees were 
17 
removed. In 2018, 3,078 trees were cut down. And in 2019, 4,303 trees were cut. That is an 
18 
increase of 60% between 2017 and 2018, and 71.5% between 2018 and 2019.  
19 
 
20 
Central Hudson’s tree removal services are an unwitting cog in a vicious feedback cycle. Fossil 
21 
fuels, such as natural gas that is fracked and burned for the generation of electricity, are the 
22 
primary driver of the Earth’s rising average temperatures. The resulting climate change 
23 
weakens trees by altering their environments and turbocharging insect infestations and arboreal 
24 
diseases. The trees fall or utilities that deliver electricity—most of it still from fossil fuels—must 
25 
remove these weak trees to protect the transmission and distribution infrastructure. The loss of

Q. Do you have suggestions for addressing this problem? 
5 
A. Yes. First, starting immediately, tree removal should be avoided whenever possible, 
6 
by using alley arms and other structural solutions to move the danger wires away from 
7 
our trees. In the longer term, distribution wires should be buried. This is admittedly 
8 
expensive but given worsening storms resulting from climate change and the resultant 
9 
damage to poles and overhead wires, with attendant repair costs and dangers of fire 
10 
and electrocution, it will become necessary. 
11 
A valuable contribution to solving this problem is for Central Hudson to plant trees in 
12 
numbers equal to or greater than the number the company removes, in locations where 
13 
they will not pose a threat to the wires.  A tree planting program targeting Hudson Valley 
14 
towns, businesses, homeowners would provide shade, mitigate the heat island effect, 
15 
and lower electricity demand in the summer. The trees should be broad-leafed, 
16 
deciduous species, such as maple, beech, and oak, allowing sunlight to warm these 
17 
areas in winter. Native trees support local ecosystems which are stressed by climate 
18 
change. Central Hudson should inform customers about the value of trees for passive 
19 
heat management, climate change mitigation, and wildlife.

Q.

4 
A. 
Depreciation represents the return of capital invested 
5 
to provide gas (or electric) service to customers.  
6 
Consistent with the USOA, depreciation is intended to 
7 
represent the allocation of the cost of an asset 
8 
(including net salvage) over its service life.  Lower 
9 
depreciation today means that there will be more to 
10 
recover in the future and, accordingly, will typically 
11 
mean higher depreciation in the future in order to 
12 
recover these costs over the service life.    
13 
  
There is also an additional way depreciation affects 
14 
customer rates.  Accumulated depreciation, which is 
15 
effectively a running total of depreciation that has 
16 
occurred in the past, is a reduction to rate base.  
17 
While higher depreciation will have a short-term 
18 
impact of increasing the revenue requirement in a 
19 
given year, its longer-term impact will be to reduce 
20 
rate base and, in turn, the return on rate base.  The 
21 
converse is also true.  Lower depreciation expense 
22 
will have a short-term effect of reducing the revenue 
23

-7- 
requirement but over the long-term will result in a 
1 
higher rate base and a higher return on rate base.  
2 
Thus, when considering the impact on customers due to 
3 
depreciation, there are both short-term and long-term 
4 
impacts to consider and often these impacts move in 
5 
the opposite direction from one another. 
6 
  
This concept perhaps explained most succinctly by 
7 
NARUC in the publication Public Utility Depreciation 
8 
Practices (p. 23): 
9 
 
The regulatory body prescribing depreciation 
10 
rates is thus confronted with a decision 
11 
which affects both short-run and long-run 
12 
interests of the customer and the company.  
13 
If a commission prescribes rates which yield 
14 
depreciation accruals that are too low, the 
15 
revenue requirement in the short run may be 
16 
lower.  But the requirements for income 
17 
taxes and return may offset the apparent 
18 
savings in depreciation expense, so service 
19 
rates in the long run may be higher.  If 
20 
depreciation rates are set so low that the 
21 
revenue requirement fails to repay the 
22 
capital invested in a group of property by 
23 
the end of its service life, confiscation 
24 
takes place or the unpaid cost remains in 
25 
the rate base until amortized or expensed.  
26 
On the other hand, if the regulatory body 
27 
establishes depreciation rates toward the 
28 
upper end of the zone of reasonableness, 
29 
rates for service will be higher in the 
30 
short-run, but may be lower in the long-run. 
31 
While lower depreciation today can have rate impacts

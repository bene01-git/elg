21

22
ment cannot be met without a dramatic reduction in the use of gas in New York State.
23
Because such a reduction will eliminate most of the Company’s gas sales revenue, it

Q. Why are stranded assets a problem?
8
A. Stranded assets are those assets whose use no longer generates sufﬁcient revenue to
9
cover their full costs.8 As the ratio of stranded to non-stranded assets increases, gas
10
delivery rates must be increased to provide sufﬁcient revenue to pay the costs not
11
only of assets which are “used and useful,” but also those stranded assets which are
12
not.
13
As the portion of assets which are stranded increases, ratepayers are required to pay
14
ever higher rates which reﬂect the costs of assets which provide them with little or
15
no beneﬁt. This is inequitable and represents and inter-generational cost-shift. In
16
essence, taking actions today which result in the future accumulation of stranded
17
assets forces future generations of ratepayers to pay costs which may have beneﬁted
18
earlier ratepayers, but provide the later ratepayers with little or no beneﬁt. When
19
this occurs, we can say that current ratepayers, who may approve the accumulation
20
of assets which will later become stranded, will not pay their fair share of the costs
21
associated with the beneﬁts they receive and that future ratepayers will be required
22
to pay more than their fair share.

Unfortunately, future ratepayers have no representation in this proceeding. Today,

Q. How does “decapitalization” serve ratepayer’s interests?
7
A. Given current State policy and law, as exempliﬁed by the CLCPA, decapitalization
8
is required to ensure that gas rates are fair, reasonable, and non-discriminatory
9
(FRAND). Such rates are required by the Public Service Law of New York State and
10
it is in the interest of ratepayers to ensure that rates do, in fact, meet these require-
11
ments. That is, of course, why the FRAND requirement is in the law.
12
Q. How does “decapitalization” ensure that rates meet the FRAND requirements?
13
A. By “decapitalization” I mean the reduction, or elimination, of the value of capital
14
which remains in the rate base and whose costs are the borne by ratepayers. Ideally,
15
the value gas ratebase, and the costs associated with it, would decline to essential
16
zero by 2050.
17
Fair:
As mentioned before, the accumulation of assets in the rate base which will
18
become stranded requires that future ratepayers pay for assets that will not provide
19
them with a beneﬁt. When one is required to pay costs which are unrelated to the
20
beneﬁts one receives, the result is an inequity or unfairness. Thus, a reduction or an
21
elimination of stranded assets produces rates which are more fair. It is fair to require
22
that ratepayers pay for the assets whose beneﬁts they enjoy. It is not fair to require
23
them to pay for assets which provide them with little or no beneﬁt.

Reasonable:
As the volume of gas sales declines over the next thirty years, the scale
1
of the infrastructure required to deliver gas will decline at a much slower rate. The
2
inevitable result will be that volumetric delivery rates, which are primarily driven by
3
the cost of infrastructure (e.g. the ratebase), will be increased to cover the costs of an
4
increasingly large portion of the remaining infrastructure costs. In time, this means
5
that even if the cost of the gas commodity was somehow driven to zero, the per unit
6
cost of delivered gas will probably rise continuously. As that cost increases, we’ll ﬁnd
7
that the cost of gas becomes more and more unreasonable; for more and more con-
8
sumers, the cost of gas delivery will become greater than the value of the service pro-
9
vided.
10
As gas delivery rates increase, more and more gas consumers will decide that it makes
11
economic sense for them to abandon unreasonably costly gas service and convert to
12
cheaper, cleaner, and more sustainable alternatives such as heat pumps and induc-
13
tion cooking. This will, of course, accelerate the rate of decline in gas sales which will,
14
in turn, further increase the rate of increase in the already unreasonable gas delivery
15
rates. Some would call this kind of feedback a “death spiral.”
16
The rate at which gas delivery rates become unreasonable can be reduced by ensuring
17
timely reductions in the ratebase and thus the reduction of the costs that will drive
18
rates. While it is probably inevitable that we’ll experience a “death spiral” in gas use at
19
some point in the future, its timing and severity can be moderated by ensuring timely
20
decapitalization of gas assets in the ratebase.

Non-Discriminatory:
As mentioned above, the decline of gas sales will result in
1
the accumulation of stranded gas delivery assets and an inter-generational cost-shift
2
from current ratepayers to future ratepayers (or taxpayers). Thus, unless steps are
3
taken to eliminate the accumulation of stranded assets, we will be pursuing a policy
4
that discriminates in favor of current rate payers and against future rate payers. While
5
it may not be intended, it is also quite likely that the burden created by an accumu-
6
lation of stranded assets will tend to be borne disproportionately by members of the
7
low and moderate income (LMI) community.
8
While it is reasonable to expect that the alternatives to gas service will become in-
9
creasingly cheap and easily obtained, it is also reasonable to expect that conversion
10
from gas to alternatives, such as heat pumps and induction cooking, will require an
11
upfront capital investment by consumers. Due to this capital requirement, we will
12
see that those in the community who have access to greater wealth or credit will be
13
the ﬁrst to ﬁnd it feasible to replace their gas service. This means that the portion of
14
remaining gas customers who are among the LMI will increase. In time, we should’t
15
be surprised to discover that substantially all of the remaining gas customers are LMI
16
and that gas has become a “poor man’s fuel.” Thus, those who will be burdened with
17
the responsibility to pay for the assets left stranded by the more fortunate who previ-
18
ously abandoned gas service, will be those members of the community least able to
19
shoulder those burdens. Thus, a failure to decapitalize and thus avoid accumulation
20
of stranded assets will have the impact of discriminating in favor of the fortunate, at
21
the expense of the less fortunate. Surely, this cannot be a result considered proper.
22
So, implementing a managed process of decapitalization is vital to ensuring fair, rea-
23
sonable, and non-discrimintory rates, as required by the New York State Public Ser-
24
vice Law.

Q. What should be included in a well-deﬁned process of managed decapitalization?
1
A. The speciﬁcation of a full process will require a great deal of research, innovation,
2
and dialog. However, some initial steps can be deﬁned now, as the process hopefully
3
begins:
4
Cease Marketing of Gas
As has often been said, "The ﬁrst step in ﬁlling a hole is to
5
stop digging it deeper..." Thus, the ﬁrst step in reducing the burden of stranded assets
6
is simply to stop encouraging increased demand whose temporary satisfaction will
7
lead to a greater accumulation of future stranded assets.
8
Cease speculative provisioning
Gas utilities in New York State have an obligation to
9
provide safe and reliable service to existing customers. They also have an obligation
10
to provide service to new customers, when sufﬁcient gas supply exists and under a
11
variety of constraints. However, the law does not require gas utilities to ensure that
12
sufﬁcient gas supply and delivery capacity exists to anticipate the demands of po-
13
tential new customers. The primary responsibility of gas utilities is to their existing
14
customers. The needs of others, who may or may not wish to one-day become cus-
15
tomers, are secondary and conditional.
16
In the past, it was common for gas utilities to ensure that new capital projects were
17
sized in such a way that they satisﬁed not only their responsibilities to existing cus-
18
tomers, but also provided capacity that could be provided to potential customers.
19
This made sense in a world in which it was assumed by all that the scope of the gas
20
service would increase in the future. However, we are now in a very different world.
21
Given that CLCPA and other policy, it is now clear that we expect gas service to de-
22
cline in the future. Furthermore, that decline is likely, due to stranded assets, and
23
other factors, to impose signiﬁcant burdens on existing customers. The degree of

Accelerated Depreciation
In order to maintain fair,
reasonable and non-
11
discriminatory rates, and to avoid the potential of shifting the costs of the gas service
12
to taxpayers, it is essential to ensure that ratepayers pay a fair share of the costs of
13
the beneﬁts they receive. However, the current accounting practices of the Company
14
and their proposal in this rate case, ensures that this will not happen. The problem
15
is that many of the assets in the ratebase have been assigned "average service lives"
16
(ASL) which are greater than the thirty years that remain between today and 2050.
17
These use of these ASLs ensures that stranded assets will accumulate until 2050 and
18
will remain well after 2050.
19
The costs of new capital assets, additions to the ratebase, are not charged to cus-
20

21
sumed to have an average service life of say eighty-ﬁve years, ratepayers, each year,
22
pay one-eighty-ﬁfth of the initial cost of the asset plus the cost of ﬁnancing the re-
23
maining unpaid balance for an additional year. The idea here is that if an asset is to

Q. What will happen if the ratebase is not decapitalized by 2050?
13
A. If, as is reasonable to assume, the customer base will have been dramatically or en-
14
tirely eliminated by 2050, there simply won’t be sufﬁcient customer revenue to sup-
15

16
might increase, they probably can’t be increased enough to ensure that the costs are
17
covered. At that point, either the Company will have to accept that it will not be able
18
to recover costs for assets which are no longer “used and useful” or the burden of
19
paying the costs will have to be shifted either to taxpayers or to electric ratepayers.
20
Neither of these options is particularly pleasant. Thus, it is imperative that the com-
21
pany implement a process of managed decapitalization to ensure that the problem
22
doesn’t arise. Accelerated depreciation, which would more accurately reﬂect reason-
23
able expectations for asset lives, must be an essential element of such a process.

RNG and Hydrogen
1
Q. Why should the Company cease efforts to replace natural gas with RNG and Hydro-
2
gen
3
A. Investing in assets intended to replace natural gas with RNG and Hydrogen would
4
only make sense if it can be established that doing so would allow continued high
5
levels of use of gas assets after 2050. However, the Company has not presented com-
6
pelling evidence that either RNG, Hydrogen, or both would, in fact, allow continued
7
use of existing assets. Thus, it is much more rational to focus on the process decapi-
8
talization while investing any available funds in assets that are likely to provide bene-
9
ﬁts that continue after 2050. In other words, if we’re going to spend ratepayer money,
10
it should be on the electric system or on reducing the cost of displacing gas service
11
with cleaner, cheaper, more sustainable systems such as heat pumps and induction
12
cooking.

Wastewater Heat Recovery
9
Q. Why should the Company provide incentives for wastewater heat recovery?
10
A. Particularly in multi-family buildings, the heating of water can consume as much
11
or more than 20% of the energy consumed by the building. However, much of the
12
thermal energy used to heat water is almost immediately ﬂushed down the drain.
13
Wastewater heat recovery systems can recover this heat and use it to reheat water for
14
future use. This recycling of heat can signiﬁcantly reduce the total energy require-
15
ment for a building while also reducing the GHG emissions produced as a result of
16
operating the building. Today, incentives are not deﬁned in such a way that it is clear
17
that such systems are covered, even though they provide much the same beneﬁt as
18
other systems that are covered. In order to address this lacunae in the existing and
19
proposed incentives, heat pump wastewater heat recovery systems should be explic-
20
itly covered.

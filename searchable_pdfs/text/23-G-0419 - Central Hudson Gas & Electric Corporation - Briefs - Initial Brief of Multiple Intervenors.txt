I. 
INTRODUCTION  
 
A. 
Overview 
 
Multiple Intervenors, an unincorporated association of over 55 large industrial, 
commercial, and institutional energy consumers with manufacturing and other facilities located 
throughout New York State, including the Central Hudson Gas and Electric Corporation (“Central 
Hudson” or “Company”) service territory, hereby submits its Initial Post-Hearing Brief pursuant 
to the  bench ruling issued during the evidentiary hearing held on February 6, 2024.1 
Central Hudson is proposing electric and gas revenue requirement increases that are 
exorbitant and not in the public interest.  A utility’s revenue requirement necessarily reflects a 
balancing of potentially-competing interests.  One such interest shared by many parties – including 
Multiple Intervenors – is a desire to reduce emissions in the Central Hudson service territory.  
Electric and gas revenue requirements must balance this interest with sufficient, necessary capital 
investments to maintain system reliability without rendering electric and gas services unaffordable.   
Every request for rate relief necessarily requires careful distinction between different 
“wants” and “needs” and it is becoming increasingly difficult to discern how State goals regarding 
energy affordability, system reliability, and decarbonization can be harmonized.  Central Hudson’s 
rate filing exemplifies this challenge because it (i) proposes dramatic rate increases that threaten 
affordability, (ii) claims that a material portion of the requested rate relief is driven by capital 
investments necessary to maintain system reliability, and (ii) claims that a material portion of the

It is increasingly important to prioritize affordability for all customer types, especially in

B. 
Procedural History 
 
On July 31, 2023, Central Hudson filed electric and gas delivery rate cases with the New 
York State Public Service Commission (“Commission”).  The rate cases focus on the 12-month 
period running from July 1, 2024 through June 30, 2025 (the “Rate Year”), and seek extraordinary 
amounts of rate relief from customers.  Central Hudson proposed initially to increase annual 
electric delivery revenues by $139.5 million, or 31.6 percent, and annual gas delivery revenues 
by $41.5 million, or 29.8 percent.2  These increases are understated insofar as they do not reflect 
the impact of electric ($21.5 million) and gas ($5.6 million) bill credits that will expire on June 30, 
2024 (i.e., delivery rates on July 1, 2024 will appear to increase by this amount in addition to any 
rate relief granted in this proceeding).3

C. 
Summary of Multiple Intervenors’ Positions 
 
For the reasons detailed herein, the revenue requirements proposed by Central Hudson – 
and the associated delivery rate increases that customers would experience – are excessive, 
contrary to the public interest, and would result in significant harm to customers of all types, as 
well as to ongoing economic attraction and retention efforts in the Company’s service territory.  
Multiple Intervenors demonstrates in Section IV – Rate Base Issues, that the capital budgets

proposed by Central Hudson represent material rate drivers, likely are inflated, and should be 
reduced. 
In Section V – Climate Leadership and Sustainability Initiatives, Multiple Intervenors 
demonstrates that the exorbitant request for rate relief leaves little to no room to consider 
discretionary incremental investments, including those related to the energy policy targets 
established by the CLCPA, beyond the substantial investments for which the Company’s 
customers already are obligated.  The additional spending that Central Hudson proposes to 
undertake on a voluntary basis should be denied in full.  Multiple Intervenors explains that these 
investments are not necessary for the Company to achieve its pro rata share of the statewide 2030 
emissions reduction target early, in 2028, and the incremental discretionary costs would prop up 
proposed rate increases that are excessive and must be reduced materially.  If the proposed 
voluntary investments are not denied in full, then the Company should be required to evaluate 
whether it would be more cost-effective for a third party to own and operate assets such as onsite 
solar facilities. 
In Section VI – Rate of Return / Financial Issues, Multiple Intervenors supports the Staff 
recommendation that the allowed return on common equity (“ROE”) for Central Hudson be set at 
9.2 percent, and not at 9.8 percent as proposed by the Company.

Central Hudson should not be awarded any financial incentives tied to Earnings Adjustment

recommendation is not adopted, however, then the EAMs should be set for no longer than one-
year at no higher than the lesser of the current rate plan and the basis points recommended by Staff,

A. – C. Electric Capital, Gas Capital, and Common Capital 
a. 
Capital Budgets, Generally 
 
Increased electric and gas plant in service are material drivers of the Company’s proposed 
revenue requirement increases.  Capital investment represents approximately $33.6 million 
(24.1%) and $14.3 million (34.5%) of the Company’s initial electric and gas rate increase 
proposals.  Multiple Intervenors understands that the provision of electric and gas delivery service 
is capital-intensive, and utility capital expenditures may rise gradually over time.  Capital 
expenditures also are necessary to maintain – and improve – system reliability and resiliency, both 
of which are essential to the operations of Multiple Intervenors members.  Significantly, however, 
Central Hudson’s capital budgets have been rising sharply for a decade.  The proposed increases 
are an inflection point where the pace of capital spending could increase sharply if the Company’s

XIV. REVENUE ALLOCATION AND RATE DESIGN 
 
The Company included two ECOS studies in its rate filing: (1) an historic study that used 
actual data for calendar year 2021; and (2) a pro forma study (also referred to at times as a Rate 
Year study) that used forecast data for the proposed Rate Year ending June 30, 2025.117  ECOS 
studies generally are used to determine each class’s responsibility for a utility’s costs,118 and the 
Company explained that these studies serve different purposes.  Whereas the historical ECOS 
studies document actual realized rate base, revenues, and expenses by rate class, the primary 
purpose of the pro forma study is to “provide a frame of reference and guidance for the design of 
cost-based delivery service rates that will produce relative ROR uniformity among the various rate 
classes.”119   
The basic procedure for conducting a class ECOS study consists of three steps.  First, plant 
investment and expenses are functionalized according to the activity for which the cost was 
incurred.120  The utility’s electric investments and expenses are separated into production, 
transmission, distribution, and other functions, whereas its gas investments and expenses are 
separated into production, storage, transmission, distribution, and other functions.121  This exercise 
is conducted largely according to the Uniform System of Accounts developed by the Federal 
Energy Regulatory Commission (“FERC”).122

Second, functionalized costs are then classified based on their primary causative

investment and related fixed operations and maintenance (“O&M”) expenses.  Energy-related 
costs vary with production in proportion to the total volume of electricity or natural gas that is 
delivered, and are measured in kW-hours (“kWh”) or dekatherms and include fuel and variable 
O&M expenses.125  Customer-related costs vary in proportion to the number of customers and 
include such expenses as portions of the electric distribution network and gas distribution mains.126    
Third, functionalized and classified costs are allocated to the various customer classes.127  
This is accomplished using allocation factors that reflect cost causation, meaning that they assign 
to each class a percentage of the total cost that should be paid by each class.128 
Finally, the “Matching Principle” is a cornerstone of ratemaking that, as applied to ECOS 
studies, means that the same assumptions should be used in determining both costs and 
revenues.129

Legacy Generation Fleet 
 
Central Hudson owns and operates a small fleet of gas-fired combustion turbines and run-
of-river hydroelectric generation units.176  Multiple Intervenors, Central Hudson, and Staff have 
conflicting positions regarding the method by which revenues associated with these assets are 
accounted for in the ECOS studies, and the amount of revenues that should be assumed regardless

that Central Hudson fails to pass the benefits of its generation fleet to ratepayers.  The issue instead

distorting the cost study results, including class rates of return.

Hudson generating assets either should be included in, or excluded from, the historic and pro forma

for the asset’s corresponding benefits in the study violates the Matching Principle, which is a core 
tenet of how ECOS studies should be conducted.  Also, the amount of revenues associated with 
the generation fleet must be increased to more accurately reflect likely Rate Year benefits.

Q. 
What is the Climate Justice Working Group and what has been your role? 
1 
A. 
The Climate Justice Working Group (“CJWG”) is a working group mandated by the 
2 
CLCPA to define “disadvantaged communities.”  The CJWG is made up of 
3 
representatives from state agencies and environmental justice communities. My role on 
4 
the CJWG is as a representative of an environmental justice community. I attend 
5 
meetings and advise on the development of the criteria to define DACs. I also contributed

7 
State’s Community Air Monitoring project, and advised all Climate Action Council 
8 
advisory panels on the development of their recommendations. The CJWG is mandated 
9 
to meet at least once annually in perpetuity to adjust the DAC criteria as needed.

Q. 
Please summarize your conclusions and recommendations. 
15 
A. 
I begin by defining environmental justice and describing the significance of the 
16 
terminology of “disadvantaged communities” in the CLCPA. I then outline how the 
17 
Companies are subject to CLCPA requirements. Then, I describe how DACs are 
18 
disproportionately burdened by environmental and public health burdens from the 
19 
Companies’ fossil fuel infrastructure and combustion in homes and would be 
20 
disproportionately burdened by RNG and hydrogen infrastructure and combustion in 
21 
homes. I particularly focus on energy burden and air pollution in homes as central issues 
22 
that disproportionately impact these populations. From there, I describe how the 
23 
Companies can reduce their contributions to disproportionate environmental and public 
24 
health burdens primarily through energy efficiency and electrification. I then provide 
25 
specific recommendations. The Companies must increase funding to energy efficiency 
26 
and electrification programs with specific strategies to ensure these investments reduce 
27 
health and energy burdens in DACs. The Companies must direct at least 35–40% of their 
28 
investments to disadvantaged communities to reduce pollution burden, increase public 
29 


--- Page 13 ---
Case Nos. 23-G-0225 and 23-G-0226 
   WE_ACT Direct: SONAL JESSEL 
7 
education, meet workforce hiring targets, and reduce utility bill rates for low-income 
1 
customers. 
2 
 
 
I then discuss the Companies’ ratepayer-funded political activities. State and 
3 
federal law as well as guidance from the Commission prohibit utilities from passing 
4 
political expenditures onto ratepayers. In April 2022 National Grid released a report—
5 
paid for by ratepayers—called “Our clean energy vision,” which makes policy 
6 
recommendations for using alternative combustion fuels to decarbonize New York’s gas 
7 
supply and discourages policymakers from supporting full electrification of the building 
8 
sector. National Grid has used the report as a political advocacy tool, including in 
9 
comments to the Climate Action Council on the State’s Scoping Plan. The Companies 
10 
also use the report to promote National Grid’s positions on policy issues through social 
11 
media and in communications with customers for which ratepayers foot the bill. The 
12

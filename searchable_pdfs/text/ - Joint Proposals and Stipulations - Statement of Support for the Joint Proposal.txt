Statement of Support for the Joint Proposal​
Alliance for a Green Economy (AGREE) 
 
 
I.​
Introduction 
Alliance for a Green Economy (“AGREE”) is a public interest intervenor, which has 
participated in most major rate cases in New York since 2017 in order to ensure utility rates are 
affordable and consistent with New York state energy and climate policy and the public interest. 
We advocate for a just, affordable, and rapid transition to a renewable energy economy that 
powers clean and healthy heating and cooling in buildings across New York State. 
We support the Joint Proposal (”JP”) filed by Consolidated Edison (“the Company” or 
“ConEd”), the Department of Public Service Staff (“Staff”), and other parties on November 5th, 
2025 in the above-referenced proceedings. The Joint Proposal represents a reasonable result of 
negotiations and is within the range of outcomes that could be expected from a litigated case, is 
supported or unopposed by several normally adversarial parties, and contains many public 
interest provisions that came out of the settlement process.  
II.​
Specific Provisions Supported by AGREE 
The Joint Proposal resolves issues that AGREE raised in testimony related to 
misalignment of rate design and rate outreach and education with New York’s policies and law. 
The resolution of these issues in the Joint Proposal, coupled with the major reduction in the 
proposed rate increases when compared to the Company’s original proposal, are a major reason 
we support the settlement. Additionally, we support the inclusion of several other public interest 
provisions  – improved language access (Section M-4),1 improved outreach around the Energy

In considering whether a negotiated settlement in a Public Service Commission 
(“Commission”) proceeding is in the public interest, the Commission must consider whether the 
settlement is consistent “with law and with the regulatory, economic, social, and environmental 
policies of the Commission and the State.”5 Further, New York’s Climate Leadership and 
Community Protection Act (“CLCPA” or “Climate Act”) requires all state agency decisions to be 
consistent with the greenhouse gas reduction mandates outlined in the CLCPA. The Commission 
first formally acknowledged that this provision of the CLCPA applies to rate cases in 2021.6  
To achieve the greenhouse gas emissions reductions required by the CLCPA (40% 
reduction from 1990 levels by 2030 and 85% reduction from 1990 levels by 2050), New York’s 
Climate Action Council found that most buildings need to switch to heat pumps.7 Heat pumps 
are highly efficient heating and cooling appliances that use electricity to move heat into and out 
of buildings. They do not combust fossil fuels to operate, and their high efficiency means that 
they use less electricity for heating than electric resistance heating and less electricity for cooling

strategy for achieving the CLCPA mandates, New York provides rebates to help customers 
overcome the upfront costs of heat pumps through the Clean Heat program administered by the 
investor-owned electric utilities and Empower+ administered by NYSERDA, as well as other

Statewide, utilities have supported approximately 83,000 heat pump installations through 
the Clean Heat Program from 2020-2024. Con Edison itself has upgraded 43,852 households to 
efficient heat pumps through the Clean Heat program, including 14,018 households in 2024 
alone.8 While this represents progress toward the CLCPA goals, the Climate Action Council 
found that approximately one to two million customers need to switch by 2030 in order to hit the 
goals.9 One factor in the slower than necessary adoption rates of heat pumps is the operating cost 
barrier created by electric rate design in New York. When considering what technology to use for 
heating and cooling, customers must take into account not just the up front cost of the appliances 
but also the cost of utilizing the appliances over time to meet their heating and cooling needs. In 
New York, due to the way customers are charged for electricity, the vast majority of heat pump 
users are paying more to operate their heat pumps than is just and reasonable.  
The issue of heat pump electricity rates as a barrier to heat pump adoption was first 
acknowledged by the state in a January 2019 NYSERDA filing entitled “Analysis of Residential 
Heat Pump Potential and Economics.”10 In that analysis, NYSERDA found “an inverse cost shift 
effect where heat pump customers could significantly overpay on their electricity bills under

prevailing residential electric rate structures.”11  NYSERDA describes the inverse cost shift like 
this:12 
The “inverse cost shift” refers to the following effect. Customers who install heat pump 
technology to replace conventional oil or gas combustion heating and air conditioning 
increase electricity usage during the winter and decrease electricity usage during the 
summer. For many customers, the result is a net increase in annual electricity usage that 
results in a net annual bill increase and increased revenues for the utility. Because the 
system is generally less constrained in the winter heating season, the increase in cost for 
the utility to provide the additional electricity in the winter is often less than the increase 
in revenue for the utility. This phenomenon most typically occurs for installations in the 
residential sector and is largely due to the structure of volumetrically based retail rates in 
the residential sector, which are designed to recover both variable costs as well as a 
portion of fixed-system infrastructure costs through a variable rate.     
 
For regulated utilities that earn a specified return on invested capital, an increase in utility 
revenues that exceeds the cost to serve additional load cannot be retained as profit but 
must be returned to utility ratepayers. As a result of these dynamics, the installation of a 
heat pump may lead the customer to start paying for a relatively larger fraction of the 
total systemwide grid infrastructure costs, which in turn, translates to a rate decrease for 
ratepayers as a whole; an “inverse cost shift” from non-heat pump ratepayers to the heat 
pump customer occurs. Rectifying this cost shift could improve the payback for 
customers.  
In January 2020, the Commission approved a rate plan for Consolidated Edison that 
included a new residential rate option called SC1 Rate IV for geothermal heat pump customers, 
which the Commission described was “to address concerns raised in some parties’ testimony that 
Con Edison’s volumetric delivery rates include[d] recovery of fixed system costs that geothermal 
customers do not cause to be incurred, and so create a subsidy for other rate payers.”13 Access to 
this rate was expanded to all residential SC1 customers in 2023.14

While SC1 Rate IV has the potential to provide significant savings for heat pump users in 
ConEd territory, vanishingly few heat pump customers take service under this rate. Fewer than 
1% of households that have upgraded to heat pumps through Con Edison’s Clean Heat program 
are enrolled in SC1 Rate IV – the remaining 99% of heat pump households likely overpay for 
electricity service.15  In direct testimony filed on behalf of Alliance for a Green Economy in this 
rate proceeding on May 30, 2025, Alexander Lopez of Rewiring America attributed the 
abysmally low uptake of SC1 Rate IV to two main factors: the complexity of SC1 Rate IV and 
the inadequacy of the Company’s rate outreach and education.16 If allowed to continue, the low 
uptake of appropriate rates for heat pump users will mean that heat pump customers in the 
Company’s territory will lose out on, or effectively be overcharged, by up to $131 million over 
the next three years.17 
​
Lopez testified that SC1 Rate IV is a demand based rate, which is difficult for customers 
to understand and that “simpler and more understandable rate options are needed to support the 
broader population of ConEd customers adopting electric heat pumps.”18 Lopez recommended 
the Company make additional rate options available to customers, such as a seasonal time of use  
rate and a seasonal flat rate, both of which were proposed by Environmental Defense Fund 
(“EDF”) witness Ron Nelson,19 and which Lopez described as more understandable and 
predictable than SC1 Rate IV.20

Additionally, Lopez recommended several improvements to the Company’s rate 
education and outreach, including customer-facing rate tools that allow customers to compare 
their bills under different rate options. 
The Joint Proposal incorporates some of these recommendations. First, the Company 
commits to a study that will be used to improve its existing seasonal time of use rate (SC1 Rate 
III), making the peak periods more appropriate. This is expected to result in a seasonally 
differentiated time of use rate that will help heat pump customers save money as compared to the 
default rate.21 This rate will be simpler and easier to understand than SC1 Rate IV, which will 
remain in effect as an option for customers who want to manage their demand to save money on 
that rate. 
Second, the Company commits to a variety of improvements to its education and 
outreach to customers about their rate options, including a “Heat Pump Operating Economics 
Customer Engagement Plan” (“Engagement Plan”).22 The Engagement plan will include 
promotion of rate options in the Clean Heat program literature and educational initiatives for 
contractors. It will also include marketing and outreach about rates to heat pump customers and 
will incorporate personalized rate tools that help customers compare rates and understand how 
rate options could benefit them. The Company will also provide proactive rate comparison 
reports, at a minimum of once per year, to residential customers that have adopted heat pumps, 
EVs and other priority Distributed Energy Resources “to provide sufficient information for 
customers to make an informed decision to determine the most appropriate rate.”

III.​
Conclusion 
Taken together, the improvements to SC1 Rate III and the Heat Pump Operating 
Economics Customer Engagement Plan will help families save money when they switch to clean, 
efficient heat pumps. The provisions in the Joint Proposal make the Company’s rates more just 
and reasonable, reduce an operating cost barrier to heat pump adoption, thereby advancing 
achievement of the state’s greenhouse gas reduction policy.  
The Joint Proposal contains several other improvements over the Company’s original 
proposal. These include, but are not limited to, the dramatic reduction in proposed bill impacts, 
improved language access commitments, improved outreach around the Energy Affordability 
Program, inclusion of greenhouse gas accounting, and improvement of outreach for the 
Company’s Energy Exchange program which helps customers avoid gas service line 
replacements by fully electrifying their homes. 
​
For all of these reasons, AGREE supports the Joint Proposal and recommends the 
Commission adopt the agreement at an upcoming session.

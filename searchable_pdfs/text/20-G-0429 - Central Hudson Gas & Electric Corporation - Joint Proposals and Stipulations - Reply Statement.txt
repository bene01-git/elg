PRELIMINARY STATEMENT 
 
 
 
Pursuant to the Ruling on Schedule issued by Administrative Law Judges Anthony 
Belsito and Michael Clark on September 1, 2021, Multiple Intervenors hereby submits this Reply 
Statement in Support of Joint Proposal in Cases 20-E-0428, 20-G-0429, and 20-M-0134.1  For the 
reasons set forth herein, as well as its Statement in Support of Joint Proposal filed herein on 
September 21, 2021, Multiple Intervenors urges the New York State Public Service Commission 
(“Commission”) to adopt the Joint Proposal, without modification, in this proceeding.2 
 
Multiple Intervenors has reviewed statements in support from the following fellow 
signatories to the Joint Proposal (“Signatories”; listed in alphabetical order): (a) Alliance for a 
Green Economy; (b) Central Hudson Gas & Electric Corporation (“Central Hudson”); (c) New 
York Geothermal Energy Organization (“NY-GEO”); (d) New York Power Authority; (e) New 
York State Department of Public Service Staff; (f) Public Utility Law Project of New York, Inc.; 
and (g) Utility Intervention Unit of the New York State Department of State’s Division of 
Consumer Protection.  While each of the Signatories has a unique focus and perspective on 
provisions within the Joint Proposal, Multiple Intervenors does not perceive any need to respond 
to other Signatories’ statements in support.

ARGUMENT 
 
POINT I 
 
 MR. WYMAN’S ARGUMENTS REGARDING THE GRIC 
SHOULD BE REJECTED 
 
 
 
In his statement in opposition, Mr. Wyman takes issue with proposed modifications 
to and the scheduled expiration of the GRIC, as well as the funding mechanism for the GRIC.  
(Wyman at 2-5.)  For the reasons set forth below, Mr. Wyman’s arguments should be rejected. 
 
The GRIC is a program that provides a $200 annual credit to residential customers 
that (i) were enrolled in the program as of July 1, 2021, or (ii) purchase a ground source heat pump 
and participate in Central Hudson’s Clean Heat Program.  (JP at 68-69.)  In the Joint Proposal, the 
Signatories agreed (i) to minor changes to the program (e.g., eligibility for the credit no longer will 
require the customer to pay for a subscription to Central Hudson’s “Insights+”), (ii) that funding 
for the program would come out of the utility’s Commission-approved heat pump budgets, and 


--- Page 6 ---
4 
(iii) that the program would be “eliminated following the development of a technology agnostic 
rate structure, such as opt-in standby or demand rates, or a rate that addresses the bill impact that 
geothermal heat pump customers face under the current mass market volumetric rate structure.”  
(JP at 69.)  If such a rate is not developed by December 31, 2025, the GRIC will end on that date 
unless extended through a Commission order.  (Id.). 
 
The GRIC essentially provides a financial credit to residential customers that utilize 
heat pumps.  As large non-residential customers, Multiple Intervenors members are not eligible 
for the GRIC and, consequently, have limited direct interest therein, other than the program’s 
potential cost to ineligible, non-residential customers.  Accordingly, Multiple Intervenors will 
confine its response to Mr. Wyman’s arguments related to GRIC to a few, narrow matters. 
 
Initially, Mr. Wyman argues that the rates charged to geothermal heat pump users 
are unjust and unreasonable because such customers are included in the same rate classes as other 
customers that do not use geothermal heat pumps.  (Wyman at 2.)  Such argument is flawed, and 
presumes that customers with geothermal heat pumps somehow are entitled to separate rate classes 
of their own.  Merely wishing that were the case, however, does not make it a legal requirement, 
nor does Mr. Wyman’s statement provide any legal authority or precedent for his positions. 
 
Mr. Wyman argues that customers with geothermal heat pumps have different 
usage characteristics as customers without heat pumps.  (Wyman at 2.)  Multiple Intervenors notes 
that residential customers with usage characteristics different from the norm have the option of 
participating in S.C. 6, Central Hudson’s technology-agnostic, voluntary residential time-of-use 
rate.4  Multiple Intervenors is not aware of any legal requirement that customers with geothermal

POINT II 
 
 MR. 
WYMAN’S 
ARGUMENTS 
REGARDING 
GAS 
DEPRECIATION RATES SHOULD BE REJECTED 
 
 
 
In his statement in opposition, Mr. Wyman characterizes the negotiated gas 
depreciation rates as “irresponsible” and argues that the Commission should reject the Joint 
Proposal and instead adopt accelerated gas depreciation rates – at an unknown incremental cost to 
customers – based on Mr. Wyman’s expectations as to how Central Hudson’s gas assets will 
become stranded as a result of the Climate Leadership and Community Protection Act (“CLCPA”).  
(Wyman at 5-8.)  For the reasons set forth below, Mr. Wyman’s arguments should be rejected. 
 
First, Mr. Wyman argues – or speculates – that the CLCPA will result in substantial 
stranded gas assets for Central Hudson unless depreciation is accelerated, and he recommends 
using a “Unit of Production” methodology as opposed to one based on the expected useful life of 


--- Page 9 ---
7 
the asset.  (Wyman at 5-6.)  Mr. Wyman cites to no legal precedent or Commission decisions in 
support of his position.  He dismisses potential alternatives to natural gas (e.g., hydrogen and 
renewable natural gas) which potentially could be utilized in the future – and make use of existing 
assets – in barely a paragraph without citation to scientific studies.  Multiple Intervenors does not 
know whether and to what extent natural gas and/or potential alternatives thereto will be utilized 
within the Central Hudson service territory in 2030, 2040, 2050, or beyond, and with all due 
respect, neither does Mr. Wyman.  Consequently, the Commission is faced with the question of 
whether the broadly-supported Joint Proposal should be rejected to modify the longstanding (and 
Commission-approved) methodology for depreciating gas assets based on Mr. Wyman’s 
expectations of the future.  It should not. 
 
Second, Mr. Wyman fails to provides information as to how adoption of his 
preferred gas depreciation methodology would impact the negotiated delivery rates.  If, arguendo, 
the negotiated gas depreciation rates advanced in the Joint Proposal (see JP at 35, App. V) were to 
be rejected and accelerated, the negotiated gas delivery rate increases would grow, probably 
substantially, at a time when customers of all types are struggling to recover from the COVID-19 
pandemic and its economic aftermath.  The Commission should not modify the Joint Proposal in 
a manner that enlarges the already-material, negotiated gas delivery rate increases. 
 
Third, it appears that the Commission already has considered – and rejected – Mr. 
Wyman’s arguments following full litigation in another utility’s rate proceeding.  In Case 20-G-
0101, involving Corning Natural Gas Corporation (“Corning”), the utility proposed to depreciate 


--- Page 10 ---
8 
its long-lived gas assets by 2050 in response to the CLCPA.7  Mr. Wyman actively supported 
Corning’s proposal in that proceeding.8 
 
After reviewing the evidence and the parties’ submissions, the Commission 
rejected the positions of Corning and Mr. Wyman, concluding that: “Logically, the shortening of 
depreciable lives to a 2050 end-point amounts to a forecast that the Company will cease utility 
operations by 2050 and that none of its assets at that time will have any value.  Because the 
Company has not met its burden of proof that such a result is probable, we find the Company’s 
position to be speculative.”9  Similarly, Mr. Wyman has not met the burden of proof needed to 
justify substituting his proposed depreciation methodology for that used by New York gas utilities, 
with Commission approval, for decades. 
 
Referring to the CLCPA, the Commission noted that: “It simply does not follow 
that a target of 85% statewide greenhouse gas reductions by 2050 means that the Company will 
close up shop by 2050 and that none of its assets will remain in service.”10  The Commission also 
noted that it is not possible to predict what Corning’s gas business will look like in 2050: 
 It is impossible to make a precise forecast at this time of what the 
Company’s business will look like in 2050, but it is clear that the 
assumption that the Company will be out of business at that time is 
at the extreme end of many possibilities.  These possibilities include 
business as usual if the State’s climate goals can be met through 
other means, the total or partial use by the Company of renewable 
natural gas supplies, and the total or partial conversion of the 
Company’s system to renewable hydrogen gas.  All of these

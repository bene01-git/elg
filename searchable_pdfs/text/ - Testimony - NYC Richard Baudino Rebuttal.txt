Q. 
MR. BAUDINO, PLEASE EXPLAIN WHY ALLOCATING DISTRIBUTION MAINS 
3 
COSTS BASED ON PEAK DEMAND ONLY IS INAPPROPRIATE. 
4 
A. 
The two main functions of distribution mains are to deliver gas during the system winter 
5 
peak and to connect customers to the system. A properly designed zero-intercept study or 
6 
minimum size system study recognizes these two functions by classifying main costs into 
7 
demand-related and customer-related costs, which can then be assigned to customer classes 
8 
based on their respective contributions to system peak and on the number of customers in 
9 
each class.  
10 
 
 
Peak winter demand is one of the primary drivers of the Companies’ investment in 
11 
gas distribution mains.  The Companies must have sufficient capacity available on their 
12 
systems to satisfy the peak winter heating demand, which is caused mainly by heating 
13 
customers.  If the peak winter demand increases, the Companies may need to invest in 
14 
additional mains to serve the load.  During the non-winter months, substantial excess 
15 
capacity exists on the system.  Use of the Companies’ distribution system during these 
16 
months does not cause additional fixed costs to be incurred by National Grid.  In a similar 
17 
manner to peak winter demand, if the number of customers increases, the Companies may 
18 
need to expand their distribution system investment.  Thus, the number of customers 
19 
connected to the distribution system is another important causative factor in distribution 
20 
main investment.

Q.   
IS THE MINIMUM SIZE DISTRIBUTION SYSTEM METHOD AND ZERO 
17 
INTERCEPT METHOD THE SAME THING? 
18 
A.  
In concept, yes they are the same.  The minimum size system approach identifies the 
19 
minimum sized distribution main needed to serve customers and then classifies that portion 
20 
of distribution mains as customer-related.  The zero intercept method uses regression 
21 
analysis to identify the cost of a hypothetical “zero sized” main, the cost of which is 
22 
necessary to serve customers connected to the system whether or not they place any demand 
23 
on the system.  While there may be subtle differences between the two methods, both 
24 
recognize that certain distribution main costs should be classified as customer-related and 
25 
allocated based on the number of customers not on peak demand.

Q. 
WHAT IS THE RESULT OF FAILING TO RECOGNIZE A CUSTOMER-RELATED 
1 
COMPONENT IN THE COST OF DISTRIBUTION MAINS? 
2 
A. 
The result is a significant misallocation of costs that fails to allocate proper cost 
3 
responsibility to the Company’s customer classes.  This point can be illustrated with the 
4 
following simple example.  Assume that there is a single industrial customer on KEDNY’s 
5 
system with a peak demand of 300 dekatherms (“Dth”).  Further assume that elsewhere on 
6 
the system, there is a neighborhood of 750 residential customers with a peak demand of 300 
7 
Dth.  In order to connect all of those residential customers to its system, the Company will 
8 
have to invest in far more footage of distribution mains for those customers than it would 
9 
have to invest in for the one industrial customer.   That extra investment in distribution mains 
10 
is due solely to the number of customers on the system, not the peak demands of those 
11 
customers.

I.   INTRODUCTION 
 
 
Corning Natural Gas Corporation (Corning or the Company) filed on February 27, 
2020, initial testimony, including panels and individual witnesses, and exhibits in support of a 
request to increase its annual gas revenue requirement by approximately $6.25 million (24.3% 
increase in delivery revenues) for the 12-month period ending January 31, 2022  (Rate Year or 
RY1).  The Company also provided tariff leaves for RY2 (12-months ending January 31, 2023) 
and RY3, (twelve-months ending January 31, 2024) as it sought a three-year rate plan.  The 
increases reflected for those two years are $845,142 and $680,913, respectively.  The Company 
stated that those three years of stand-alone increases would impact customer total bills by 23.4%, 
2.56% and 2.01%, respectively.  In its filing, however, Corning proposes a rate plan with 
levelized increases over three years.  The levelized increase would be $3,523,167 for each of the 
three Rate Years and would impact customer total bills by 10.93% in each rate year.  In 
testimony, Staff recommended a Rate Year revenue requirement increase of $270,773.     
By this initial brief, staff of the Department of Public Service (Staff) provide a 
summation of Corning’s and intervenors’ proposals and requests their testimony and Staff’s 
recommendations to the Commission regarding its determinations on the issues raised in this 
proceeding.  In addition, Staff recommends four adjustments to its proposed revenue requirement

III.  AUSTERITY 
 
When Corning filed its rate case on February 27, 2020, it was unclear how long COVID-
19 would continue to significantly impact New York State.  Prior to the Pandemic and resulting 
economic impacts, Corning filed its rate case seeking a rate year revenue requirement increase of 
more than 23%, or over $6.25 million.  Yet, in its rate case update, filed on May 12, 2020, and 
well into the Pandemic, the Company still sought a similarly substantial revenue requirement 
increase of over $6.22 million, or only $32,323 less than that proposed in its initial filing.  Such 
significant revenue requirement increases are almost unheard of, whether they are filed during 
periods of economic prosperity, much less times such as these, where individuals, businesses, 
and long-standing institutions are grappling with economic and social struggles and problems 
directly resulting from the Pandemic and the efforts to confront it, especially prior to the 
development of effective treatments early in the initial stages of the Pandemic, and later, 
vaccinations.

Corning claims that its proposed Rate Year revenue requirement reflects the 
Commission’s policies and goals related to the Climate Leadership and Community Protection 
Act (CLCPA) and efforts to reduce greenhouse gasses, particularly with regards to the 
accelerated depreciation of its plant.  However, the proposals put forward by the Company would 
result in a significant increase to revenue requirement.  As discussed in Staff’s brief, the proposal 
by the Company was ill-conceived in light of the tremendous revenue and rate impacts during 
this Pandemic, which is unprecedented in our lifetimes and in the history of the Public Service 
Commission.  Corning’s failure to take action to mitigate its proposed revenue requirement 
increase of 23% demonstrates why the Commission exists, to control and regulate utilities who 
provide a monopoly service critical to the preservation of life and property in their service 
territories.  Corning’s customers in the Southern Tier of New York rely on safe and reliable 
natural gas service to be able to heat their homes and businesses and protect themselves and their 
families from the winter cold.  The Commission’s responsibility is to act as a “break” or 
regulator in instances where the monopolistic provider of service seeks to leverage its status to 
exact from customers revenues and profits greater than it could if it operated in a competitive 
environment. 
In instances such as the present Pandemic, the Commission has both the power and the 
responsibility to take action to mitigate, as much as it believes reasonable, the need for revenue 
to cover certain of a utility’s operating expenses, and limit the negative impacts of unneeded 
revenue requirement relief and the attendant rate increases. 
Furthermore, the Commission is also presented with a situation where trial Staff’s 
recommendations do not reflect the sort of belt-tightening and austerity that might be needed to 
mitigate the economic impacts on customers that are impacted by the Pandemic and economic

IV.  REVENUES 
A. 
Gas Sales Forecast 
Corning Natural Gas forecasts total gas sales to be 20,169,473 CCF for the Rate Year for 
Service Classifications (SC) 1, 3, 6, and 14 (Exhibit 3 (CNG-2, Sch. 2, p. 5)).  These four 
classes, along with contract customers SC 11 are the largest delivery customer groups in 
Corning’s service territory, amount to 16.5 million or 91% of total delivery revenue for the Rate 
Year (Exhibit 3 (CNG-2, Sch. 1, p. 1)).   
The Company’s forecast used the test year actual data ending September 2019 to make 
weather adjustment and project customer additions.  Assuming weather normalized energy use 
per customer is unchanged going forward, the Company projects growth in sales is a result of 
200 new customers in the residential class (SC 1) and 10 new customers in the commercial class 
(SC 3) for the period from September 2019 to January 2022.  No customer additions were 
projected for other service classes (Tr. 70, Ln. 19 to Tr. 71, Ln. 3).   
Staff has concerns with the Company’s static forecasting methodology as it relies on only 
one year of historical data.  The Company’s forecast for residential customer count is also 
subjective and does not reflect many of the dynamic conditions that impact sales in the 
Company’s service territory.  It is Staff’s view that by considering historical trends, the forecasts 
can be done in a more objective manner (Tr. 758, Ln. 9 to Tr. 759, Ln. 3).  The Staff Forecasting 
Panel applied econometric methodology and utilized five years of historical data to develop a 
forecasting model.  Staff forecasts 19,819,077 CCF for the SCs 1,3, 6, and 14.  Although overall 
lower, this forecast calls for an upward adjustment for the residential SC 1 and downward 
adjustments for other commercial classes (Exhibit 43 (SFP-2, p.1)).   
Staff’s adjustment for the residential class is primarily a result of more reasonable 
forecast for customer counts.  Staff does not agree with Corning Gas’s forecast for residential

the use of renewable resources, and decrease the use of fossil fuels.

D. 
Amortization of Excess Accumulated Deferred Federal Income Taxes 
In December 2017, the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 (Tax Act) was signed into law 
which contained provisions that substantially modified the Internal Revenue Code (IRC).  Most 
notably the Tax Act reduced the maximum federal corporate tax rate from 35% to 21% effective 
January 1, 2018.  Utilities were required to remeasure accumulated deferred income taxes 
(ADIT), given the lower tax rate and record excess ADIT as a regulatory liability on their 
balance sheets.  For regulatory purposes, excess ADIT are classified as either protected by the 
IRC or unprotected.  Protected excess ADIT are subject to normalization procedures, whereby 
they must be returned to ratepayers over the remaining book life of the assets.  Unprotected 
excess ADIT are not subject to normalization and their treatment is subject to determination of 
the governing regulatory agency.  Most state regulatory commissions and FERC have opened 
proceedings into tax reform impacts and treatment of utility jurisdictional rate bases and rates 
(Tr. 554-556). 


--- Page 34 ---
CASE 20-G-0101 
 
 
31 
On December 29, 2017, in Case 17-M-0815,6  Proceeding on Motion of the Commission

The use of a proxy group analysis is a widely accepted standard, which has been 
consistently applied by the Commission to estimate the cost of equity for a utility.  Using a proxy 
group is necessary because calculating the cost of equity requires the use of estimates published 
by equity analysts, and those estimates, by their nature, could be biased or potentially inaccurate

criteria to construct a sufficiently large group of companies whose risk characteristics align as 
close as possible to the specific utility being analyzed (Tr. 1159, Ln. 12).  Once the proxy group 
is constructed, the average cost of equity for the proxy group can be estimated.  As a result, part 
of the difference between Staff and the Company’s cost of equity stems from the differences in 
their respective approaches in determining the proxy group for Corning (Tr. 1197, Ln. 1).  As 
discussed below, Staff recommends that its proxy group selection criteria be adopted.

The Company calculated six multi-stage DCF estimates to inform its ROE

each proxy group company (Tr. 500, Ln. 16).  The second stage is effectively a transition from 
its first stage to its third stage (Tr. 500, Ln. 21).  The third stage uses a long-term GDP growth

Company used earnings growth forecasts rather than dividend growth forecasts for the short-term

The first stage of Concentric’s DCF model uses earnings growth forecasts, rather than 
dividend growth forecasts, to estimate the short-term growth rate (Tr. 500, Ln. 16).  Doing so 
assumes that growth in dividends will approximate growth in earnings, and Concentric did not

to receive during the time they own the stock, which are both quarterly dividend payments and

As Staff points out, while earnings do drive companies’ payout policies, the value of the 
company’s common stock is equal to the present value of all future dividends (Tr. 1167, Ln. 12).  
This is because earnings that are retained only have value to the stockholder when they are paid 
as dividends in the future (Tr. 1167, Ln. 16).  Concentric’s claim that earnings are the 
fundamental determinant of a company’s ability to pay dividends, while accurate, misses the 
point.  Staff notes that Concentric did not provide any basis to support a one-to-one linear 
relationship of earnings growth to dividend growth in either its direct or supplemental 
testimonies (Tr. 1215, Ln. 23), and it is notable that it did not muster any evidence of this in 
rebuttal testimony either.  Concentric itself concedes that dividends are based on management

decisions related to cash management and other factors, rather than by earnings alone (Tr. 600,

survey data and academic research demonstrating that earnings are the most important variable 
of consideration to investors and that they dominate the reports of the equity analysts to which 
they subscribe (Tr. 601, Ln. 11; Tr. 601, Ln. 17).  This point should be understood in context.  
Even in the relatively stable utility industry, earnings can be very difficult to predict, while 
dividends are far more predictable by comparison (Tr. 1216, Ln. 14; Tr. 1216, Ln. 22).  Indeed, 
dividend policies is one of the ways that companies insulate shareholders from the volatility of 
earnings from one time period to another (Tr. 1214, Ln. 1).  Given the relative difficulty in 
forecasting earnings, compared to dividends, it is not surprising that investors are especially 
eager for insights into that variable, nor that the analysts are eager to meet that demand.  In any 
case, none of this supports the problematic assumption that earnings growth approximates 
dividend growth, nor that forecasts of one can substitute for the other.  For these reasons, the 
Commission should reject the use of earnings growth forecasts to estimate short-term growth, 
and it should instead continue to rely on dividend growth forecasts instead.

1. Electronic Deferred Payment Agreements (E-DPAs) 
As required by the Home Energy Fair Practices Act (HEFPA), a deferred payment 
agreement (DPA) is a written agreement for the payment of outstanding charges over a specific 
period that signed by both the utility and customer.  Generally, once a written agreement has 
been developed, a hard copy is created for the signatures of both parties.  An e-DPA allows for a 
customer to enter into a legally valid DPA with an electronic signature by the customer via a 
secure portal on its website. 
In Staff’s testimony, Staff recommended the Company file an e-DPA implementation 
plan that details the program feasibility and include, at a minimum, the projected costs and 
savings, implementation timeframe and a risk assessment and mitigation plan that addresses the 
possibility for intrusion of unauthorized third-party, repudiation and fraud or human error (Tr.

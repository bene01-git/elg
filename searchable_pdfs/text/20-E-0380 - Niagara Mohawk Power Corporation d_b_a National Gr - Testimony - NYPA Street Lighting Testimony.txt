II. 
BACKGROUND 
1 
Q.  
Why are municipalities opting to purchase their street light assets from utility 
2 
companies? 
3 
A.  
In November 2015, Governor Andrew Cuomo signed Chapter 495 of the 2015 Laws of 
4 
New York into law, which added Public Service Law Section 70-a and established 
5 
procedures for the transfer of ownership of street lighting systems to municipalities or 
6 
other government entities. In an October 2016 order in Cases 15-E-0745, et al., which we 
7 
will refer to as the “2016 Street Light Transfer Order,” the Public Service Commission 
8 
(“PSC”) approved tariff changes for all of New York State’s investor-owned utilities, 
9 
including the Company, that implemented the new street lighting ownership transfer 
10 
procedures. This regulatory change opened new opportunities for municipalities to 
11 
achieve both energy and cost reductions that did not exist previously. Municipalities 
12 
choose to buy the street light systems in their jurisdiction because they can achieve 
13 
savings from both the reduced energy usage associated with conversion to LED 
14 
luminaires and avoid the facilities charges associated with utility-owned street light 
15 
systems.  
16 
Q. 
Are there other factors driving street light asset sales?  
17 
A.  
Dissatisfaction with utility maintenance and operations of street light facilities are 
18 
another factor driving municipalities to purchase their street light assets. A common 
19 
complaint that NYPA hears from municipalities throughout the state is that many of their 
20 
street lights do not function properly and have not been functional for an extended period, 
21 
yet the utility company continues to assess facilities and usage charges. To address this 
22 
problem, municipalities want to purchase their lights and make them functional again.  
23 


--- Page 7 ---
 
 
6 
 
Purchasing their street lights allows municipalities to have control over the 
1 
maintenance necessary to keep their street lights in working order, as well as ensure more 
2 
streamlined communications with constituents regarding street light maintenance issues. 
3 
Furthermore, the municipality can competitively bid the maintenance and realize 
4 
maintenance cost savings compared to what otherwise would be paid to the utility. Some 
5 
municipalities also are interested in maintaining a uniform or unique appearance, and 
6 
purchasing the street lights gives them the flexibility to install various styles of street 
7 
lights that would not be offered by the utility. 
8 
Q.  
What role does NYPA play in the conversion of customer-owned street light assets 
9 
to LED? 
10 
A.  
Governor Cuomo directed NYPA to lead the Smart Street Lighting NY Program in his 
11 
2018 State of the State Address, with a goal to reduce energy consumption from street 
12 
lights across the state. NYPA implements the Smart Street Lighting NY Program as a 
13 
one-stop-shop model for customers to replace inefficient streetlights with LED 
14 
technology. Municipalities rely on NYPA’s technical expertise in design, procurement, 
15 
and construction to purchase and own their streetlight facilities and retrofit them with 
16 
LED fixtures, dimming nodes, and other smart solutions, thereby maximizing energy 
17 
efficiency and the resulting bill savings. NYPA also offers upfront financing for street 
18 
light projects, where financing costs are offset by the cost-savings created by the reduced 
19 
energy use of LED streetlights. NYPA currently has completed, or is under contract to 
20 
conduct, LED conversions with 60 municipalities, representing over 100,000 street lights, 
21 
in the Company’s service territory. 
22 
 
23 


--- Page 8 ---
 
 
7 
 
Q.  
Are there additional benefits to the Smart Street Lighting NY Program?

A.  
As recognized by the Commission in the 2016 Street Light Transfer Order, the 
2 
conversion of street lights from high-intensity discharge (“HID”) luminaires to LED

III. 
ASSET SALES 
8 
Q.  
What is the process for a municipality to purchase their street light assets from a 
9 
utility company?  
10 
A.  
The process for the transfer of ownership of street light assets from a utility to a 
11 
municipality in New York was established in the 2016 Street Light Transfer Order. 
12 
Interested municipalities submit a formal request via letter or e-mail to the utility to 
13 
purchase all or part of the street light system. The municipality should state their desire to 
14 
receive a proposal for the entire street light system or include a description of the portion 
15 
of the system the municipality is interested in purchasing, such as the number of street 
16 
lights, geographic region, etc. In response to the formal purchase request, the utility 
17 
provides a proposed purchase price, which must include the book value of the street light 
18 
assets, including the original cost and depreciation, as well as an explanation of how each 
19 
cost component is developed.  
20 
The municipality then has up to 180 calendar days from the receipt of the 
21 
purchase price proposal to review and inform the utility of their intent to move forward 
22 
with the purchase. Once the purchase price is agreed upon, the municipality must request 
23 


--- Page 9 ---
 
 
8 
 
the asset purchase and pole lease agreements in writing. Upon municipality review and 
1 
signature of both agreements, the municipality must send both documents to the 
2 
Company for a counter signature. Within 60 calendar days of the Company counter 
3 
signature, the Company must petition the PSC for approval of the asset transfer pursuant 
4 
to Section 70 of the Public Service Law. If approved by the PSC, the municipality and the 
5 
utility may move forward with the street light sale. 
6 
Q.  
Please describe the specifics of Niagara Mohawk’s street light asset sale process.   
7 
A.  
Under its process, Niagara Mohawk will sell its street lighting assets at net book value 
8 
(“NBV”) plus administrative costs to any municipal customer that agrees to purchase all 
9 
street light assets within its taxing jurisdiction. The Company explained in its response to 
10 
NYPA-003 that their plant asset repository system (“PowerPlan”) maintains asset records 
11 
by vintage year and by tax district, not at an individual asset level. Therefore, the NBV 
12 
for a specific customer is derived by performing a series of calculations to extrapolate 
13 
and apply an average NBV for the tax district to specific customer billing units.   
14 
Niagara Mohawk also breaks out the administrative costs included in the purchase 
15 
price into transaction costs, which generally do not vary based on the number of street 
16 
lights being sold, and transition costs, which do vary based on the number of assets 
17 
impacted by the sale. The administrative costs are negotiated between the utility and the 
18 
municipality and approved by the Commission as part of the Section 70 filing. The 
19 
Commission approved this sales process in the Company’s earlier electric rate proceeding 
20 
in Case 17-E-0238. 
21

9

10

Yes. The Company should provide a more detailed breakdown of each variable and 
11 
assumption utilized in the NBV calculation and how it contributes to the final NBV when 
12

13 
documentation to demonstrate how the original asset values and depreciation are 
14 
determined. The detailed breakdown should also explain how additional factors, such as 
15 
the cost of removal factor, impact the NBV. The more detailed explanation should be 
16 
presented in a way that allows an average customer to understand the calculations. These 
17 
fixes would improve transparency in the calculations and will allow customers to have a 
18 
better understanding of how the sale price is calculated so they can make a more 
19 
informed decision about moving forward with the sale.

Q.  
What other challenges have customers experienced in purchasing their street light 
1 
assets from Niagara Mohawk?  
2 
A.  
The Company’s Outdoor Lighting Panel acknowledged that the Company’s accounting 
3 
and record keeping systems were not designed to separate out individual assets for sale, 
4 
and that the process to perform such a separation and calculate an NBV for specific assets 
5 
has been complicated. They further acknowledge that the Company had encountered 
6 
several unanticipated issues and considerations as they processed initial street light asset 
7 
sales. 
8 
While the Company did not define the unanticipated issues it encountered, NYPA 
9 
customers have experienced several lengthy delays in the sales process. Such delays 
10 
include a territory-wide system audit which rendered the Company unable to provide 
11 
street light purchase price proposals within the required 90-day period in 2017, a one 
12 
month pause while the Company completed revisions and standardization of contract 
13 
documents in 2019, and a six month pause in early 2020 while the Company corrected an 
14 
error in the model used to calculate NBV.  
15 
During each pause in the sale process, customers are financially harmed by 
16 
forgoing energy and cost savings, both from the delayed LED conversion and continued 
17 
payment of facility fees. The facility fees comprise 60 to 70 percent of the average 
18 
customer outdoor lighting utility bill and are therefore a large expense for the customer to 
19 
continue paying due to delays. Additionally, industry experience indicates that customers 
20 
realize average energy bill savings of approximately 50 percent after an LED conversion. 
21 
Such delays harm both the customer, by delaying energy bill savings, as well as the state, 
22 
by delaying energy savings needed to achieve state emission reduction goals.

 These difficulties were compounded by a lack of communication from the

Company on progress toward resolving such issues. Requests for information on the

3

2

15 
sale price to a concrete date.

allow the projects to be completed and start generating energy savings on a faster 
21

2 
Q.  
What should be done if lengthy delays are not mitigated by process improvements? 
3 
A.  
Customers are financially harmed by delays because they continue to incur facility fees 
4 
and are unable to realize energy cost savings. To address this harm and promote 
5 
compliance with tariff deadlines that Niagara Mohawk legally is obligated to meet, the 
6 
Company should compensate the customer by providing a credit on future energy bills 
7 
for the facility fees incurred due to missed deadlines.  
8 
The proposed credit would be triggered if the Company misses a tariff deadline 
9 
by more than 10 percent. Using the applicable deadlines, for example, a customer would 
10 
be eligible for the credit if Niagara Mohawk takes greater than 99 days to provide the sale 
11 
price, or more than 66 days to submit the Section 70 filing. For each day beyond the 10 
12 
percent buffer, the Company would credit the customer an amount equivalent to the 
13 
average daily facility charge, based on the average cost in the prior 12-month period. The 
14 
credit would be provided on a future monthly energy bill. This crediting mechanism 
15 
would provide an incentive that does not exist under the current tariff structure for the 
16 
Company to meet the required deadlines and would limit the financial harm the customer 
17 
experiences from any delays.

18 
the customer plans to replace in the near term, which would be an inefficient use of

Company resources.

Q. 
What benefits are associated with the adaptive dimming control capabilities of 
14

15 
A.  
The Seattle LED Adaptive Lighting Study, prepared by Clanton & Associates in 2014 
16

17 
usage by an additional 5 percent to 25 percent as compared to pre-LED energy usage 
18 
levels, depending on the dimming strategy. The adaptive dimming control capabilities 
19 
provide a mechanism to implement such dimming strategies. Without network lighting 
20 
controls, street lights can only be operated at full brightness or be turned off. The Seattle 
21 
Study demonstrated that, based on field tests with new lamps, dimming does not 
22 
significantly impact object detection by the naked eye, and even that dimming 50 percent 
23 


--- Page 25 ---
 
 
24 
 
did not significantly reduce object detection in dry pavement conditions. Installation of 
1 
NLC nodes will allow municipalities to reduce energy consumption and extend the useful 
2

3 
costs for the municipal tax payers. 
4 
Customers can benefit from larger scale dimming strategies to reduce street light 
5 
energy use. For example, lighting can be reduced to a greater extent during low traffic 
6

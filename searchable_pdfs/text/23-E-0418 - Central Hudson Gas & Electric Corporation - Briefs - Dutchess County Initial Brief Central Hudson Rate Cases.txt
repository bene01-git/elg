I.  INTRODUCTION 
   Dutchess County Government’s position in these cases has always been and continues to be 
focused upon Central Hudson providing the highest quality essential energy products at the 
lowest reasonable price. Repeatedly within the testimony filed, discovery, cross examination, and 
now in this Initial Brief, this focus has not changed. Some key points noted in these rate case 
proceedings is that the primary stakeholder is the customer, such cases must be microeconomic 
in analysis, and that Central Hudson as a privately held entity must be able to act in a 
competitive fashion with boundaries provided by the Public Service Commission. 
    Up until the advent of the New York Energy Highway supplemented by the Reforming the 
Energy Vision (REV), the Public Service Commission, Central Hudson, and Dutchess County 
were completely aligned with promoting high quality customer service at the lowest reasonable 
customer price. Not so today, with the electric and natural gas customer becoming a funding 
vehicle for reducing carbon, protecting the community, financing social justice initiatives, 
promoting electric vehicles, reducing carbon generation in other states, and even providing state 
climate leadership to the rest of the world.  
   The climate initiatives proposed by Central Hudson for customers to go beyond simply paying 
for energy products is a well-intentioned laudable objective but does not belong on an electric or 
natural gas energy bill. These rate cases are littered with issues not directly related to a customer 
purchasing a reliable high-quality product, complicating the proceedings while adding reams of 
material to the proceeding record.  At the same time, these issues drive up the price for electric 
and natural gas service provided to Central Hudson delivery or full requirements customers in 
Dutchess County.  


--- Page 4 ---
4 
 
   On average the price of a kilowatt-hour of electric purchased by a customer in New York State 
is around 20¢. Of this price the NYISO bulk power day ahead market place in 2023 supplied a 
kilowatt-hour on average for around 4¢.  
   The other 16¢, (which is not exact) is representative of a bunch of other “stuff” which goes into 
electric billing required by the State of New York, and, as indicated finds its origins in the goals 
outlined in the New York Energy Highway and REV, which have now been codified into 
legislation within the Climate Leadership and Community Protection Act (CLCPA).  
   As an example, the REV set a clean energy goal of a 40% reduction in greenhouse gas 
emissions from 1990 levels by 2030, that has now been established as a mandate through the 
CLCPA.  Other REV goals such as 50% of generation coming from renewable energy sources by 
2030 was raised and mandated in the CLCPA to 70% by 2030. The CLCPA took a set of 
aggressive REV goals, made them even more aggressive and mandated their accomplishment by 
certain dates into State law.  
   Accomplishment of these CLCPA mandates is placed in part within these Central Hudson rate 
case proceedings. However, customers need to be protected from extra costs generated by 
Commission and Central Hudson staff zealously adding to the already aggressive 
implementation.  
   When a customer hires Central Hudson to provide a kilowatt-hour of energy, the customer 
believes that the per unit price pays for a unit of delivered energy. Yes, as noted with the 16¢/kwh 
differential example, there are numerous cost items which get added into the electric bill and, in 
a similar vein into the gas bill. Central Hudson must track the hundreds of cost items which 
eventually end up getting billed to the customer. Central Hudson has no flexibility in this regard 


--- Page 5 ---
5 
 
and all billing is subject to audit using existing tariffs as the standard for audit. It is impossible 
for a customer to know, picking up a typical electric and or natural gas bill in New York State, 
what they are buying. Cross examination by the County of the Customer Experience Panel in 
these cases on January 31, 2024, noted such, using an actual bill provided on the record by the 
Town of Olive, (Exhibit 565).  
    The Commission can ill afford continued additions to customer billing without cautiously and 
deliberately understanding the ramification of such billing complexity. The customer needs 
understandable transparency of cost origination in their billings, i.e. what is delivered to the 
meter along with everything else.  
    One of the County’s direct interests in the proceedings, as noted in direct testimony and during 
cross examination in the hearings, stems from proposed discretionary payments for systems, 
facilities, and staffing, accelerating implementation of the CLCPA. Central Hudson’s future rate 
payer funded expenditures should be limited to balanced CLCPA implementation compliance. 
Implementation acceleration in the current market-place for carbon reduction is an inefficient 
application of customer funding. Carbon markets need to develop and stabilize prior to making 
discretionary investments.  
   These rate cases will in all practicality generate increases in customer billing in the here and 
now. Individual customers will feel the additional cost impact in their current bill payments 
while the investments to implement the CLCPA will not generate the projected worldwide 
benefit until as far as 2050 and beyond.  Accelerating revenue collections from individual 
customers to meet timing mandates over 25 years in the future means the local customer will 
have added costs in the here and now, when they are already facing cost burdens of rising 
housing, food, and other costs. This acceleration of already aggressive clean energy programs

lacks balance which is required by Commission Staff and Company to meet bill paying customer 
immediate needs in Dutchess County.  
    In support of the County’s direct interests in these proceedings and the tenor of the hearings 
conducted, the County asserts the reasons set forth fully below.      
 
 
 
 
 
 
II. CLIMATE LEADERSHIP AND SUSTAINABILITY INIATIVES 
 
A. 
The Discretionary CLCPA Revenue Requirements Proposed in the Central Hudson  
             Direct Testimony by the Climate Leadership and Sustainability Panel Should be  
                                                                     Denied  
 
   Company programs need to be directed to fulfilling the very basic mission of high-quality 
low-customer cost service. The installation of solar panels on company buildings, will add to 
the cost borne by customers. The Climate Leadership and Sustainability Panel’s (CLSP) 
initial testimony assertion on page 34 lines 2-7 that the company “strives to be a role model 
and leader in promoting local and carbon-free technologies”… “providing a good example 
for customers”… thus “supporting the state’s ambitious solar generation goals”, may be fine 
for a company operating  in a competitive market place. Such customers are free to choose to 
purchase their products or service and therefore support these initiatives, or customers can 
choose to purchase from alternative companies.  
   Not so with Central Hudson’s gas and electric service. The customer has only a Central 
Hudson electric and/or gas meter-the distributing infrastructure of which cannot be readily 
replicated to support a competitive immediate choice.   


--- Page 7 ---
7 
 
   The solar arrays proposed for Central Hudson buildings by the panel were accompanied by 
other revenue requirement proposals including: 
 (1) a projected rate of emission reduction to 2.66 mmt/year by the year 2030 versus a 
projected required statewide allocated reduction of 1.91 mmt/year (page 11 lines 4 through 
11 of the initial CLSP testimony) 
(2) the company taking “significant steps” to “incorporate climate change considerations into 
operations” while “understanding stakeholder interests” (page 13 lines 5-13 of the initial 
CLSP testimony) 
(3) the desire to purchase “RSG Responsibly Sourced Gas” at a potential premium (initial 
testimony of the CLSP page 23 lines 9-15 inclusive)  
(4) hiring a contractor to do a study to assess “potential sites for on -site clean hydrogen 
production” (page 23 lines 9-15 of the initial CLSP testimony) 
(5) electrification of the fleet in which “Central Hudson will lead by 
example”…“encouraging customers to consider electric alternatives” (page 31 lines 13-15 of 
the initial testimony of the CLSP)  
(6) the addition of “45 incremental employees” required to facilitate the “achievement of the 
CLCPA targets” (page 34 lines 20-23 of the initial testimony of the CLSP). 
   The hearing questioning that took place by the County on January 30, 2024 in the cross 
examination of the CLSP, was directed at the customer benefiting on a product delivery basis 
from expenditures identified as necessary by the CLSP. The proposals listed above fall short of 
providing direct customer benefit and rely upon macroeconomic considerations such as reducing 


--- Page 8 ---
8 
 
carbon content on a worldwide basis to assert an upside return for the customer purchase 
sometime in the future.  
    Based on reasoning offered in Dutchess County’s direct testimony and reinforced in the 
hearing cross examination of the Climate Leadership and Sustainability Panel, the solar array 
installations along with the above numbered proposals should be denied.  An alternative plan 
should be ordered by the Commission as part of a rate award in these proceedings. The basis for 
the alternative plan developed by the Company and Staff of the Commission would be to reduce 
CLSP proposed revenue requirements while still adhering to the mandates built into the CLCPA. 
Such plan proposals must result in driving down revenue requirements while producing tangible 
monetary benefits for a Central Hudson customer residing within Dutchess County.  
B.   The Bill Paying Customer Should be Recognized as the Primary Stakeholder in All  
                                  Central Hudson Rate Case Proceedings  
 
   While all indicated as stakeholder groups in these proceedings, it is critical to remember that 
disadvantaged communities, rate case intervenors and politicians outside of Central Hudson’s 
region, national environmental protection groups, peer companies, the SEC all do not pay CH 
gas and electric bills. Individual metered customers pay for CH gas and electric service.  As 
such, within enacted legislated constraints, bill paying customers must be held up as the primary 
stakeholder in all rate case proceedings conducted within the State of New York and afforded 
preference over all other competing interests. Without customers to the pay bills, Central 
Hudson’s operations becomes unsustainable and all other intervening interests in these rate cases 
collapse. The Public Service Commission itself exists because a customer should be protected 
from overpayment of electric and natural gas billing.  


--- Page 9 ---
9 
 
   Foundational is the requirement that the customer for any rate case identified expenditure find 
a direct benefit, unless otherwise mandated by law.  Central Hudson and the Commission must 
assert that the customer is the primary stakeholder in these rate case proceedings and, as such, 
the proceedings must generate direct gas and electric service benefit at the lowest reasonable 
cost.      
   The direct testimony of the Climate Leadership and Sustainability Panel on pages 14 and 15 
speaks of company stakeholders and the dedication of the company to meet the expectations of 
these stakeholders based upon climate change and sustainability strategies.  
   Certainly, all companies have stakeholders beyond the customer who pays the bills. However, 
when it comes to a gas and electric utility in New York both the Company and the Commission 
should state clearly that the customer is given priority to the extent that such priority is legally 
permissible. The Commission’s Reforming the Energy Vision Proceeding changed the electric 
and natural gas utility industry in New York.  Whereas the utility industry was substantially 
changed, changes accruing to regulation within the state were limited. By and large state utility 
regulation remained the same with the possible exception that the regulation has intensified. The 
Commission needs to reaffirm its commitment to the public service starting with the bill paying 
customer. In addition, active parties in rate cases should be limited to those whose interests align 
with direct customer benefit and the Commission should police proceedings to focus on quality 
customer service.  
 
III.   CUSTOMER SERVICE 
A.    
The Discretionary Revenue Requirements Proposed by the Customer Experience  
                                                    Panel (CEP) Should be Denied  


--- Page 10 ---
10 
 
 
   Historically Central Hudson and for that matter the Public Service Commission set leadership 
standards for customer service. The focus was the customer period. What does a customer want 
and what does service entail? The customer wants high quality reliable service at the lowest 
reasonable price. Such desires on the part of the customer set the standard for the Company and 
Commission. In regard to the Public Service Commission, service still remains in its title.   
   The Customer Experience Panel in their direct testimony and rebuttal testimony list customer 
service a total of 34 times, 18 in the direct and 16 in the rebuttal. Reference to customer service 
comes in the form of performance indicators, employee titles, requests for bench marking 
studies, target levels for reporting to the Commission, and organizational titles. No where in the 
direct or rebuttal testimony is there a commitment stated that the Company is going to 
organizationally do something and as a result quality customer service will be delivered.   
   As pointed out in the cross examination conducted by the County on January 31st, 2024, 
Central Hudson needs to reaffirm a commitment to high quality customer service at the lowest 
reasonable cost. Adders to the operation at a customer cost without such a commitment is 
unlikely to produce a noticeable customer benefit. As discussed in the Dutchess County direct 
testimony as well as the cross examination of the Customer Experience Panel, the company has 
one primary stakeholder-the bill paying customer-who should be assured of an accurate bill prior 
to the Company speculating on enhancing the customer experience though other means.  
   Thus, the proposed discretionary revenue requirements suggested in the direct and 
rebuttal testimony of the Customer Experience Panel should be denied until the customer 
billing is software error free and the collection of accurately billed accounts have been 
reduced to pre Covid levels.  


--- Page 11 ---
11 
 
   In addition, the County generally agrees with the direct testimony of the DPS Staff Consumer 
Services Panel (SCSP) dealing with cost constraints especially as those cost constraints relate to 
the number of full time-equivalent employees required, the Energy Affordability Program, 
electric vehicles, and the establishment of a billing department. However, the County also 
believes that in regard to negative revenue adjustments or NRAs, “tripling and quadrupling the 
NRA’s” and “enhancing the Company’s reporting regarding customer service performance” (P. 
13, L. 7-10 of the DPS SCSP direct testimony) is problematic.   
   As a general statement, an incentive should be applied to promote organizational behavior that 
differs from the core behavior expected from any going concern. In the case of a regulated utility 
in New York, the Commission has numerous ways of applying the proverbial stick versus the 
carrot. If negative incentives or NRA’s are to be applied at all they should be applied sparingly 
otherwise the NRA will be viewed as a hedge against incentives applied. 
   Tripling and quadrupling NRAs will cause a focus on the NRA and not on delivered customer 
service. Balance is needed in the application of incentives. Tripling and quadrupling incentives 
whether positive or negative typically comes with unintended consequences.  
   Going hand and hand with the need for balance are new requirements for enhanced reporting. 
Currently the level of reporting that is taking place by utilities in NY State is significant. It would 
serve the Commission well to eliminate archaic reporting when new reporting is added. Some 
portion of the number of full time equivalent employees proposed by the company find their 
origins in the expanding levels of reporting which are developing for regulated utilities within 
the State, especially as the New York State Cap-and-Invest program moves forward.    
    
B.    Customer Billing Needs to Become Error Free, Transparent, and Understandable                                  
 


--- Page 12 ---
12 
 
 
   A considerable amount of discussion and testimony in these rate cases has been devoted to 
customer billing errors. Billing errors have led to an avalanche of Commission complaints and 
have even made their way to the US Congress. The steps taken by the Company to address 
billing errors resulted in a reorganization at the highest level. 
   Utility billing in New York is unusually complex and is getting more and more complex as 
more programs requiring funding get approved as part of statewide proceedings or by virtue of 
rate cases. Every dime generated utility revenue amount must find a home in a customer bill and 
such billing must be codified in a filed tariff.   
   The Commission needs to work to find ways to simplify the gas and electric billing that takes 
place and, in the process, develop more transparent bills that customers can understand.  
   Going hand-in-hand with the billing is the collection of the billing revenues which become 
justifiably uncollectable without an accurate bill. 
 
IV.   EARNINGS ADJUSTMENT MECHANISMS 
 
A.  The New Earning Adjustment Mechanisms (EAM) Proposed as Well as the EAMs for  
                   the Continued Implementation of the CLCPA Should Be Denied 
 
   As indicated in the initial testimony of the Central Hudson Policy Panel (P. 26) as well as the 
 
 initial testimony of the Earnings Adjustment Mechanism Panel the company proposes a better 
  
alignment with the goals of the CLCPA through new and modified EAMs.  
 
   The County supports the use of Earnings Adjustment Mechanisms as a tool to promote cost 
efficient enhanced customer service. The County’s interest is not with nebulous macroeconomic 
benefits accruing to an entire customer base but rather how to keep more dollars in a customer’s 
pockets.  


--- Page 13 ---
13 
 
   The State’s CLCPA mandates are directed toward benefit of the world community.  There is no 
direct one to one correspondence between reducing GHG emissions rates by Central Hudson and 
a tangible benefit to a Central Hudson customer in Dutchess County. On a world-wide basis 
Central Hudson is not a major player and the actions of the Company to make a world-wide 
carbon reduction impact is very small, bordering on the nonexistent. However, Central Hudson’s 
customer cost impacts related to CLCPA implementation are sizable.  
   Existing CLCPA implementation programs with earning adjustment mechanisms should stand 
on their own and compete with other Company programs that have no incentive mechanisms 
assigned.   
 
V. 
REVENUE ALLOCATION AND RATE DESIGN 
A.   Deny the Elimination of Gas Declining Tail Block Rates Along with the High-   
       Volume Usage Discount in Gas Tariff SC-6  
 
   Eliminating the gas declining block rates and the high-volume usage discount in the S.C. No. 6 
gas tariff in order to address climate change makes very little sense. As noted by the Customer 
Experience Panel during cross examination by the County, the elimination of such discounts may 
in fact improve a delivery rate design price signal but it might also signal to the user that enough 
is enough and it is time to move operations out of the state. Commercial customers are under 
significant pressure to remain competitive not only in the local market-place but also 
internationally. Such changes in rate design are hard to support from a customer perspective 
weighed against a benefit that only accrues on a worldwide basis.  
 
      VI. CONCLUSION  


--- Page 14 ---
14 
 
   These rate cases have many costs layered in that have little to do with quality electric and 
natural gas service at the lowest reasonable price. New York State has some of the highest utility 
rates in the United States and has already achieved one of the lowest per capita carbon emissions 
rates in the nation.  
   Dutchess County has participated in the last number of Central Hudson rate cases going back 
some 20 years. The reason, necessity, and justification for such participation was to support the 
citizens of the County as well as a corporation headquartered in the County which delivered vital 
services. 
   The County when intervening in rate cases has always pushed for revenue requirement 
proposals that were cost effective and would deliver direct customer service benefit.  
   Historically participation in the rate cases also rested upon a Public Service Commission whose 
Staff was made up of dedicated regulators who had a grasp on the pulse of the Company 
knowing how and when to intervene. The County relied on such experienced Department of 
Public Service Staff to provide rate case balance. 
    Overall-interests were aligned-to deliver a high quality, reliable service to the customer at the 
lowest reasonable price with direct benefits provided to a customer who resided in Dutchess 
County.  
   Starting with the New York Energy Highway initiative, the Reforming the Energy Vision 
proceeding, and now the Climate Leadership and Community Protection Act the landscape 
within which rate cases are conducted, has changed. Without a doubt all three of these State 
actions hold the promise of improved gas and electric customer service at the lowest reasonable 
price point. However, in order to accomplish this service goal, the customer must remain the 
focus of attention by the PSC and Company. 


--- Page 15 ---
15 
 
   As part of the Dutchess County initial testimony, an Exhibit DC-1 was provided consisting of a 
cover letter for the submission of comments by the County on the Draft Scoping Plan developed 
by the Climate Action Council to implement the Climate Leadership and Community Protection 
Act. The County, after an investment of a significant amount of resources to understand what 
was proposed in the Draft Scoping Plan, justified and concluded that without modification what 
was proposed in the Plan simply could not work under the scheduling mandates codified in the 
legislation.  Thus, although the State now has a mandated Scoping Plan to implement the CLCPA 
the County’s submitted analysis remains the same- there is much work remaining to bring that 
Scoping Plan to fruition.  
   In these rate cases the County has focused its positions on mitigating the potential for 
investments and expenditures going beyond the absolute requirements of the Company to 
proceed responsibly under the CLCPA. In other words, why invest and build without the 
assurance that such investments will be used and useful in the future to the direct customer 
served.  In addition, the County has pointed out repeatedly in the testimony and cross 
examination conducted in these proceedings that the customer and cost of service to the 
customer must remain the primary order of business for Central Hudson and the 
Commission.
